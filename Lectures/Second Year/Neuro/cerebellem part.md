## Slide 3

- Gross cerebellar anatomy
- About 80% of the cortex (around 17 cm x 120 cm surface)
- (surface grey matter) is hidden from view on the surfaces of the folia.

**Notes:** The cerebellum is made up of two hemispheres connected by
the vermis in the midline (Fig. 3.16). The vermis is distinct
only on the undersurface, where it occupies the floor of a
deep groove, the vallecula. The hemispheres show numerous
deep fissures, with folia in between. About 80% of the cortex
(surface grey matter) is hidden from view on the surfaces of
the folia.
The oldest part of the cerebellum (present even in fishes) is the
flocculonodular lobe consisting of the nodule of the vermis and
the flocculus in the hemisphere on each side. More recent is the
anterior lobe, which is bounded posteriorly by the primary fissure
and contains the pyramis and the uvula. The most recent is
the posterior lobe. Prominent features of the posterior lobe are
the tonsils. The tonsils lie directly above the foramen magnum of
the skull; if the intracranial pressure is raised (e.g., by a brain
tumour), one or both tonsils may descend into the foramen and
pose a threat to life by compressing the medulla oblongata.
The white matter contains several deep nuclei. The largest of
these is the dentate nucleus

## Slide 4

- Different cerebellar areas and functions
- Back view

**Notes:** Phylogenetically, the initial development of the cerebellum (in
fish) took place in relation to the vestibular labyrinth. With the
development of quadrupedal locomotion, the anterior lobes of
the cerebellum (in particular) became richly connected to the
spinal cord. Assumption of the erect posture and achievement
of a whole new range of physical and cognitive skills have been
accompanied by the appearance of massive linkages between
the anterior and posterior lobes of the cerebellum and the cerebral
cortex. In general, cerebellar connections with the vestibular
system, spinal cord, and cerebral cortex are arranged such
that each cerebellar hemisphere is primarily concerned with the
coordination of movements ipsilaterally.
The gross anatomy of the cerebellum is described briefly in
Chapter 3, where it may be reviewed at this time.

## Slide 8

- Cerebellar afferents: brain
- Corticopontocerebellar
- From motor cortices and SSC to pontine nuclei and then to contralateral cerebellar lateral zones
- Olivocerebellar
- Excited in the olives by projections from MC, basal ganglia, RF, and spinal cord
- Vestibulocerebellar
- Terminate in the fastigial nucleus and the flocculonodular lobe
- Reticulocerebellar
- Terminates mainly in the vermis

## Slide 9

- Cerebellar afferents: body
- Dorsal spinocerebellar tract
- Signals from muscle spindles, golgi tendons organs, skin tactile receptors, joint receptors
- Ventral spinocerebellar tract
- Corticospinal and rubrospinal tracts collaterals
- Ventral horns of the spinal cord motor pathways - efference copy of the anterior horn motor drive
- Dorsal columns nuclei of the medulla (cuneatus and gracilis)
- Spinoreticular and spino-olivary pathways (relayed in the reticular formation and the olive)

## Slide 19

- Cerebellar disfunction
- Often damage to deep cerebellar nuclei
- Dysmetria  and ataxia
- Inability to predict extent of movements  Dysmetria
- Overshooting of movements and overcompensation of opposite movements  Ataxia
- Can also be caused by lesion of spinocerebellar tracts
- Inability to perform rapid movement sequences  Dysdiadochokinesia
- Difficulties in talking  Dysarthria
- Inability to fix eyes in one spot  Cerebellar nystagmus
- Loss of muscular tone  Hypotonia

**Notes:** Video 1—Friedreich's Ataxia: A patient with Friedreich's ataxia, a gait disorder due to both cerebellar and proprioceptive ataxia, has imbalance, staggers, and has difficulties in making a half-turn. Because of difficulties with tandem walking, the patient requires the intermittent support of a wall. The patient has dysmetria during nose--finger and finger chase tests, and instability in a standing position with the feet together because of cerebellar ataxia. There is an obvious loss of balance when the patient's eyes are closed because of the proprioceptive component of the ataxia. Saccadic ocular pursuit and square-wave jerks due to fixation instability are shown, as well as diffuse abolition of the reflexes and right extensor plantar reflex. Video 2—Ataxia with Oculomotor Apraxia Type 1: Choreic movement of the hands and trunk with cervical dystonia in a patient with ataxia with oculomotor apraxia type 1. Video 3—Ataxia Telangiectasia: Right horizontal gaze-evoked nystagmus and nasal as well as temporal telangiectasias in a patient with ataxia telangiectasia. Video 4—Oculomotor Apraxia Type 1: Oculocephalic dissociation in a patient with ataxia with oculomotor apraxia type 1. During the rotation of the head, a dissociation is expressed as a delay between the movement of the eyes following that of the head.
