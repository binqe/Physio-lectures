## Slide 6

- Parkinson Disease (Parkinson’s)
- Chronic, slowly progressive degenerative disease
- Idiopathic
- “153,000 people live with PD in the UK	=	1 person
- in every 350” (Parkinson’s UK)
- Onset age: variable, average age 55; only 1 in 20 cases in people aged under 50.
- ↑ prevalence with age; 1% of people aged above 65
- Men slightly more affected (3:2)
- James Parkinson

## Slide 8

- Pathophysiology
- Occurs in basal ganglia:	striatum – (caudate N and putamen) and globus pallidus, subthalamic N, and substantia Niagra
- Neuro-degeneration occurs mainly in the pars compacta of substantia Niagra – rich in neuromelanin-containing cells.
- In PD, there is apoptosis of neuromelanin containing neurones
- Immune cells remove these dying cells
- Dopamine is depleted as a consequence of this.
- The ↓ dopamine leads to:
- ↑ inhibitory drive to the thalamus, suppressing movement
- Changes in background tone	- rigidity
- Releasing of inhibition of tremor

## Slide 13

- Cardinal sign: Resting Tremor
- Involuntary oscillation of body parts at slow frequency of about 4 to 6Hz
- initial symptom 50% of PD cases
- Resting tremor; “pill-rolling”
- Postural tremor (8-12Hz) may also be present
- Diminishes with voluntary effort; disappears completely during sleep
- Aggravated by stress and fatigue
- Origin is disputed; hypothesized to be as a result of oscillations in a hyperactive long loop reflex pathway

## Slide 14

- Cardinal sign: Rigidity
- Increased stiffness	- passive ROM
- cogwheel and lead pipe; cogwheel rigidity is pathognomonic of PD
- unequal in distribution
- decreases ability to move easily
- Rigidity affecting neck, trunk and knees leads to stooped posture
- Not constant – affected by stress, anxiety, movt of contralateral limb, posture

## Slide 15

- Cardinal sign: Bradykinesia
- Bradykinesia	- used interchangeably with akinesia and hypokinesia
- Hypokinesia –reduced amplitude of movement
- Bradykinesia – slowness of movement
- Akinesia – loss of movement, slowness to initiate, freezing while moving
- Freezing –refers to the difficulty starting	or continuing
- rhythmic movement e.g. walking, handwriting , speech
- It is the most disabling manifestation of PD

## Slide 16

- Cardinal Sign: Freezing
- Freezing – refers to difficulty starting or continuing rhythmic
- repetitive movements
- Schools of thought exit which considers it a distinct clinical sign of PD
- Freezing of Gait (FOG) is an episodic gait disturbance typically
- experienced when walking
- Festination often occurs prior to freezing
- Feet appears glued to the floor while momentum carries body mass
- forward
- Occurs more frequently in cluttered environments, stressful circumstances or when patient is distracted
- Likelihood of falls increases

## Slide 17

- Development of PD
- Early stage may be vague and non-specific:
- e.g. inexplicable tiredness, unwarranted fatigability and mild
- muscle aches and cramps
- Cognitive impairment affecting executive function & memory
- – 25% of newly diagnosed cases
- Excessive levels of physical and mental fatigue common
- Depression may be present and adds to reduced QoL
- As disease progresses
- Clinical signs and symptoms develops
- Leads to embarrassing and upsetting incidence
- Movement difficulties compromises independence and confidence

## Slide 18

- Problems in PD
- Loss of automaticity: - inability to perform movement without attention
- directed towards the movement
- Habitual behaviour is affected
- Flexibility of motor behaviour is reduced
- Dual tasking is impaired
- Exaggerated dependence on external motor drive
- Contributes to micrographia, decreased arm swing, decreased stride length, reduced facial expression

## Slide 21

## Slide 22

- Greater incidence than neurologically intact adults
- Injuries particularly fractures common -	5 to 9 times the risk of age-matched adults without	PD to sustain a hip fracture
- Environmental factors – moderately correlated with falls; 	rapid turns, complex tasks, and transfers: highly correlated 	with falls
- Fear of falling

## Slide 30

- Unified Parkinson Rating Scale (UPDRS)
- UDPRS
- Part I: evaluation of mentation, behaviour, and mood
- Part II: self-evaluation of the activities of daily life (ADLs) including speech, swallowing, handwriting, dressing, hygiene, falling, salivating, turning in bed, walking, and cutting food
- Part III: clinician-scored monitored motor evaluation
- Part IV: complications of therapy
- Part V: Hoehn and Yahr staging of severity of Parkinson's disease
- Part VI: Schwab and England ADL scale
- MDC –UPDRS
- Part I (non-motor experiences of daily living),
- Part II (motor experiences of daily living),
- Part III (motor examination) and
- Part IV (motor complications).
- (Fahn et al. 1987)

## Slide 31

- Medical Management
- No cure for PD
- Pharmacological management:
- –Levodopa (L-dopa) – mainstay of therapy
- Usually reserved for patients in middle & late stages
- Initial improvement often dramatic “honeymoon period”; long term use
- effectiveness deteriorates
- End-of-dose deterioration ; On-off phenomenon
- –COMT – catecholamine-O-methyltransferase
- –Monoamine oxidase (MAO)
- –Amantadine
- – Side effects are common with drug management
- Surgery is considered (more advanced cases)
- Deep brain stimulation (DBS)
- Nutritional management, surgical management

## Slide 59

- Summary
- PD is a chronic, progressive disorder of the basal 	ganglia characterised by the cardinal features of 	rigidity, tremor, and bradykinesia
- Pharmacological interventions have become the 	mainstay of treatment
- Physiotherapy interventions focus on management of strength, ROM, endurance and functional skills, balance, gait according to disease stage
- MDT involvement at all stages to provide psychosocial support as needed and to direct patient towards maintenance of function
