1. Tell me about your fatigue — when is it worst, what triggers it, and
does it improve with rest?
💡 MS fatigue is multifactorial. I need to distinguish primary central
fatigue (driven by increased cortical effort to conduct signals along
demyelinated axons) from secondary fatigue (deconditioning, disturbed
sleep, depression). I also want to identify Uhthoff's phenomenon —
symptom worsening with heat — which directly shapes my exercise
environment and scheduling. This question drives my fatigue
management strategy.
2. Can you describe the leg heaviness when you walk — at what point
does it come on, does it affect both legs equally, and what does the
walking look like when it gets bad?
💡 This characterises the functional impairment in detail. I want to know: is
this weakness, spasticity, fatigue, or foot drop? Does it follow a consistent
threshold (suggesting heat/metabolic loading of demyelinated pathways)
or is it variable? The answer determines whether I target strength, gait re-
education, ankle-foot orthosis referral, or energy conservation first.
3. Describe the pins and needles in your feet — constant or
intermittent, does it affect your confidence or balance on different
surfaces?
💡 Sensory loss in the feet reduces proprioceptive afferent input to the
cerebellum and somatosensory cortex. This directly degrades postural
stability. I need to know whether Sophie's balance relies heavily on visual
input to compensate (suggesting dorsal column involvement), which
shapes my balance retraining approach. It also informs whether sensory
substitution strategies are appropriate.
4. Have you had any falls or near-falls in the last three months — and
does your balance feel different in the dark, or on uneven ground?
💡 Fall history is the strongest single predictor of future falls in MS. This
determines the urgency and intensity of my balance programme. Asking

about darkness and uneven terrain probes whether she's compensating
with vision — if she's much worse in the dark, that confirms proprioceptive
impairment is dominant over vestibular. This directly changes my exercise
design.
5. You mentioned being terrified exercise might cause a relapse —
can you tell me more about where that belief came from?
💡 This is potentially the most important question I ask. Fear-avoidance
and kinesiophobia are major barriers to rehabilitation in RRMS. If I don't
address this belief explicitly and with evidence, she will not adhere to any
programme I prescribe. Understanding the source of the fear tells me
whether to use brief education, motivational interviewing, or a graded
exposure approach. The evidence is clear: moderate exercise does not
trigger relapses in RRMS.
6. How is your vision since the optic neuritis — any residual blurring,
colour change, or depth perception problems?
💡 Residual visual deficits from optic neuritis impair the visual
compensation that her nervous system is likely using to offset the
proprioceptive loss. If vision is compromised, her balance risk is higher
than it appears — and I cannot rely on visual feedback-based balance
exercises as heavily. I need to know this before designing her balance
programme.
RED FLAGS
🚨 New or worsening bladder / bowel dysfunction
Significance: May indicate a new relapse with spinal cord involvement
(transverse myelitis pattern). Also a major fall risk — urgency
incontinence causes rushed, unbalanced movement to the toilet. Can
precipitate significant distress.
Action: Urgent MS nurse / neurologist notification. Continence
physiotherapy referral. Withhold intensive exercise until relapse ruled out.

🚨 New neurological deficit within the past 4–6 weeks (possible
active relapse)
Significance: A relapse is defined as new or worsening neurological
symptoms lasting >24 hours in the absence of fever/infection. Exercise
during an active relapse can worsen symptoms and delays recovery.
Action: Refer back to neurologist urgently. Suspend formal exercise
programme until relapse confirmed resolved (typically ≥4 weeks post-
relapse onset).
🚨 Lhermitte's sign (new onset)
Significance: An electric shock sensation down the spine on neck flexion
— indicates active cervical cord demyelination. New onset suggests an
evolving lesion or early relapse.
Action: Avoid all cervical flexion-loaded activities. Urgent neurologist
referral. MRI cervical spine may be indicated.
🚨 New or rapidly worsening visual loss
Significance: Given the recent optic neuritis, any new visual loss could
represent a second attack on the optic nerve. Bilateral optic neuritis
should raise suspicion of NMOSD rather than RRMS.
Action: Do not proceed with assessment. Urgent ophthalmology and
neurology referral. Consider aquaporin-4 antibody serology.
🚨 Severe or persistent depression / suicidal ideation
Significance: Depression prevalence in MS is approximately 50%. Severe
depression makes meaningful engagement with rehabilitation impossible
and carries significant personal risk.
Action: PHQ-9 screening. Urgent GP or psychology referral. Involve MS
specialist nurse. Do not prioritise exercise over mental health crisis.

💡 With SPMS, I signal from the opening that I respect her pace,
understand that fatigue and effort cost are real, and that I'm there to work
with her priorities — not impose a rehabilitation agenda she hasn't bought
into.
KEY QUESTIONS + CLINICAL REASONING
1. Can you describe the spasticity in your legs — when is it at its
worst, what triggers it, and how does it interfere with your personal
care and transfers?
💡 Spasticity in SPMS is the central clinical problem and is the prerequisite
barrier to everything else. I need to understand: is it worse in the morning
(diurnal pattern — common in UMN spasticity), triggered by specific
movements, or constantly severe? Identifying triggers is critical because
the most effective intervention may be treating a precipitating cause (UTI,
constipation, pressure area) rather than physiotherapy alone. The impact
on personal care and transfers defines my functional goal-setting.
2. Have you had any changes in your bladder or bowel recently —
urgency, frequency, incomplete emptying, or constipation?
💡 This is simultaneously a red flag screen and a spasticity precipitant
inquiry — the two most important functions of the same question. Bladder
dysfunction affects 75–80% of people with MS. A urinary tract infection or
bladder distension is the single most common cause of sudden spasticity
increase. If present, treating the UTI may resolve the spasticity escalation
without any additional physiotherapy intervention. I must ask this before I
do anything else.
3. Tell me about Lhermitte's sign — is it longstanding or has it
changed recently, what position triggers it, and how long does the
sensation last?

💡 Lhermitte's sign indicates cervical dorsal column pathology.
Longstanding, stable Lhermitte's tells me to avoid cervical flexion-loaded
positions in treatment — relevant to positioning, exercise, and
hydrotherapy. New or worsening Lhermitte's is a potential red flag
suggesting active disease. The answer directly restricts or permits certain
physiotherapy techniques and positions.
4. Walk me through a typical transfer — for example, bed to chair and
chair to toilet. At exactly which point does it break down, and what
does it look like when it goes wrong?
💡 Transfer safety is Catherine's highest-priority functional problem. I need
a task-analysis-level understanding of the transfer sequence to identify the
specific failure point: is it the initial rise from sitting (quadriceps/hip
extensor weakness), the pivot step (adductor spasticity closing the legs), or
the controlled lowering (eccentric control failure)? Identifying the exact
failure point allows me to prescribe a targeted intervention rather than a
generic transfer training programme.
5. Are you having any pain — particularly in the legs, hips, or lower
back — and have you or your carer noticed any skin changes,
redness, or pressure areas?
💡 Pain in SPMS is commonly neuropathic (dysaesthesia from dorsal horn
sensitisation) or musculoskeletal secondary to spasticity (adductor-origin
groin pain, hip flexor tightness). Both worsen spasticity via nociceptive
input to the spinal cord. Skin breakdown is both a medical emergency and
a major spasticity precipitant. I must screen proactively — patients with
limited mobility often do not spontaneously report pressure changes.
6. Who helps you with your care at home, and how are they managing
with the transfers? Are there any concerns about their safety or
yours during care?
💡 Carer capacity and technique directly determine whether my
rehabilitation plan is deliverable at home. If the carer is using unsafe

manual handling, both Catherine and the carer are at injury risk. I need to
know this so I can include carer moving-and-handling training in my plan.
Carer burden and burnout are also real — if the carer is struggling, the
whole care package is at risk of breakdown.
RED FLAGS
🚨 Sudden or significantly worsening Lhermitte's sign
Significance: May indicate a new active cervical cord lesion, cord
compression (rare but possible — atlantoaxial or subaxial), or evolving
myelopathy. New or dramatically worsened Lhermitte's is a neurological
review trigger.
Action: Avoid all cervical flexion-loaded activities immediately. Urgent
neurologist referral. MRI cervical spine may be required. Suspend neck-
involving physiotherapy until reviewed.
🚨 Rapidly escalating spasticity over days or weeks
Significance: In SPMS, sudden spasticity escalation is almost always
triggered by a noxious stimulus — UTI, pressure ulcer, deep vein
thrombosis, ingrowing toenail, bowel impaction, fracture — rather than
disease progression alone. Missing this keeps the precipitant untreated
and makes all physiotherapy interventions ineffective.
Action: Systematic screen for all noxious precipitants. Urgent GP referral
for UTI/urinalysis. Do not escalate physiotherapy intensity until the cause
is identified and treated.
🚨 New or worsening pressure ulcer / skin breakdown
Significance: In SPMS with reduced mobility and sustained spasticity,
pressure ulcers are a major complication. They cause significant pain, are
a potent spasticity trigger, and can lead to life-threatening sepsis.
Action: Urgent tissue viability nurse referral. Immediate seating and
pressure relief review. Halt exercise over or adjacent to affected areas.
Document and escalate same day.

🚨 Autonomic dysreflexia features (if high spinal cord involvement
suspected)
Significance: In MS with high thoracic or cervical cord lesions, noxious
stimuli can trigger a sudden, massive, potentially fatal sympathetic surge:
severe hypertension, pounding headache, facial flushing, profuse
sweating, bradycardia. A medical emergency.
Action: Immediately sit patient upright; identify and remove the trigger
(full bladder, tight clothing, pressure). Call for emergency medical help.
Do not continue treatment.
🚨 Signs of deep vein thrombosis in lower limb
Significance: Reduced mobility in SPMS significantly elevates DVT risk.
Unilateral leg swelling, warmth, redness, and calf tenderness warrant
urgent investigation. A DVT is also a major spasticity precipitant.
Action: Wells Score assessment; urgent GP/ED referral for Doppler
ultrasound. Withhold passive stretching of the affected limb until
excluded.

PSYCHOSOCIAL / YELLOW FLAGS
⚠️ Grief and Adjustment to Progressive Disability
Secondary progressive MS is a diagnosis of unremitting decline. Catherine
will be navigating loss of independence, roles, and identity. I use a "What
Matters to You" framework for goal-setting — focusing on her priorities
(dignity in personal care, safe transfers) rather than imposing
impairment-based goals. Psychology or MS counselling referral if
adjustment difficulties are significant.
⚠️ Carer Burden and Fatigue
If the carer is struggling — physically, emotionally, or practically — the
care package is at risk of breakdown, and both parties are at injury risk
from unsafe manual handling. I will involve the carer explicitly in all
sessions, provide formal moving-and-handling training, and escalate
carer support needs to social work and community care services if
required.
