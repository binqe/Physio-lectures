I PATHOPHYSIOLOGY (KEY POINTS)
• Ibrahim has sustained an ischaemic infarct of the left cerebellar hemisphere , with probable involvement of the vermis given the prominent truncal
ataxia.
• The cerebellar vermis specifically coordinates axial and gait stability, and its damage produces the characteristic wide-based gait, trunk sway, and
staggering steps Mr.
• Sign / Symptom Pathophysiological Explanation "Boat rocking" sensation (oscillopsia) Disrupted cerebellar-vestibular integration — the cerebellum
normally damps oscillatory vestibular signals.
• Without this damping, a persistent sense of motion is experienced even at rest.
• Dysmetria / past-pointing The cerebellum computes the "brake" signal that terminates a limb movement precisely at the target.
• Without it, movements overshoot or undershoot — visible as finger-nose past-pointing and heel-shin inaccuracy.

I OBJECTIVE ASSESSMENTS & HOW TO PERFORM
Key Neurological Observations Before Testing
Tone: Passive movement of left UL and LL — expect ipsilateral hypotonia (cerebellar hypotonia). No spasticity expected; note any that is present as it would
suggest additional corticospinal involvement. Coordination: Finger-nose test (dysmetria, intention tremor), heel-shin test (trajectory accuracy), rapid
alternating movements (dysdiadochokinesia if present) — all expected to be impaired ipsilaterally. Posture:
Test 1 — Berg Balance Scale (BBS)
What it measures: Static and dynamic balance across 14 functional tasks (sitting, standing, reaching, turning, tandem standing, single-leg stance). Scored
0–56; <45 indicates significantly elevated fall risk; MCID = 4 points. Why I choose it: The BBS is the gold-standard functional balance measure for stroke
rehabilitation in the community. With two near-miss falls in the last month, documenting balance severity with a validated, widely-used tool is essential for
justifying ongoing physiotherapy, communicating risk to the GP and MDT, and setting measurable rehabilitation goals. Critically, the BBS items include
tandem standing, turning 360°, and reaching — the exact functional challenges Mr. Ibrahim reports — making it directly ecologically relevant to his
presentation. How to perform:
Test 2 — Timed Up and Go Test (TUG)
What it measures: Functional mobility — time to rise from a chair, walk 3 metres, turn, return and sit. Normative values for healthy adults aged 60+ ≈ 9–10
seconds; >13.5 seconds = significantly elevated fall risk. MCID = 2.9 seconds post-stroke. Why I choose it: The TUG specifically captures the transitions Mr.
Ibrahim reports as his highest-risk moments: rising from a chair, turning, and walking. It is quick, practical, and can be repeated at every session to track
progress. I will also administer the Dual-Task TUG (walking + counting backwards from 20 by 3s) to simulate real-world cognitive-motor demands — in
cerebellar ataxia, dual-task performance degrades significantly because the damaged integration system has reduced spare capacity, and this directly
reflects community mobility ability. How to perform:

• Core Neurophysio Interventions:
• Task-specific cerebellar coordination training (adapted Frenkel's exercises):
• Highly repetitive, progressively demanding coordination tasks targeting the specific impairments — dysmetria and trunk ataxia.
• Progressive balance retraining using sensory manipulation (mCTSIB-guided):
• Begin at the condition where the patient is just at the limit of stability; systematically progress by removing vision, challenging the surface, and adding
conflicting visual backgrounds.
• Gait retraining — systematic base-of-support narrowing with directional challenge:
• Progressively narrow walking stance, add deliberate turns (beginning at 45°, progressing to 90° and 180°), change surfaces (carpet, grass, gravel), add
obstacles.
