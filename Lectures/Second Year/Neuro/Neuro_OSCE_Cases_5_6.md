1. "What are the main things that have been bothering you

Significance: May indicate Parkinson's Disease Dementia (PDD),

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF WORKING DIAGNOSIS
WHAT IS HAPPENING NEUROLOGICALLY
Parkinson's Disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder
characterised by the selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the
substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc). These neurons project via the
nigrostriatal pathway to the striatum (caudate nucleus and putamen),
where dopamine normally facilitates the direct pathway — the "go"
signal for voluntary movement — while modulating the indirect
pathway — the "stop" signal that suppresses unwanted motor
programmes. As dopaminergic neurons are lost (typically 60–80% must
be lost before motor symptoms appear), the direct pathway becomes
underactive and the indirect pathway becomes overactive, resulting in
excessive inhibitory output from the globus pallidus interna (GPi) and
substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) to the thalamus.
This thalamic suppression dampens motor cortex excitation, producing
the cardinal features of hypokinesia, bradykinesia, and akinesia.
Concurrently, misfolded alpha-synuclein aggregates form Lewy bodies
within surviving neurons; these propagate through the nervous system
in a predictable pattern (Braak staging), eventually involving the cortex,

limbic system, and autonomic nervous system — explaining the wide
spectrum of non-motor symptoms. By the time of Mr. Vance's

before his resting tremor became the presenting complaint.
TYPICAL SIGNS AND SYMPTOMS — EXPLAINED BY
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
Resting tremor (4–6 Hz, "pill-rolling"): Arises from pathological
oscillations in the thalamo-cortical and basal ganglia-thalamic
circuits when normal dampening by the dopaminergic system is
lost. Suppressed during voluntary movement because cortical
volitional drive temporarily overrides the oscillation — this is why
the tremor disappears when he reaches for a cup.
Bradykinesia and akinesia: Direct consequence of reduced
dopaminergic facilitation through the direct pathway — the motor
cortex receives less thalamic excitation, meaning movement
initiation requires much greater cortical effort. Movement amplitude
and velocity progressively decline, and initiation is delayed or fails
(akinesia).
Rigidity (cogwheel or leadpipe): Loss of basal ganglia modulation
of descending motor pathways leads to co-contraction of agonist
and antagonist muscle groups simultaneously. Passive joint
movement feels uniformly stiff (leadpipe) or intermittently
"catching" (cogwheel, thought to be rigidity superimposed on
tremor).
Stooped posture and reduced arm swing: Flexor dominance
emerges from rigidity — the flexor muscle groups are tonically over-
active relative to extensors. Reduced arm swing is a direct
expression of right-sided bradykinesia and rigidity reducing the
amplitude of the normal reciprocal arm movement during gait.
Micrographia: A form of bradykinesia specific to sequential,
repetitive fine motor tasks — movement amplitude progressively
decreases during the task as dopaminergic drive is insufficient to
sustain normal motor scaling across repeated strokes.

DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES
DIFFERENTIAL 1: ESSENTIAL TREMOR (ET)
Most common movement disorder in adults; bilateral upper limb
tremor is frequent in men over 60
Supporting features that could overlap: upper limb tremor, older
male, affecting handwriting
Key distinguishing features of ET: tremor is kinetic and postural
(worsens on action and sustained posture, e.g. holding a cup
outstretched) — opposite to PD resting tremor which reduces on
action; no bradykinesia; no rigidity; no gait or posture changes;
often positive family history; improves with alcohol; no response to
levodopa
DIFFERENTIAL 2: PROGRESSIVE SUPRANUCLEAR PALSY (PSP)
A Parkinson's-Plus syndrome with parkinsonism, early falls, and
supranuclear gaze palsy

Supporting features that could overlap: stooped posture (though
PSP tends to cause axial extension rather than flexion), bradykinesia,

Key distinguishing features of PSP: axial rigidity greater than limb

I favour idiopathic Parkinson's Disease over Essential Tremor

specific indicator of basal ganglia dysfunction — accompanied by
bradykinesia (micrographia, slow gait, akinetic sit-to-stand) and
postural changes, none of which occur in ET. I favour PD over PSP
because his onset is insidious over 12 months with predominantly
right-sided (asymmetric) features, he has no early severe falls or

Test 1: Timed Up and Go (TUG)
What it measures: A composite measure of functional
mobility including sit-to-stand, walking, turning (180°), walking
back, and returning to sitting. It captures gait initiation speed,
walking velocity, turning ability, and sit-to-stand — exactly the
functional problems Mr. Vance reports. It also serves as a fall
risk screening tool.
How to perform:
1. Use a standard chair with armrests (height ~46 cm); patient
seated fully back in the chair
2. Place a cone or mark on the floor exactly 3 metres in front
of the chair
3. Instruct: "When I say go, stand up, walk to the marker, turn
around, walk back, and sit down. Walk at your comfortable,
normal pace."
4. Begin timing on "go"; stop the clock when the patient is
fully seated
5. Allow one practice trial to familiarise; record two timed
trials and take the mean

6. Observe quality: hesitation at initiation, arm swing, step
length, turning strategy, how they lower to sit
Positive / Abnormal finding: >12 seconds is associated with
increased fall risk in community-dwelling older adults. In PD,
>11.5 seconds indicates significant mobility impairment.
However, I pay as much attention to quality as time —
hesitation at sit-to-stand, a narrow shuffling turn, absent arm
swing, and a slow lower back to the chair are all clinically
significant observations even if time is borderline.
Why I chose it: TUG directly captures both of the functional
concerns raised — difficulty starting from a chair and reduced
walking speed while also providing a standardised, repeatable
outcome measure. It requires no equipment beyond a chair
and a stopwatch, can be performed in any clinic room, and
has good sensitivity for detecting early gait impairment in PD.
It is my primary baseline measure for tracking treatment

Test 2: Pull Test (Postural Instability — MDS-UPDRS Item
3.12)
What it measures: Integrity of automatic postural righting
reflexes — the ability to recover from an unexpected backward
perturbation without taking excessive corrective steps or
falling. Directly assesses a key domain of the Hoehn & Yahr
staging (postural instability distinguishes H&Y III from II).
How to perform:
1. Patient stands with feet shoulder-width apart, eyes open, in
a clear space
2. Inform patient: "I'm going to give you a firm pull
backwards on your shoulders. Try to stay balanced — you
may take a step if you need to."
3. Stand directly behind the patient, hands on both shoulders;
position yourself to catch them

4. Deliver a brisk, firm backward pull (not a light nudge — it
should be genuinely challenging)
5. Count the number of corrective steps taken; observe
whether they recover independently
Positive / Abnormal finding: Normal = 0–1 corrective steps.
Mildly abnormal = 2–3 steps (UPDRS score 1–2). Severely
abnormal = 4+ steps, or would fall without being caught
(UPDRS score 3–4). Even 2 corrective steps in early PD is
clinically meaningful as it predicts future falls and indicates
postural reflex impairment earlier than patients typically
report symptoms.
Why I chose it: Postural instability is the single strongest
predictor of falls in PD and is the key variable that
differentiates Hoehn & Yahr stages. Establishing this baseline
at 12 months — when it may still be mildly impaired or normal
— allows me to detect deterioration at future reviews, justify
proactive balance training even before instability becomes
severe, and communicate clearly to the neurologist about
staging.
Test 3: Finger-Tapping Speed & Amplitude (MDS-UPDRS Item
3.4)
What it measures: Bradykinesia of the right upper limb —
specifically the speed, amplitude, and pattern of repetitive
sequential finger movements. A decremental pattern
(progressively slowing and decreasing amplitude over 10
seconds) is highly characteristic of PD and reflects
dopaminergic motor drive failure during sustained motor
output.
How to perform:
1. Demonstrate to patient: tap index finger to thumb
repeatedly, as fast as possible and as wide as possible —
emphasise "big and fast"

3. Each hand performs 10 seconds of continuous tapping

TREATMENT PLAN
INITIAL PHASE — WEEKS 1–4: RATIONALE
My overarching goals in this phase are to address bradykinesia and
hypokinesia through high-amplitude, high-effort movement training
(consistent with LSVT BIG principles), improve gait quality and sit-to-
stand function, and build Mr. Vance's and his wife's self-management
capability. Evidence strongly supports that physiotherapy initiated in
early PD — before compensatory strategies become entrenched —
exploits remaining neuroplasticity to slow functional decline. I will
schedule sessions during his documented "on" medication window.
Exercise 1: LSVT BIG — Large Amplitude Whole-Body
Movements
Clinical reasoning: In PD, the internal sense of "normal"
movement amplitude is recalibrated downward by the basal
ganglia — Mr. Vance's brain believes small movements are
normal-sized. LSVT BIG exploits preserved cortical pathways by
training conscious, exaggerated large-amplitude movements. The
key insight is that what feels "too big" to the patient is actually
closer to normal. Evidence demonstrates improvements in gait
speed, stride length, balance, and ADLs following this approach.
How to perform: Select 8 whole-body functional movements (e.g.
large arm swings forward and back, wide step forward and return,
trunk rotation left and right, overhead reach). Instruct with
maximum amplitude: "Think BIG — make every movement as
large as you possibly can." Use verbal cueing: "BIG step," "WIDE

## Page 15

swing." Perform 8 repetitions × 4 sets, daily. Apply the principle to
ADLs immediately: "Take a BIG step when you stand up. Swing
your arms BIG when you walk."
Regression: Seated position; reduce to 4 repetitions; therapist
provides tactile cueing on limb to guide amplitude; focus on
single movements before combining; reduce pace
Progression: Add dual-task challenge (count backwards by 3
while performing big steps); increase walking speed while
maintaining big arm swing; practise on outdoor uneven

Exercise 2: Cueing-Based Sit-to-Stand Training
Clinical reasoning: Sit-to-stand difficulty is akinesia — failure to
initiate a stored motor programme. External cueing strategies
(auditory counts, visual targets, attentional focus instructions)
activate the supplementary motor cortex and lateral premotor
cortex directly, bypassing the deficient basal ganglia initiation
circuit. This is one of the most functionally critical transfers in daily
life and a documented safety risk (falling when rising from a
chair).
How to perform: Seat Mr. Vance at edge of chair, feet hip-width
apart behind knees, hands on thighs. Teach the "nose-over-toes"
strategy: lean trunk forward until nose is over toes before
standing — this shifts the centre of mass forward over the feet,
reducing the motor threshold for initiation. Apply rhythmic
auditory cue: therapist counts "1… 2… 3… STAND!" or patient
learns to self-cue. Also practise using a floor target to step onto
after standing (provides a movement goal to drive initiation). 10
repetitions × 3 sets per session.
Regression: Raise chair seat height (use cushion or raised
toilet frame); allow armrest push-up; reduce to 5 repetitions;

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF WORKING DIAGNOSIS
WHAT IS HAPPENING NEUROLOGICALLY — ADVANCED PD
After 12 years, Mr. Miller's disease has progressed to a state of near-
total dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra, with Lewy body
pathology (Braak stage V–VI) extensively involving the frontal cortex,

## Page 25

limbic system, brainstem reticular formation, and autonomic nervous
system. The basal ganglia thalamocortical motor circuits are profoundly
disrupted — the GPi and SNr generate massive inhibitory output onto
the thalamus, suppressing motor cortex activation to a degree that
makes voluntary movement initiation extraordinarily effortful and
unreliable, particularly for complex, multi-step motor sequences such
as turning.
Freezing of Gait (FOG) in advanced PD is understood to arise from
dysfunction at multiple levels: (1) failure of the mesencephalic
locomotor region (MLR) and pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN) to receive
adequate facilitation from the basal ganglia, disrupting the spinal
locomotor rhythm generator; (2) impaired communication between the
subthalamic nucleus and PPN that normally coordinates gait initiation
and continuous stepping; and (3) attentional resource competition —
when cognitive demands are high (navigating a small kitchen, planning
a directional change), the deficient motor system cannot
simultaneously process spatial navigation and maintain continuous gait
rhythm, resulting in a complete cessation of movement. This explains
why FOG is most dangerous in confined, complex environments.
Motor fluctuations — the "on-off" phenomenon — arise because after
12 years of levodopa therapy, the remaining dopaminergic neurons
have lost their capacity to buffer and gradually release dopamine.
Dopamine availability in the striatum now mirrors levodopa plasma
levels directly, oscillating sharply with each dose cycle. When levodopa
levels fall (wearing-off) or fluctuate unpredictably (on-off), the striatum
loses dopaminergic tone abruptly, and the patient transitions from
functional mobility to near-immobility within minutes. This sudden
transition — combined with FOG and impaired postural reflexes — is
what makes advanced PD so dangerous from a falls perspective.

Technique 2 — Safe Wide U-Turn Teaching: Demonstrate both
turning strategies side by side — first the dangerous single-foot
pivot (explain: "Full weight on one leg, crossing over, pivot — this is
how a turn becomes a fall in advanced PD"). Then demonstrate the
wide U-turn: multiple small steps in a clear arc, feet never crossing,
wide base of support throughout. Invite the examiner to try both.
Explain: "The wide U-turn keeps both feet under the base of support
at all times. There is no single-limb stance phase during the turn.
The trade-off is it takes longer and requires more space — which is
why the kitchen layout modification is also essential. I would mark
the wide turning arc on Mr. Miller's kitchen floor with tape so the
path is always visually guided."
