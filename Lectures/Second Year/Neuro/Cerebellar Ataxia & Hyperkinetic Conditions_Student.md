Learning Objective:
By the end of this session, students should be able to:

•

•
Perform and document a systematic clinical assessment for cerebellar dysfunction
•

•
Formulate SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Timely) tailored to 
ataxic patients.
•
Design an evidence-based treatment plan focusing on core stability, weighted feedback, 
and compensatory strategies.

## Page 4

The Cerebellum (1)
•
Anatomy
•
The little brain
•
Location: Situated at the back of the head, tucked below the temporal 
and occipital lobes and positioned above the brainstem.
•
Structure: Much like the cerebrum, it consists of two hemispheres with 
an outer layer of neurons and an inner area that communicates with 
the cerebral cortex.
•
Ipsilateral Control: Unlike the motor cortex, the cerebellum influences 
the same side of the body (e.g., a left-sided lesion leads to left-sided 
ataxia).
•
Primary Roles Quality Control
•
Coordination: Its main function is to coordinate voluntary muscle 
movements to ensure they are smooth and precise.
•
Balance & Posture: It is the primary center for maintaining balance, 
equilibrium, and steady posture.
•
Motor Learning: It plays a vital role in "fine-tuning" movements through 
repetition (e.g., learning to play an instrument or perfecting a gait 
pattern).

What is Ataxia? (2)
• Ataxia:
• Derived from the Greek word ataxis, meaning "without order.
• A neurological disorder characterized by a lack of voluntary coordination of muscle movements 
• It is the result of damage to the parts of the brain responsible for coordination and balance—primarily the 
cerebellum
• "disordered contractions of agonist and antagonist muscles and the lack of normal coordination between 
movements at different joints," emphasizing the lack of "order"
• Ataxia is the neurological impairment, whereas Ataxic Gait is the functional consequence.
• Key Characteristics
•
Impaired Coordination: Difficulty with fine motor tasks like picking up objects or writing [Loss of Balance: An 
"unsteady" or "staggering" gait, often compared to being intoxicated 
•
Dysarthria: Slurred or slow speech (scanning speech) due to poor coordination of the vocal muscles 
•
Visual Disturbances: Nystagmus (involuntary eye movements) and difficulty with gaze stabilization 
• The Three Functional Types:
•
Cerebellar Ataxia: Damage to the cerebellum itself 
•
Sensory Ataxia: Damage to the nerves that carry "position sense" (proprioception) to the brain 
•
Vestibular Ataxia: Damage to the inner ear and balance systems

## Page 24

Subjective Assessment – The "Ataxia" Interview (4)
In ataxia, the history often reveals "clumsy" functional failures rather than "weakness" failures.
Specific Questions to Ask:
Nature of the Deficit: "Do you feel like your legs are weak, or just that they don't go where 
you want them to?" (Distinguishes between UMN weakness and cerebellar incoordination).
Falls History: "When you lose your balance, do you tend to fall in a specific direction 
(left/right) or is it unpredictable?" (Helps localize the side of the lesion).
Vision & Environment: "Is it harder to walk in the dark or on uneven surfaces like grass?" 
(Identifies if they are compensating with vision).
Functional Impact: "Do you find yourself 'furniture walking' or using walls for support at 
home?"
Fine Motor: "Have you noticed changes in your handwriting or difficulty with buttons and 
zippers?".

## Page 25

Objective Assessment - Strength & Sensation (5, 6)
Strength (The Power):
•
Manual Muscle Testing (MMT): Assess for true weakness vs. movement inefficiency.
•
Proximal Stability: Specifically test the core, glutes, and shoulder girdle. 
•
Patients often feel weak because their movements are inefficient, but you may find their raw 
muscle strength is actually 5/5
•
Documentation Tip: "Strength is 5/5 bilaterally, but movement is poorly graded with significant 
tremor during eccentric control.” or ‘’UL strength is within functional limits’’
Sensation (The Input):
•
Proprioception & Kinaesthesia: Can the brain "see" where the limb is without looking?.
•
Visual Dependency (Romberg’s): Does their balance significantly worsen when they close their 
eyes? This tells us if they are using their eyes to compensate for a faulty cerebellum.
•
Documentation Tips: 
•
"Pt. unable to accurately identify hallux position; requires visual monitoring for limb placement."
•
"Impaired kinaesthesia noted in R upper limb; pt. overshoots intended target during reaching."
•
"Positive Romberg’s noted; pt. demonstrates significant lateral sway and requires physical assist when vision is removed."

## Page 26

Let’s Practice Those Tests! (4, 6)
Joint Position Sense Test:
Focus: Static awareness of a joint's position.
1.
Have the patient sit or lie comfortably. Explain the test: "I 
am going to move your toe/finger up or down. I want you 
to tell me which way it is pointing without looking.”
2.
Grasp the sides of the distal phalanx (e.g., the big toe or 
thumb).
3.
With the patient’s eyes open, move the joint into extreme 
"Up" and "Down" positions so they understand the 
terminology.
4.
Ask the patient to close their eyes.
5.
Move the joint in small, unpredictable increments (about 
10°–15°). Once you have stopped moving ask "Is your toe 
pointing up or down?"
6.
Perform at least 5–10 trials.
Documentation: "Patient correctly identifies 3/10 trials at the R 
Hallux; indicates impaired proprioception."
Mirroring Test:
Focus: Dynamic awareness of movement and limb-to-limb 
coordination.
1.
Have the patient sit with both arms free. Explain: "I am 
going to move one of your arms into a specific position. I 
want you to try and copy that exact position with your 
other arm at the same time.”
2.
Ask the patient to close their eyes to ensure we are testing 
the internal "map," not visual tracking.
3.
Take the patient's affected limb (the one with suspected 
ataxia) and move it slowly into a mid-range position (e.g., 
90° shoulder flexion with elbow at 45°). Instruct the 
patient: "Move your other arm to match this one perfectly.”
4.
Look for accuracy
5.
Progress the Challenge - Move the limb through a 
dynamic path (like a circle) and ask them to mirror the 
movement simultaneously.
"Impaired upper limb kinaesthesia noted; pt. demonstrated a 
consistent 20-30° undershoot when mirroring shoulder 
flexion with eyes closed."

Objective Assessment - Balance (Static & Dynamic) (1,4)
Assessment of the "Vermis" (midline) of the cerebellum.
Sitting Balance (on plinth):
Observe for Truncal Ataxia (swaying/titubation of the trunk or head).
Document the use of Upper Limb (UL) support (e.g., "Sits on plinth with Rt arm support; cannot sit 
without it").
Standing Balance:
Base of Support (BOS): Are they standing with feet wide apart to increase stability?.
Postural Sway: Note the direction and frequency of sway.
Reactions: Assess "Protective Stepping"—ataxic patients often have delayed or "overshot" steps 
when nudged.

## Page 28

Objective Assessment - Functional Activities (2, 5, 4)
1. Bed Mobility & Sit to Stand
• Bed Mobility: Look for "all or nothing" movements. Instead of a smooth roll, do they "heave" and then "flop" onto 
their side?.
• Sit to Stand: Note the timing of the lean forward. Do they struggle to keep weight over their feet (e.g., "Unable to 
keep weight over left foot during stand")?
2. Reaching & Walking
• Reaching: Assess for Intention Tremor and Dysmetria (past-pointing).
• Documentation: "During reach, pt. overshoots the target (dysmetria) and demonstrates a 2cm intention tremor."
• Walking (Gait Analysis): * Identify a broad-based gait or lateral veering.
• Use timed tests like the 10-meter walk test to measure speed and efficiency.
3. Stairs
• Ascent/Descent: This is a high-risk task. Observe for "foot-slapping" (poor force scaling) and heavy reliance on 
handrails for sensory feedback.
• Safety: Document if the patient requires a "spotter" or physical assistance from one to maintain balance.

## Page 30

Key Safety Risks in Ataxia & Hyperkinesis (2, 4, 6)
1. Unpredictability of Movement
A. Variable Force: Due to poor force scaling (dysmetria), a patient may over-correct a small sway with 
a massive, uncoordinated step, leading to a "propulsive" fall.
B. Delayed Reactions: The time it takes for the cerebellum to process a loss of balance and trigger a 
protective step is often increased, meaning they hit the floor before they can "catch" themselves.
2. High-Risk Functional Transitions
A. The "Plopping" Risk: In sit-to-stand and stand-to-sit, the lack of eccentric control can cause a 
patient to "drop" into the chair, risking spinal fractures or skin breakdown if they miss the seat.
B. Turning: Turning 180° is the most common point of failure; ataxic patients often "tangle" their feet or 
lose their base of support during the pivot.
3. Sensory Overload & Fatigue
A. Visual Dependency: Because these patients "walk with their eyes," they are at extreme risk in low-
light environments (e.g., getting up at night) or busy environments with moving crowds.
B. Neurological Fatigue: As seen in MS, as the patient tires, the "quality" of movement degrades 
rapidly, turning a safe walker into a high-fall risk by the end of a session.

## Page 31

Writing Goals for our patients (5, 7)
1. Prioritize Safety Over Independence
A. A patient who is "Independent" but demonstrates unpredictable lateral veering or poor eccentric control is a 
safety liability.
B. Shift the focus from whether they can do it to how safely they do it.
C. Example: Instead of "Pt. will walk 10m independently," use: "Pt. will walk 10m with zero loss of balance and 
consistent foot placement while using a weighted rollator".
2. Target the "Fail Points" (Transitions)
A. Falls rarely happen during steady-state walking; they happen during the "in-between" moments like turning, 
starting, or stopping.
B. Write goals for the high-risk moments identified in your assessment.
C. Example: "Patient will perform a controlled sit-to-stand transition without 'plopping' or requiring physical 
assistance in 5/5 consecutive trials".
3. Quantify the "Quality"
A. Use standardized scores to define what "safe" looks like.
B. Integrate the SARA or Timed Tests to track the dampening of ataxic symptoms .

## Page 32

Evidence Based Treatment Strategies (8)
1. Core & Proximal Stability (The "Stable Base")
•
Studies in cerebellar ataxia suggest that "distal" clumsiness is often exacerbated by "proximal" 
instability. If the trunk is swaying, the hand has no chance of hitting a target.
•
High-dosage core stability training (e.g., quadruped reaching, pelvic bridging with perturbations).
•
Create a "rigid cylinder" in the trunk so the cerebellum only has to coordinate the limb, not the 
entire body.

## Page 33

Evidence Based Treatment Strategies (9, 10)
2. Weighted Feedback (Proprioceptive Loading)
•
Adding weight to a limb or the trunk increases the proprioceptive input to the brain, essentially 
"turning up the volume" on where the limb is in space.
•
Practical Application:
•
Weighted Vests: Can reduce truncal sway and improve gait velocity.
•
Weighted Wrist/Ankle Straps: Helps dampen intention tremors during reaching or walking.
•
Weighted Utensils: Improves independence in ADLs like eating.
•
Note: According to Stephen et al. (2019), the weight helps the brain "see" the trunk better when the cerebellum is too noisy to do it 
alone. But remember the Zimmet (2018) rule: Weights on the moving limbs can actually make them more clumsy—keep your heavy 
loading for the trunk!'"

## Page 34

Evidence Based Treatment Strategies (11)
3. Compensatory & Environmental Strategies
•
In degenerative or severe cases, recovery of "normal" movement may not be the primary goal; 
Safety-first compensation is.
•
Practical Application:
• Rhythmic Auditory Stimulation (RAS): Using a metronome to provide a steady "beat" for 
walking, which helps bypass the faulty internal timing of the cerebellum.
• Visual Cues: Using floor markers to help with foot placement accuracy.
• Equipment: Weighted rollators (to dampen gait tremors) and "locking" wheelchairs during 
transitions.

## Page 35

Evidence Based Treatment Strategies (7)
4. Task Specific Training
•
"Practice makes Permanent”
•
The 3 Pillars of Task-Specific Training:
•
High Intensity & Dosage: The cerebellum 
requires thousands of repetitions to 
recalibrate. Training must be frequent and 
challenging.
•
Decreasing Constraints: Start with the 
stable base or static (e.g., reaching while 
sitting) and progress to unstable base or 
dynamic (reaching while standing or walking).
•
Real-World Context: Don't just do exercise, 
do the task. If they struggle with stairs, train 
the specific movement of stair climbing with 
weighted feedback.
The Scientific Evidence:
Reference: Ilg, W. et al. (2009) 'Intensive coordinative 
training improves motor performance in degenerative 
cerebellar disease', Neurology, 73(22), pp. 1823-1830.
The Study: 16 patients with degenerative ataxia underwent 
4 weeks of intensive coordinative training (including whole-
body tasks like balancing, reaching, and gait).
The Result: Significant improvement in SARA scores and 
gait speed. Crucially, the study showed that coordinative 
training was more effective than simple strength training for 
ataxic patients.
Key Finding: The brain can still demonstrate 
neuroplasticity in the cerebellum and secondary motor 
pathways (like the basal ganglia or cortex) to compensate for 
the "noisy" signals.
