## Slide 2

- WHAT IS NEUROPLASTICITY?
- Neuroplasticity, the brain’s capacity to reorganize itself by forming new neural connections, is central to
- modern neuroscience. Once believed to occur only during early development, research now shows that plasticity continues throughout the lifespan, supporting learning, memory, and recovery from injury or disease.

## Slide 3

- WHAT IS NEUROPLASTICITY?
- Definition: The ability of the central nervous system (CNS) to change its structural and functional organization in response to experience, learning, or injury
- Mechanisms:
- Synaptogenesis: Creating new connections between neurons to support new
- skills.
- Cortical Remapping: Areas of the brain "taking over" functions for damaged
- sections.
- Functional Compensation: Using different neuromotor processes to achieve the same action (Motor Equivalence).
- Key Principle: "Neurons that fire together, wire together"—repeated, goal-
- directed action drives these changes

## Slide 4

- A BIT MORE ON HOW IT HAPPENS (1)
- Synaptogenesis
- This is the process of forming new synapses between neurons. Think of it as building new roads to connect cities that previously had no direct path.
- Clinical Trigger: It is driven by High Intensity Practice and Task Specificity—the brain won't build a "road" it doesn't think you'll use.
- The Goal: To move from a temporary "Performance" change to a relatively permanent "Learning" state

## Slide 5

- A BIT MORE ON HOW IT HAPPENS (2)
- Cortical Remapping
- Also known as map expansion or reassignment. If a specific area of the brain is damaged, the surrounding healthy tissue can be "recruited" to take over its duties.
- How it works: Through the "Use it or Improve it" principle, the brain rededicates its neural real estate based on which parts are being stimulated by sensory and motor neurons.
- Risk: This is a double-edged sword; if a patient stops moving a limb ("Use it or Lose it"), the area of the brain once dedicated to that limb will be "colonized" by other functions.

## Slide 6

- A BIT MORE ON HOW IT HAPPENS (3)
- Functional Compensation
- This is less about fixing the "broken road" and more about finding a different route to the same destination. In your slides, this aligns perfectly with the concepts of Equivalence.
- Movement Equivalence: The patient changes the way they move (biomechanical  strategies or patterns) to accomplish the same task.
- Motor Equivalence: The patient changes the neuromotor processes (the brain areas or nerves utilized) to produce the same outcome.

- Maximizing the "Plastic" Brain
- Critical & Sensitive Periods: Early development represents a 	peak "window" where the CNS is highly adaptable to 	environmental and task demands.
- Post-Injury "Spontaneous" Recovery: In the early stages after 	neurological insult (like stroke or TBI), there is a biological prime 	time for recovery of function.

## Slide 8

- WINDOWS OF OPPORTUNITY FOR
- PHYSIOS (WHEN)
- Pediatric Focus:
- The first 3 years of life represent a peak for neuroplasticity and motor foundation.
- Significant improvements in gross motor function are typically observed up to age
- 6–7.
- Between ages 7 and 12, this is a prime time for children to acquire complex skills like
- swimming, cycling, or gymnastics
- Bone Density Loading: Puberty (approx. ages 10–14). This is a sensitive period for the 	skeletal system where impact-loading exercises have the greatest lifelong effect on 	bone mineral density.

## Slide 9

- WINDOWS OF OPPORTUNITY FOR
- PHYSIOS (WHEN)
- Adult Focus
- The brain's capacity to rewire itself peaks approximately 2 weeks after a stroke.
- Most "spontaneous" recovery occurs within the first 3 to 6 months, making early,
- intensive physiotherapy most effective.
- Stroke
- The Hyper-Acute Window (0–48 hours): The brain is in "survival mode." Therapy focuses on preventing further damage and basic investigatory focus.
- The Acute Window (2 days to 2 weeks): This is the peak of Post-Stroke Plasticity. Levels of growth-promoting genes are at their highest. This is the "window of opportunity" where high-intensity practice can drive the most significant cortical remapping.
- The Sub-Acute Window (2 weeks to 3 months): Most "spontaneous" recovery occurs here. After 3 to 6 months, the "window" doesn't close, but the brain enters a "chronic" state where changes require significantly more repetitions (e.g., the 300+ reps mentioned in your slides) to achieve the same result.

## Slide 11

- WINDOWS OF OPPORTUNITY FOR
- PHYSIOS (HOW)
- The Therapist's Role…
- High Intensity: Plasticity requires high repetition—e.g., 300 reps for
- upper limbs or 2,000 steps for the lower limb.
- Challenge: The task must be difficult enough to require new neural
- strategies, often involving dual-tasking.
- Salience: Activities must be meaningful (e.g., reaching for food) to 	trigger the cognitive processes essential for motor control.
- Use it or Lose it: If the "window" is missed or the limb is not used, the 	brain area may be taken over by other functions, leading to "learned 	non-use".
