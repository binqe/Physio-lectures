## Slide 3

- Interpreting tissue sections
- Nuclei: collections of neurons more densely packed together, with similar functions and projections
- Tracts: bundles of neuronal projections, often ascending or descending the CNS and connecting different levels
- Commissure: bundles of neuronal projections connecting areas between brain hemispheres
- Association fibres: bundles of neuronal projections connecting areas within the same hemisphere
- Projecting fibres: bundles of fibers connecting the cortex to brainstem, spinal cord, and subcortical nuclei

## Slide 17

- Basal ganglia: Input/output
- Cortex afferent (layer V) are predominantly excitatory (glutamate)
- Primary motor, premotor, supplementary motor and somatosensory cortices project mainly to the putamen
- Prefrontal cortex and many other associative areas project to the caudate
- The information processed by the basal ganglia returns to the cortex (mainly supplementary motor area, but also somatosensory and motor) through the thalamus (ventroanterior and ventrolateral nuclei)

## Slide 20

- Corticostriato-pallido-thalamocortical loop
- Direct pathway  Promotion of movement
- Stimulation of the striatum by the cortex leads to disinhibition of the thalamus
- Striatum inhibits GPi/SNr  thalamic activation
- Indirect pathway  suppression of movement
- Stimulation of the striatum by the cortex inhibits the GPe
- GPe no longer inhibits the STN which can stimulate GPi/SNr  thalamic inhibition
- STN also receives direct projection from the cortex
- Hyperdirect pathway

## Slide 24

- Neuromodulation within the basal ganglia: dopamine
- Striatal neurons can be divided into two groups, according to their dopamine receptors:
- D1 neurons - express D1 type dopamine receptors
- D2 neurons - express D2 type dopamine receptors
- D1 neurons mostly belong to the direct pathway
- Release of dopamine supports strong phasic inputs from D1 neurons  Facilitated direct pathway
- D1 type dopamine receptors are activating GPCRs (Gs)
- D2 neurons mostly belong to the indirect pathway
- Release of dopamine suppresses the firing of D2 neurons  Reduced impact from the indirect pathway
- D2 type dopamine receptors are inhibiting GPCRs (Gi)

## Slide 27

- Lesions of the basal ganglia
- Lesions of the caudate
- Rarely motor disturbances, mostly behavioural
- Abulia - apathy with loss of initiative and of spontaneous thought and emotional responses
- Lesions of putamen/globus pallidus
- Dystonia - muscle contraction and spasms
- Athetosis - writhing movements of hand, arm and neck
- Lesion of the subthalamic nucleus
- Hemiballismus - Flailing movement of a limb

- Parkinson’s disease
- Motor
- Muscle rigidity
- Involuntary tremor, moving or resting, at 3-6 cycles/sec
- Akinesia - difficulty in movement initiation
- Bradykinesia: Slowness in executing movements

- Speech disorder

- REM sleep disorder
- Autonomic dysfunction

## Slide 31

- Parkinson’s disease: unclear etiology
- Mostly late onset with sporadic cases
- Genetics
- 10% of cases are familiar (mutations in PARK1-10 genes)
- PARK-1 gene for a-synuclein  higher in PD
- LRRK2 gene
- Environmental
- Chemicals like MPTP (1-methyl-4phenyl-1,2,5,6,-
- tetrahydropyridine) can induce PD
- Agrochemicals (pesticides)?
- Gut microbiome? (a-synuclein aggregates found in the gut)

## Slide 32

- Parkinson’s disease: mechanism
- Destruction of SN dopaminergic neurons will remove inhibition on the indirect pathway
- Direct pathway becomes underactive
- Indirect pathway becomes overactive
- Some feedback circuits based on acetylcholine could be the cause of the oscillations which cause the tremor.
- Akinesia might also be connected to the lack of mental drive due to decrease dopamine secretion in the accumbens (another basal ganglia nucleus mostly related to emotions and reward).

## Slide 34

- Treatment
- L-DOPA
- Converted in the brain into dopamine (dopamine itself doesn’t pass the BBB)
- Ameliorates many symptoms, especially rigidity and akinesia.
- Chronic L-DOPA can lead to on-off effects (motor fluctuations) or levodopa-induced dyskinesia (excessive movement)
- Amantadine
- Partial dopamine agonist
- Decrease dopamine reuptake
- Antiglutammatergic and anticholinergic properties
- L-DOPA and Amantadine often given together

## Slide 35

- Treatment
- DOPA decarboxylase inhibitors  Increasing the levels of DOPA
- Carbidopa or benserazide
- MAOs and COMT inhibitors
- Reduced dopamine degradation
- Reduced degradation of DOPA (COMT inhibitors)
- Reduces the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the SN (unexplained)
- Anticholinergic drugs (benzhexol, benzatropine)
- Loss of DA dis-inhibits striatal cholinergic interneurons
- Inoculation of fetal ESC or iPSC-derived dopaminergic neurons (clinical trials)
- Physical activity and physiotherapy

## Slide 36

- Treatment
- Deep brain stimulation of STN or GPi
- Patients nonresponsive to drugs
- Reduces rigidity, tremors and bradykinesia
- N Engl J Med 2012;367:1529-38

## Slide 37

- Failings in current therapies
- Not all symptoms addressed
- Postural balance unaffected
- Side effects can be severe
- L-DOPA-induced dyskinesia
- Amantadine can give nausea, psychotic episodes, restless legs, convulsion
- Do not address the degeneration of neurons
- Urgent need of neuroprotective approaches
- Lots of research going on!
- Lancet Neurology 2021 Jul;20(7):559-572

## Slide 38

- Huntington’s Disease (Chorea)
- Onset 30-40 years - inherited
- Flicking movements that worsen into severe distortional movements of the entire body
- Followed by severe dementia
- Loss of GABAergic neurons in caudate and putamen
- Early stages only indirect pathway D2 striatal neurons
- Loss of inhibition on SN and GP causing outbursts
- Later stages interest many other areas of the brain, including the cortex
- Mutation in the gene of huntingtin causes multiple codon repeats (CAG) of the amino acid glutamine  poly-glutamine disease
- Over 35 CAG repeats
- Degradation causes macromolecular aggregates
- Various mechanisms of neurotoxicity involved
