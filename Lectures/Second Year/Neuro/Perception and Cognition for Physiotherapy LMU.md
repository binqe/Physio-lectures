## Slide 10

- Hierarchy of cognition

**Notes:** The hierarchy of cognition describes how complex cognitive abilities are built upon simpler ones, often visualized as a pyramid with foundational skills at the bottom and higher-order skills at the top. Foundational elements like processing speed, attention, and sensory input are at the base, which support more complex functions such as working memory, executive function, and language. This structure explains how weaknesses at a foundational level can impact higher-level thinking and problem-solving.

## Slide 12

- Sensory registration
- Information reaches the nervous system from the sense organs: sight, hearing, touch, smell, taste (proprioception)
- External physical stimuli are converted to a form that the nervous system can use – coding/registration
- Vision is the dominant sense in cognition
- Visual assessment:
- Visual acuity (normally wear glasses)
- Tracking and scanning
- Visual fields: confrontation test

**Notes:** The initial stage of cognitive processing where the brain detects and records sensory input (visual, auditory, tactile, proprioceptive, vestibular, etc.) from the environment and body.
It occurs automatically and rapidly, often below conscious awareness.
Sensory information is held briefly (milliseconds to seconds) in sensory memory before being filtered for relevance.
Purpose: To provide raw data for further processing (attention and perception).
🔹 Example:When a physiotherapist touches a patient’s arm, the skin and proprioceptors detect the stimulus — this sensory input must be registered before the patient can attend to it or move purposefully.

## Slide 14

- Attention
- Attention is a basic part of processing- prerequisites include alertness and arousal
- Focuses cognitive resources on selected stimuli from sensory registration.
- Attention is affected by internal (fatigue, thirst) and external influences (temperature), individual differences and by task novelty (new tasks increase attentional demands)

**Notes:** Focuses cognitive resources on selected stimuli from sensory registration.
Filters out irrelevant information (e.g., background noise).
Foundation for all conscious learning and motor control.🔹 Clinical relevance: A patient with impaired attention may not engage safely in balance or gait tasks.

## Slide 17

- Visual and spatial perception
- Complex mechanism in which sensory information is given meaning through association with our stored knowledge
- Divided into visual perception and visuospatial perception
- Visuoperceptive skills include colour perception, depth perception and figure ground perception (What things are)
- Visuospatial perception skills includes the ability to appreciate the spatial relationships between objects and landmarks (Where things are)

**Notes:** The brain interprets sensory input into meaningful information (“what” and “where”).
Integrates multiple sensory modalities.🔹 Example: Recognising an object as a cup or understanding spatial orientation for reaching.

- retrieval (recall or recognition: accessing stored information)

## Slide 27

- Praxis
- Praxis refers to the ability to use our hands and body in skilled/previously learned tasks in order to function
- Praxis involves three component systems (which can be selectively impaired with neurological damage)

**Notes:** Praxis is the ability to plan, organise, and carry out purposeful, skilled movement — even when there is no weakness, sensory loss, or incoordination.It’s what allows someone to use tools, perform gestures, and sequence complex motor actions automatically (like making tea or brushing teeth).
👉 In short: Praxis = motor planning — knowing what to do, how to do it, and in what order.

- Neglect/Inattention
- A body scheme disorder

## Slide 37

- Inability to find your way in space.
- Multiple visual discrimination skills required; distance of objects, colour, shape, form.
- Patients may not be able to navigate within familiar environments or on new routes that they have practiced multiple times.

## Slide 44

- The ACE cognitive assessment refers to the Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination (ACE-III), a 15-minute test used to screen for cognitive impairment, particularly dementia. It evaluates five cognitive domains: attention, memory, verbal fluency, language, and visuospatial abilities, with a higher score (out of 100) indicating better cognitive function

## Slide 48

- Cognitive and visual perceptual rehabilitation
- 4 principles:
- Education; ‘drip feed’ approach; lots of repetition.
- Process/impairment based; targeting specific impairments with exercises to overcome the problem.
- Strategy/ compensatory; training to compensate for the problem.
- Function; practice the skills learned in their everyday life.

## Slide 58

## Slide 59

- Labelling
- Hand patient objects required

- Hand over hand facilitation
- Use same objects, e.g. same ball, same frame
