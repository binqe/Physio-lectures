## Slide 4

- MS
- Multiple sclerosis is a chronic autoimmune disease affecting the central nervous system and is characterized by inflammation, demyelination, gliosis, and neuronal loss.
- Varied disease courses, including:
- Relapsing-remitting, primary progressive, and secondary progressive

## Slide 5

- MS Aetiology

**Notes:** The exact cause of multiple sclerosis (MS) is unknown, but it is believed to result from a combination of genetic predisposition and environmental factors, including a possible link to certain viral infections like the Epstein-Barr virus. Other risk factors include smoking, low vitamin D levels, and a family history of the disease. MS is an autoimmune disease where the immune system mistakenly attacks the myelin sheath that protects nerve fibers in the brain and spinal cord.

## Slide 6

- MS  Epidemiology
- The average age for an MS diagnosis is around 30 years old, with most people being diagnosed between the ages of 20 and 40.
- 2.9 million people in the world have multiple sclerosis. The UK has more than 150,000 people living with MS.
- MS is two to three times more common in women than it is in men
- The distribution of MS around the world is uneven. Generally, the prevalence increases as you travel further north or south from the equator. Countries that lie on the equator tend to have extremely low levels of MS, whilst places closer to the Poles such as New Zealand, Canada and Scotland have particularly high rates.

**Notes:** Multiple Sclerosis (MS) is more common further from the equator primarily because of lower levels of vitamin D, which is synthesized in the skin from sunlight exposure. People who live closer to the equator receive more year-round sunlight, which leads to higher vitamin D levels. Research suggests that vitamin D plays a role in immune system regulation, and low vitamin D is considered a risk factor for developing MS

## Slide 8

- Pathophysiology
- • Immune-mediated attack on myelin → inflammation and demyelination.
- • T-cells and B-cells cross the blood–brain barrier, triggering immune response.
- • Oligodendrocyte damage → impaired nerve conduction.
- • Chronic neurodegeneration and axonal loss → disrupted communication in CNS.

**Notes:** Gliosis – healing, causes scars and does not help in regeneration
Complement system: The complement system, a part of the immune response, gets activated in MS and marks synapses for elimination. Specifically, complement component C3 localizes to synapses targeted for engulfment.

MS affects both white and grey matter, though it was historically considered a disease of the white matter. The immune system attacks the myelin sheath in white matter, but also damages nerve cell bodies and axons in grey matter, leading to both physical and cognitive symptoms.

## Slide 9

- Types and Progression
- Relapsing–Remitting (RRMS): Most common (≈85%), episodes of symptoms with recovery.
- Secondary Progressive (SPMS): Initially RRMS, then gradual worsening.
- Primary Progressive (PPMS): Gradual decline from onset.
- Clinically isolated syndrome (CIS): Single episode of neurological symptoms that are similar to those of MS. Can have future attack – diagnosed with RRMS

- Motor Neurone Disease

## Slide 19

- MND
- MND is a  progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting motor neurons.
- Causes muscle weakness, atrophy, and loss of voluntary movement.
- Sensory and autonomic systems usually preserved.
- Main types:
- 1. Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)
- 2. Progressive Bulbar Palsy (PBP)
- 3. Progressive Muscular Atrophy (PMA)
- 4. Primary Lateral Sclerosis (PLS)

**Notes:** amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), degeneration of both the upper and lower motor neurones occurs, leading to paralysis of most voluntary muscles in the body (especially limbs).
When only the muscles involved in speech and swallowing are affected, this is known as progressive bulbar palsy, causing slurred speech and difficulty swallowing
In less common forms of MND, there is a more selective degeneration of either the upper motor neurones (primary lateral sclerosis; PLS) or lower motor neurones (such as progressive muscular atrophy; PMA)

## Slide 20

- MND Aetiology and Epidemiology
- The cause of MND is still not fully understood.
- The majority of cases are thought to be caused by a number of contributory factors, including subtle genetic, environmental and lifestyle influences. It is important to note that these factors must all coincide to trigger the disease.
- More common in men and typically onset occurs between ages 55 and 75.
- Female to Male ratio:  1.5:1 to 2:1.
- In approximately 5-10% of cases, MND is directly caused by a genetic mistake – this is inherited.
- A number of genes have been identified that can cause inherited MND, including SOD1, TDP-43, FUS, UNC13A, C9ORF72, MATR3, NEK1 and ANXA11.

## Slide 22

- Pathophysiology
- Degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons.
- • Loss of motor neuron input → muscle denervation and atrophy.
- • Mechanisms: glutamate excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, protein misfolding (TDP-43, SOD1). Axonal transport defects.

**Notes:** Mechanisms involved:
Glutamate excitotoxicity → neuronal overactivation and cell death
Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction → energy failure in motor neurons
Protein misfolding and aggregation (e.g., TDP-43, SOD1) → toxic cellular accumulation
Axonal transport defects → impaired nutrient and signal transmission
Neuroinflammation → microglial activation and further neuronal injury
Result: irreversible motor neuron loss → progressive weakness, fasciculations, and paralysis

## Slide 23

- Disease Progression
- Onset is focal (e.g. limb or bulbar) and spreads regionally.
- Progressive weakness → respiratory involvement → death (2–5 years average).
- Up to half of the people living with MND may also experience difficulties with learning, language and concentration.  30–50% show frontotemporal cognitive involvement – Diagnosed with frontotemporal dementia (FTD)
- Stages:
- 1. Focal weakness
- 2. Functional limitation
- 3. Widespread weakness
- 4. Loss of independence
- 5. Respiratory failure

## Slide 24

- Clinical Features
- Upper Motor Neuron Signs:
- Spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski sign (Corticospinal tract degeneration)
- Lower Motor Neuron Signs:
- Muscle wasting, fasciculations, weakness, hyporeflexia. (Anterior horn cell loss)
- Bulbar signs:
- Dysarthria, dysphagia, pseudobulbar affect (Brainstem motor nuclei involvement)

**Notes:** Fasciculations: These twitches are a result of the lower motor neurons not receiving signals from the upper motor neurons - when a single peripheral nerve that controls a muscle is overactive, resulting in involuntary muscle movement. motor neurons become electrically unstable and fire spontaneously, causing small groups of muscle fibers to contract involuntarily. This happens due to the damage and degeneration of motor neurons, which disrupts the normal signals sent to muscles, eventually leading to a loss of muscle control and weakness. 

Pseudobulbar affect (PBA) is a neurological condition that causes outbursts of uncontrolled or inappropriate laughing or crying. These episodes don't match your internal emotional state. PBA develops as the result of a brain injury or underlying neurological condition, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).

## Slide 38

- “On” and “Off”

**Notes:** Parkinson’s is caused by loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra, leading to disrupted control of movement.
Levodopa (and other dopaminergic drugs) temporarily restore dopamine levels, improving movement (“ON” phase).
Why ON/OFF Fluctuations Occur:
Disease progression: fewer surviving neurons to store and release dopamine → brain relies directly on medication levels.
Levodopa’s short half-life (~90 min): blood levels rise and fall rapidly → fluctuating dopamine availability.
Reduced buffering capacity: loss of neuronal terminals means the brain can’t smooth out dopamine peaks/troughs.
Receptor and enzyme changes: long-term levodopa use alters dopamine receptor sensitivity and metabolism.
Dietary or absorption factors: protein-rich meals and delayed gastric emptying can reduce levodopa absorption → sudden “OFF.”
Clinical result:
“ON” period: medication working → smooth, mobile movement.
“OFF” period: drug effect wears off → tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia return suddenly.
Physiotherapy relevance:
Schedule therapy sessions during predictable ON phases for best mobility and participation.
Educate patients to track fluctuations and coordinate with the MDT for medication review.

**Notes:** LSVT BIG (Lee Silverman Voice Treatment – BIG) is an evidence-based physiotherapy programme designed to improve movement amplitude (size) in people with Parkinson’s disease.
Core Principle:Parkinson’s causes patients to perceive their movements as normal, even when they are small or slow.LSVT BIG retrains the brain to recognise and produce bigger, more normal movements.
Key Features:
Focus on large, exaggerated movements (“Think BIG!”)
High intensity and repetition (4 sessions/week for 4 weeks)
Task-specific practice for daily activities (walking, reaching, turning)
Combines motor learning, cueing, and amplitude calibration
Benefits:
Improves speed, balance, and gait
Enhances functional mobility and confidence
Can be integrated into home exercise and community programmes
Physiotherapy Role:
Deliver structured sessions, reinforce home practice, and adapt exercises to functional goals.

- Cerebellar Ataxia

## Slide 46

- Overview of Cerebellar Ataxia
- • Ataxia = incoordination of movement due to cerebellar dysfunction.
- • Affects timing, accuracy, and control of movement.
- • Types: congenital, acquired, degenerative.
- • Common causes: stroke, multiple sclerosis, toxins, hereditary ataxias.

**Notes:** • Cerebellum has three lobes: anterior, posterior, flocculonodular.
• Responsible for coordination, balance, and error correction.
• Input: spinal cord, vestibular system, cerebral cortex.
• Output: thalamus and motor cortex for movement control.

Normal Function of the Cerebellum:
Integrates sensory input (proprioception, vestibular, visual) with motor output.
Ensures smooth, coordinated, and precise movement.
Regulates muscle tone, posture, and balance.

## Slide 47

- CA Aetiology and Epidemiology
- Cerebellar ataxia has diverse aetiologies, including genetic causes like spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) and Friedreich's ataxia, and acquired causes such as autoimmune disorders, toxins, trauma, stroke, and infections
- Epidemiological data shows varying prevalence rates depending on the aetiology:
- Prevalence of late-onset cerebellar ataxia (LOCA) to be 8.4 per 100,000 for sporadic idiopathic cases and 1.8 per 100,000 for inherited forms.

## Slide 48

- Pathophysiology
- • Damage to Purkinje cells and cerebellar pathways disrupts timing of muscle activation.
- • Leads to dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, intention tremor.
- • Degenerative ataxias show neuronal loss, gliosis, and synaptic dysfunction.
- • Genetic forms involve protein aggregation (e.g., spinocerebellar ataxia).

**Notes:** Damage to cerebellar neurons (especially Purkinje cells) →these cells normally inhibit over-activity and fine-tune signals to the rest of the brain.When they die or stop functioning, movement signals become uncontrolled and poorly timed.
Communication breakdown between the cerebellum and the motor cortex or brainstem →the body can’t properly correct movement errors.
Loss of sensory integration →without good proprioceptive or vestibular feedback, the brain can’t sense where the limbs are in space.
Result:Movements become inaccurate, jerky, and unsteady — this is ataxia.


protein aggregation” means
Normally, cells make proteins that fold into specific shapes so they can function properly.
In many genetic ataxias, mutations cause abnormal or misfolded proteins to be produced.
These misfolded proteins clump together (aggregate) inside neurons, particularly Purkinje cells and deep cerebellar nuclei.
Over time, these protein aggregates are toxic — they disrupt normal cell function, energy metabolism, and eventually lead to cell death.

## Slide 59

- What Are Hyperkinetic Disorders?
- Group of movement disorders characterised by excessive, involuntary movements
- Result from basal ganglia dysfunction and altered neurotransmitter regulation (mainly dopamine, GABA, glutamate)
- Examples: Huntington’s disease, Dystonia, Chorea, Tremor, Tics/Tourette’s syndrome

**Notes:** Opposite of hypokinetic disorders (e.g., Parkinson’s disease)

## Slide 60

- Basal Ganglia Pathophysiology
- Normal function: modulates motor cortex via direct (excitatory) and indirect (inhibitory) pathways
- Hyperkinetic Disorders: overactivity of direct or underactivity of indirect pathway
- Leads to reduced inhibition of thalamocortical neurons → excessive movement
- Often involves dopaminergic overactivity

A feeling of tension builds up in the affected part of your body, and giving in to the tic provides momentary relief. ## Slide 87

- Pathophysiology: Ischaemic Stroke
- Arterial occlusion → ↓ oxygen and glucose supply
- Energy failure → neuronal depolarisation and excitotoxicity (glutamate release)
- Calcium influx → cell death and oxidative stress
- Ischaemic core (irreversible) and penumbra (salvageable with reperfusion)

**Notes:** Emphasise the concept of the penumbra and the goal of early reperfusion therapy.

The pathophysiology of an ischemic stroke begins with a sudden loss of blood flow to a part of the brain, leading to oxygen and nutrient deprivation. This energy failure causes an immediate cascade of events, including ionic imbalances, excitotoxicity from glutamate buildup, and a rapid influx of calcium into neurons. This results in oxidative stress, inflammation, and ultimately, cell death in the central "core" region of the brain. The surrounding area of partially damaged tissue, known as the "penumbra," is at risk and can be salvaged by restoring blood flow.

## Slide 88

- Pathophysiology: Haemorrhagic Stroke
- Rupture of blood vessel causing bleeding into brain tissue or subarachnoid space
- Causes: hypertension, aneurysm, AV malformation, anticoagulation
- Mass effect and raised ICP lead to further injury
- Blood breakdown products cause inflammation and toxicity
- Often more severe acute presentation

**Notes:** Hemorrhagic stroke pathophysiology involves a blood vessel rupturing and bleeding into the brain, causing two primary effects: damage from the blood itself and the pressure it exerts. The leaked blood can form a hematoma, compressing and damaging surrounding brain tissue. It also prevents oxygen and nutrients from reaching the affected areas, leading to the death of brain cells and a loss of function in the part of the body controlled by that brain region
