KEY QUESTIONS + CLINICAL REASONING
"Can you describe when you first noticed these symptoms, and whether they have been getting gradually worse, staying the same, or fluctuating?"
Establishes the timeline and trajectory. Slow progressive onset over months to years points toward a degenerative or hereditary cause (e.g.,
spinocerebellar ataxia), while a sudden onset would raise concern for a vascular event, demyelination, or toxic cause. Trajectory directly shapes my
urgency of referral and long-term prognosis discussion.
"Does anyone in your family have similar symptoms — unsteady walking, a tremor, or a diagnosed neurological condition?"
Hereditary spinocerebellar ataxias (SCA types 1, 2, 3, 6) are autosomal dominant — a positive family history dramatically increases this probability and
would trigger a genetics referral. Even a negative history is informative as it raises the likelihood of acquired causes.
"Have you had any changes to your vision, any difficulty swallowing, or noticed changes in your memory or concentration?"
Brainstem connections to the cerebellum can produce nystagmus, diplopia, and dysphagia — their presence widens the diagnostic picture toward
posterior fossa pathology or MSA. Cognitive change may indicate a more diffuse neurodegenerative process. Dysphagia is an immediate safety concern:
aspiration risk must be communicated to the MDT.
"Do you drink alcohol regularly, or have you been on any long-term medications such as anti-epileptics like phenytoin?"
Chronic alcohol use causes dose-dependent Purkinje cell loss, producing a predominantly gait ataxia picture. Phenytoin toxicity is a reversible cause of
cerebellar signs. Identifying a toxic cause is clinically actionable — removal of the agent can halt or partially reverse the condition.
"Have you had any falls? If so, how often, and do you tend to fall in a particular direction?"
Falls are my immediate safety priority as a physiotherapist. Cerebellar ataxia causes multidirectional falls due to truncal instability. Fall frequency informs
urgency of gait aid prescription and home safety review. I also use this to baseline the Falls Efficacy Scale.
"How is all of this affecting your daily life — things like getting dressed, eating meals, getting around, and your social activities?"
This patient-centred question directly links physical impairments to activity limitations and participation restrictions. It reveals what matters most to Anika
and allows me to build a goal-directed rehabilitation plan. It also opens a conversation about psychosocial impact that I can use to screen for low mood
and reduced quality of life.

Pathophysiology of Working Diagnosis
WHAT IS HAPPENING NEUROLOGICALLY
The cerebellum functions as the brain's real-time error-correction system — it continuously compares the intended motor command (efference
copy from motor cortex) with actual movement feedback from vestibular receptors, proprioceptors, and visual input, then sends corrective output
via the deep cerebellar nuclei (primarily the dentate nucleus) through the thalamus to the motor cortex. The key output neurons are
PURKINJE CELLS
, which provide the cerebellum's sole inhibitory output to the deep nuclei. In cerebellar ataxia, Purkinje cells are progressively damaged or lost —
without their inhibitory influence, motor output from the deep nuclei becomes disorganised, irregular in timing, and inaccurate in force scaling. The
result is the signature triad of
DYSMETRIA
(inability to accurately gauge the distance and force of a movement),
DYSDIADOCHOKINESIA
(loss of rhythm in rapid alternating movements), and
INTENTION TREMOR
(oscillation that worsens as the limb approaches its target, reflecting the absence of corrective dampening). The cerebellar vermis and
spinocerebellar tracts govern axial and gait coordination — degeneration here produces the wide-based, lurching "drunken" gait. Cerebellar
connections to the brainstem also coordinate articulatory muscles, so Purkinje cell loss produces the characteristic scanning dysarthria heard in
Anika's speech.
WHY THIS PATIENT FITS THE DIAGNOSIS
Wide-based "drunken" gait
— truncal ataxia from vermis and spinocerebellar tract degeneration; wide stance compensates for postural instability
Positive finger-to-nose dysmetria
— cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction causing limb incoordination and inability to dampen terminal trajectory
Dysdiadochokinesia
— loss of precise timing and rhythm in rapid alternating movements; a hallmark of cerebellar pathology that cannot be explained by weakness or
sensory loss
Slurred/scanning speech
— cerebellar dysarthria from disruption of cerebellar-brainstem circuits controlling laryngeal and articulatory muscles
Preserved bilateral strength
— rules out corticospinal (UMN) or peripheral (LMN) damage as the primary driver; the deficit is purely one of motor coordination
Slow progressive onset
— consistent with degenerative Purkinje cell loss rather than acute vascular event or inflammatory demyelination

Differential Diagnoses
✦ Primary Diagnosis: Hereditary Spinocerebellar Ataxia (SCA)
Supporting features: Slow progressive bilateral cerebellar syndrome in a middle-aged adult; dysarthria; fine motor and gait involvement; intact
strength. SCA types 1, 2, 3 (Machado-Joseph Disease), and 6 are the most prevalent autosomal dominant hereditary ataxias. Genetic testing using
trinucleotide repeat panel analysis is the gold standard confirmatory test. Family history, if positive, would make this near-certain.
Differential 1: Alcoholic / Toxic Cerebellar Degeneration
Supporting features: Progressive bilateral cerebellar syndrome in an adult. However, alcoholic ataxia preferentially affects the anterior and superior
vermis, causing gait ataxia with relatively preserved upper limb coordination. The prominent upper limb dysmetria and dysdiadochokinesia in Anika's
case implicates cerebellar hemisphere involvement, making a purely toxic cause less consistent — though alcohol history must be explicitly
excluded.
Differential 2: Multiple System Atrophy — Cerebellar Type (MSA-C)
Supporting features: Progressive adult-onset cerebellar ataxia with dysarthria. MSA-C would be supported by additional features: autonomic
dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, bladder incontinence), early postural instability, and Parkinsonian features. The absence of these features in
Anika's presentation makes MSA-C less probable, though it remains in the differential given the unknown aetiology.
WHY I FAVOUR HEREDITARY SCA
I favour hereditary SCA over toxic degeneration because the upper limb incoordination (dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia) and fine motor impairment
are prominent — this pattern reflects cerebellar hemisphere involvement beyond the vermis-predominant picture of alcoholic ataxia. I favour SCA
over MSA-C because there is no description of autonomic dysfunction or Parkinsonism, and the age of onset is younger than the typical MSA-C
presentation (usually 50s+). Genetic panel testing in conjunction with neurology review would confirm the diagnosis.

Objective Assessment — Special Tests
SPECIAL TESTS
Test 1 — Finger-to-Nose Test (FNT)
MEASURES
 Limb dysmetria and intention tremor — integrity of cerebellar hemisphere coordination and sensorimotor calibration
HOW TO PERFORM
1. Seat the patient comfortably. Position yourself opposite, holding your index finger at arm's length from the patient.
2. Ask the patient to touch the tip of their own nose with their index finger, then touch your finger tip, alternating repeatedly at a moderate pace.
3. Reposition your finger to different locations in space between each reach to vary the movement direction and distance.
4. Observe for: overshoot or undershoot of the target (dysmetria), an oscillating tremor that increases as the finger approaches the target
(intention tremor), and irregularity of movement velocity.
5. Test both arms and note any asymmetry.
POSITIVE FINDING
 Consistent overshooting or undershooting and/or worsening tremor approaching the target = dysmetria and intention tremor
indicating ipsilateral cerebellar hemisphere dysfunction.
WHY I CHOSE IT
 Anika's complaint of difficulty using a fork and buttons strongly suggested dysmetria. This is the gold-standard bedside test for
cerebellar limb coordination — it directly maps to her functional complaint, is reproducible, and can be re-tested at each session to track change.
Test 2 — Heel-to-Shin Test (HTS)
MEASURES
 Lower limb cerebellar coordination — tests cerebellar hemisphere and spinocerebellar tract function in the legs
HOW TO PERFORM
1. Ask the patient to lie supine on the plinth.
2. Instruct them to place the heel of one foot precisely on the opposite knee.
3. Ask them to slide the heel smoothly and accurately down the front of the shin toward the ankle.
4. Observe for: deviation of the heel off the shin laterally (ataxia), jerky/irregular speed, or inability to maintain contact with the shin throughout the
movement.
5. Repeat on both sides and compare symmetry.
POSITIVE FINDING
 Heel deviates off the shin, moves in a jerky or wavering manner, or overshoots the knee = lower limb cerebellar ataxia.
WHY I CHOSE IT
 Anika's gait presentation implies lower limb incoordination. This test lets me determine whether her ataxic gait is driven by
cerebellar lower limb dysfunction specifically — informing whether gait retraining should primarily target coordination, balance, or both.
Test 3 — Romberg Test (Eyes Open vs. Closed)
MEASURES
 Distinguishes cerebellar ataxia (unstable with eyes OPEN) from sensory/dorsal column ataxia (unstable only with eyes CLOSED) —
identifies which sensory modality is most contributing to instability
HOW TO PERFORM
1. Ask the patient to stand with feet together, arms at sides or folded across chest.
2. Stand directly beside the patient, close enough to catch them — never leave them unsupported.
3. Observe for 30 seconds with eyes OPEN — document any sway, stepping, or loss of balance.
4. Ask the patient to close their eyes — observe for a further 30 seconds.
5. A positive Romberg = marked increase in sway or loss of balance upon eye closure (indicating sensory ataxia). Instability even with eyes open
= cerebellar ataxia.
POSITIVE FINDING (Cerebellar)
 Unsteady even with eyes open — cerebellar ataxia is NOT correctable by visual input, unlike dorsal column
sensory ataxia. A purely positive Romberg (only on eye closure) would shift suspicion to proprioceptive/dorsal column pathology.
WHY I CHOSE IT
 This test directly guides my treatment strategy. If Anika can partially compensate with visual input (semi-negative Romberg), I
can incorporate visual biofeedback strategies in balance training. If she is unstable even with eyes open, my approach must focus on central
coordination retraining rather than sensory substitution.
KEY NEUROLOGICAL OBSERVATIONS
Tone:
Typically hypotonic in cerebellar pathology — assess passively at the wrist and knee; muscles may feel "floppy" or pendular on passive movement
Coordination:
Observe dysdiadochokinesia via rapid forearm pronation/supination — loss of rhythm and regularity with increasing speed confirms cerebellar
involvement
Gait:
Wide-based, irregular stride length, truncal sway, tendency to veer; I also assess tandem (heel-to-toe) walking and turning — both are particularly
impaired in cerebellar ataxia
Posture:
Assess sitting balance on plinth versus standing — truncal instability worsens as base of support narrows; also look for titubation (rhythmic
head/trunk tremor at rest)
Oculomotor:
Look for nystagmus, particularly gaze-evoked horizontal nystagmus — present in cerebellar or vestibulo-cerebellar dysfunction; relevant for driving
and falls risk
Speech:
Dysarthria quality — cerebellar dysarthria is scanning/staccato; consistent with what is described in Anika's presentation
OUTCOME MEASURE
PRIMARY: SCALE FOR THE ASSESSMENT AND RATING OF ATAXIA (SARA)
I would use the SARA — a validated, disease-specific 8-item scale covering gait, stance, sitting balance, speech, finger chase, finger-nose-finger,
fast alternating hand movements, and heel-shin slide. Scored 0–40 (higher = more severe ataxia). It has established reliability and
responsiveness to change in degenerative ataxias, making it ideal for tracking Anika's rehabilitation progress over time.
SECONDARY: BERG BALANCE SCALE (BBS)
— provides a quantified falls risk score (≤ 45/56 = elevated risk) that is widely understood by the MDT and directly relevant to Anika's primary
safety concern.

Treatment Plan
INITIAL PHASE — WEEKS 1–4 (2–3 SESSIONS/WEEK)
NEUROPHYSIO RATIONALE
Cerebellar rehabilitation leverages
ERROR-BASED MOTOR LEARNING
and
NEURAL PLASTICITY
. The cerebellum is fundamentally a learning structure — it modifies synaptic weights in response to motor errors. Even in the context of Purkinje
cell loss, the remaining cerebellar circuits and compensatory cortical pathways can adapt through high-repetition, task-specific, progressively
challenging practice. I therefore prioritise active coordination training over passive techniques, always pitching the difficulty at the "just right"
challenge level where errors occur but task completion remains possible — this is the zone of optimal learning.
Exercise 1 — Seated Target Reaching (Upper Limb Coordination)
What: Patient seated at a table. Multiple coloured stickers or objects placed at varying distances and heights in front of them. Patient touches each
target with their index finger in a specified sequence, returning to their nose between each reach — directly mirroring the FNT but as a functional
training task.
Why: This task provides the brain with continuous error signals (over- or undershooting of targets) that the remaining cerebellar circuits and
compensatory cortical areas use to recalibrate motor output. High repetition drives use-dependent plasticity. Varying target positions prevents
habituation and forces adaptive recalibration.
Dosage: 3 sets × 20 repetitions per arm, 90-second rest between sets.
⬇ Regression
Large, close targets (within arm's length). Forearm resting on table to
reduce proximal demand. Single direction (forward reach only). Slow,
deliberate pace.
⬆ Progression
Smaller targets at greater distances and varying heights. Remove
forearm support. Add time pressure. Progress to standing. Introduce
dual task (count backwards aloud) to reduce conscious monitoring of
the limb.
Exercise 2 — Frenkel's Exercises (Lower Limb Coordination & Gait)
What: Graded lower limb coordination exercises developed specifically for ataxia rehabilitation. Begin supine: slide heel along a marked line, place
foot precisely on numbered floor targets. Progress to sitting: foot placement on marked footprints with visual feedback. Then standing and walking:
step precisely onto footprint markers on the floor.
Why: Frenkel's exercises use explicit visual and verbal feedback to substitute for impaired cerebellar proprioceptive integration — they train
compensatory motor programmes using intact visual and prefrontal pathways. They are directly translatable to Anika's functional gait complaint and
have been used in ataxia rehabilitation for over a century with good clinical support.
⬇ Regression
Supine position, wide target marks, slow speed, verbal cueing ("now
slide… now stop"). Therapist provides hands-on guidance initially for the
motor pattern.
⬆ Progression
Sitting → standing → walking with floor targets. Reduce visual cues
progressively. Add tandem walking. Introduce varied surfaces (foam,
slight incline). Progress to community environments when outdoor
safety is confirmed.
SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS
Always use a gait belt and have a spotter during all balance and gait activities — Anika can fall multidirectionally
Non-slip footwear and clear, uncluttered gym space are mandatory
Home exercise programme: prescribe only seated or supported exercises initially when unsupervised
Discuss fall management strategy: how to fall safely (guided descent), how to get up from the floor, and suitability of a personal emergency
response alarm
Assess and prescribe appropriate walking aid — a wider base of support (rollator frame, quadstick) reduces fall risk while allowing rehabilitation
to progress safely in parallel
PATIENT / CARER EDUCATION — KEY MESSAGES
"The cerebellum is a learning centre of the brain — the more you practise these exercises consistently, the more your nervous system adapts and
finds new ways to coordinate movement. Repetition is the medicine here."
"It is normal to make errors during these exercises — in fact, errors are what teach your brain to recalibrate. Struggling a little is a sign you are
working in the right zone."
"Falls are one of our biggest concerns, so we are going to make your environment as safe as possible and teach you strategies for if a fall does
happen. Please do not try balance exercises without your safety measures at home."
"Staying active within safe limits is really important — reducing all activity to avoid falling can actually make your balance and strength worse over
time. We will pace this carefully together."

LONG-TERM PLAN — WEEKS 4–12+
Transition to community-based neurological rehabilitation group where available — peer support is particularly motivating in progressive
conditions. Introduce evidence-informed activity programmes: dance therapy, Tai Chi, and aquatic physiotherapy all have emerging evidence for
balance, coordination, and falls reduction in ataxia populations. MDT liaison: occupational therapy for adaptive equipment (weighted cutlery, button

programme, fatigue management strategies (common in degenerative ataxia), and regular physiotherapy review every 8–12 weeks for programme
adjustment.

Pathophysiology of Working Diagnosis
WHAT IS HAPPENING NEUROLOGICALLY
Functional Neurological Disorder is a disorder of neurological function — the brain is producing abnormal motor and sensory output despite
structurally intact neural hardware, as confirmed by Chloe's normal MRI and nerve conduction studies. The current leading neuroscientific model
is the
PREDICTIVE PROCESSING FRAMEWORK
: the brain is a Bayesian prediction machine that constantly generates prior predictions ("beliefs") about the body's movements and state,
comparing them with incoming sensory signals to produce a "best guess" of what is happening. In FND, this system becomes dysregulated — the
brain generates an overpowering, fixed prior that the limb cannot move normally, and this prediction overrides normal voluntary motor commands.
Functionally, this manifests as symptoms that are real, involuntary, and neurologically generated, but not driven by structural damage to any
specific pathway. Neuroimaging studies have shown altered connectivity between the prefrontal cortex (attention and prediction), the limbic system
(particularly the amygdala — threat processing and emotional regulation), and the supplementary motor area (preparation of voluntary movement)
— suggesting that heightened arousal and focused attention on the limb paradoxically suppress normal voluntary motor initiation. This is
consistent with the key clinical feature: symptoms fluctuate with attention — they improve when Chloe is distracted and worsen when she focuses
on them or feels observed. The precipitating psychological stress likely sensitised this prefrontal-limbic-motor circuit, functioning as a "trigger"
event for the disorder.
WHY THIS PATIENT FITS — POSITIVE DIAGNOSTIC SIGNS OF FND
Positive Hoover Sign:
Left leg weakness on direct testing, but involuntary hip extension force returns when Chloe performs hip flexion on the right — this is the most
specific bedside sign of functional leg weakness (near 100% specificity in research); it demonstrates that the neural circuitry is intact but the
voluntary motor drive is blocked by the predictive system
Tremor entrainment:
Left arm tremor frequency changes when Chloe taps a rhythm with the unaffected hand — organic tremors are driven by fixed subcortical oscillators
and maintain their own frequency regardless of competing tasks; entrainment is a validated positive sign of functional tremor
Distractibility of weakness:
Leg strength improves significantly when Chloe is distracted — consistent with an attention-mediated disorder of voluntary motor control rather than a
fixed structural deficit
Normal MRI and nerve conduction studies:
Rules out structural lesion, inflammatory demyelination, and peripheral neuropathy — making an organic aetiological cause implausible as a primary
explanation
Acute psychosocial precipitant:
Sudden onset following significant psychological stress is the classic trigger event; consistent with sensitisation of the predictive processing system

Differential Diagnoses
✦ Primary: Functional Neurological Disorder — Motor Subtype (FND)
Confirmed by positive clinical signs: Hoover sign, tremor entrainment, and distractibility — these are not signs of exclusion but validated positive
findings with high specificity for FND. Modern neurology diagnoses FND on positive clinical evidence, not simply by ruling out alternatives. Normal
MRI and NCS rule out structural and peripheral pathology. Sudden onset following psychosocial stress and the clinical profile (young woman, peak
FND incidence 20–40s, female predominance 2–3:1) all fit.
Differential 1: Early Multiple Sclerosis (MS)
Supporting features initially: Young woman, unilateral motor symptoms, sudden onset — all consistent with a first MS relapse. However, MS would
show periventricular or juxtacortical white matter lesions on T2/FLAIR MRI, abnormal visual evoked potentials, and possibly oligoclonal bands in
CSF. The entirely normal MRI makes demyelinating disease very unlikely. The improvement in strength with distraction is also not a feature of UMN
weakness from MS.
Differential 2: Essential Tremor
Supporting features initially: Young adult with action tremor, no structural cause. However, essential tremor is typically bilateral, symmetrical, and
progressive — not acutely precipitated by psychological stress. Crucially, essential tremor does not entrain to externally imposed rhythms; its
frequency is maintained by a fixed cerebello-thalamic oscillator. The unilateral tremor, acute onset, entrainment sign, and normal NCS make FND the
far more parsimonious explanation.
WHY I FAVOUR FND
I favour FND over MS because the MRI is entirely normal — MS cannot produce motor symptoms without corresponding T2 lesions in the
relevant pathways. I favour FND over essential tremor because the entrainment sign is present — this is impossible with a fixed organic tremor
generator. Most importantly, I am making a positive diagnosis based on validated clinical signs, not simply a diagnosis of exclusion. This matters
clinically: a positive FND diagnosis allows me to explain the diagnosis credibly to Chloe and begin targeted treatment.

SPECIAL TESTS
Test 1 — Hoover Sign
MEASURES
 Positive FND motor sign — tests whether lower limb weakness is voluntary (intentional motor drive absent) or associated with intact

HOW TO PERFORM
1. Patient lies supine. Explain you are going to test the strength in both legs.
2. Place one hand under the heel of the weak left leg. Ask Chloe to lift the left leg against your resistance — note the force produced (typically

3. Now move your hand and ask Chloe to press DOWN with her right (unaffected) leg against resistance — hip extension task on the strong side.
4. While she presses the right leg down, feel for involuntary downward pressure (hip extension) returning in the left heel — without asking her to
push it down.
5. Note: you can also check the reciprocal — ask her to push the right leg UP (hip flexion) and feel for involuntary hip flexion appearing in the left
leg.
POSITIVE FINDING
 Involuntary extension force returns in the affected left limb during the contralateral task — demonstrates intact corticospinal
circuitry for the movement; the deficit is in voluntary motor initiation (intentional drive), not in the structural pathway itself.
WHY I CHOSE IT
 The Hoover sign has the highest specificity of any bedside motor sign for FND. Documenting and being able to explain a
positive Hoover sign allows me to make and communicate a confident, positive diagnosis — and importantly, to demonstrate to Chloe in real time
that her motor circuitry is working, which supports the neurological explanation of FND.
Test 2 — Tremor Entrainment Test
MEASURES
 Distinguishes functional tremor (frequency entrains to external rhythm) from organic tremors (fixed frequency driven by a subcortical
oscillator)
HOW TO PERFORM
1. Ask Chloe to hold the affected left arm outstretched so the tremor is visible. Observe and note the resting tremor frequency.
2. Ask her to tap a specific rhythm with her right (unaffected) hand — start with a slow, clear 2 Hz beat, counting aloud: "one, two, one, two…"
3. Observe whether the left arm tremor changes frequency toward the tapping rhythm, reduces in amplitude, or stops entirely during the cognitive
demand of tapping.
4. Now change the tapping speed and observe the left arm response again.
5. Try a complex rhythm (irregular beats) — functional tremors often suppress entirely under this dual-task load.
POSITIVE FINDING (Functional)
 Left arm tremor changes frequency toward the tapping rhythm, or suppresses significantly during the task. Organic
tremors (Parkinson's resting, essential, cerebellar) are generated by fixed subcortical circuits and maintain their own frequency regardless of
competing motor tasks.
WHY I CHOSE IT
 Tremor entrainment is a validated, widely taught positive sign for functional tremor. It is also a powerful psychoeducation
moment — I can show Chloe that her tremor is modifiable by where attention is directed, which supports the brain-network explanation I will give
her about FND.
Test 3 — Gait Observation: Inconsistency Assessment
MEASURES
 Documents inconsistency in the gait impairment (a positive FND feature) and baselines the gait pattern as a treatment target
HOW TO PERFORM
1. Observe Chloe walking from one end of the room to the other with no instruction other than to walk normally — document foot clearance, step
length, velocity, and left foot drag.
2. Ask her to walk while performing a cognitive dual task: "As you walk, count backwards from 100 in 7s."
3. Compare gait quality between conditions — note any improvement with the dual task.
4. Observe across multiple trials for intra-session variability — does the gait pattern change when she believes she is not being watched?
5. Note any features that do not match a consistent organic pattern (e.g., the drag appears on alternate trials, or changes sides briefly).
POSITIVE FINDING (Functional)
 Gait improves during dual-task or distraction; pattern is inconsistent across trials; features are atypical for a single
organic pattern (e.g., not a pure UMN scissoring gait or LMN steppage gait).
WHY I CHOSE IT
 Gait is Chloe's primary functional complaint and the most visible, disabling symptom. Documenting it provides my clinical
baseline, demonstrates the core FND feature of inconsistency for the examiner, and gives me direct measurable targets for gait retraining.
KEY NEUROLOGICAL OBSERVATIONS
Tone:
Often normal in FND; co-contraction (simultaneous agonist and antagonist firing) may create give-way or "collapsing" weakness pattern on manual
muscle testing — distinct from true paresis
Reflexes:
Should be intact and symmetrical throughout — asymmetric or abnormal reflexes would prompt immediate reconsideration of an organic lesion
Sensory:
FND sensory symptoms often have a "split midline" or anatomically inconsistent glove-and-stocking pattern — note carefully but avoid over-
investigating
Gait:
Observe foot drag, variability across trials, response to distraction; also test tandem walking — may be disproportionately impaired or paradoxically
normal
Mental state:
Note affect, eye contact, engagement and willingness to participate — clinical markers of readiness for rehabilitation and psychological co-morbidity
level that I communicate to the MDT
OUTCOME MEASURE
PRIMARY: PATIENT-SPECIFIC FUNCTIONAL SCALE (PSFS)
Chloe identifies her 3 most important functional goals and rates each 0–10 (0 = cannot perform, 10 = able to perform at pre-illness level). This is
person-centred, highly responsive to meaningful change, and avoids the risk of using impairment-based measures that may paradoxically increase
Chloe's focus on her symptoms. Minimum clinically important difference is 2 points.
SUPPLEMENTARY: PHQ-9 AND GAD-7
— given the very high prevalence of depression (PHQ-9) and anxiety (GAD-7) in FND, I screen at baseline and re-administer monthly. These
scores are shared with the MDT to inform the psychology co-management pathway.

NEUROPHYSIO RATIONALE FOR FND
FND physiotherapy is a
BOTTOM-UP MOTOR RETRAINING APPROACH
. Rather than asking Chloe to "try harder" to move — which engages the very attentional mechanism maintaining the disorder — I use techniques
that bypass voluntary motor focus: automatic movement initiation, rhythmic facilitation, distraction during movement, and positive reinforcement of
normal movement when it appears. I also aim to shift Chloe's attentional relationship with her limb from threat-monitoring to confident automatic
use. The therapeutic alliance is itself an active treatment component.
Exercise 1 — Rhythmic Auditory-Cued Walking (Automatic Gait Initiation)
What: Use a metronome or rhythmic music to initiate and regulate walking, shifting Chloe's attentional load from monitoring the affected leg to
tracking an external rhythmic cue. Begin with bilateral stepping on the spot in standing (with support available), progressing to forward walking.
Why: Rhythmic auditory cueing engages the basal ganglia and supplementary motor area via an automatic, exogenous attentional pathway —
distinct from the effortful voluntary pathway that is dysregulated in FND. This is why many FND patients walk better to music than on direct
instruction. It also reduces the "monitoring" of the affected limb that maintains the functional deficit, consistent with the predictive processing model.
⬇ Regression
Marching on spot to metronome in standing, hands on parallel bars for
safety. Therapist taps the rhythm on the patient's shoulder as an
additional tactile cue. Focus entirely on the rhythm — do not instruct the
patient to focus on the leg.
⬆ Progression
Forward walking to music with no support. Dual-task walking
(conversation, counting). Variable environments (corridors, outdoors).
Progressively remove the auditory cue and ask Chloe to internalise the
rhythm. Progress to community walking.
Exercise 2 — Mirror Therapy / Limb Reintegration
What: Chloe places both arms into a mirror box, positioned so the reflection of the unaffected right arm appears as if it were the left arm moving
normally. She performs slow, coordinated movements with the right arm while watching the reflected image. The brain receives congruent visual
input of a normally moving left arm.
Why: Mirror therapy provides the brain with congruent motor-sensory feedback for the affected limb — it delivers the visual signal of normal
movement to the body schema, updating the brain's distorted predictive model. It is also a top-down attentional redirection: Chloe is watching
movement rather than monitoring a tremor. Good evidence base in FND and CRPS for limb reintegration and tremor reduction.
⬇ Regression
Simple slow unidirectional movements (forearm flexion/extension) while
watching mirror image. Begin with 5-minute sessions — some patients
initially find the visual incongruence unsettling; introduce gradually.
⬆ Progression
More complex bilateral movements. Functional task simulation (reaching
for a cup, turning a door handle). Progressively reduce reliance on the
mirror as normal movement patterns re-emerge and Chloe develops
confidence in the limb without visual reassurance.
SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS
Never confront, challenge, or test inconsistency in a way that Chloe perceives as disbelieving — this worsens FND by amplifying threat
responses and attention toward the limb
Frame positive clinical signs (Hoover, entrainment) as informative rather than accusatory: "These signs help us understand what type of problem
this is, which is actually good news for treatment"
Falls risk during gait retraining: sudden give-way can occur — use contact guarding and gait belt throughout; ensure clear, unobstructed space
Monitor for symptom spread (new FND symptoms in other body regions) — common in early rehabilitation; document and discuss with MDT
promptly without amplifying Chloe's anxiety about it
Have a documented, team-agreed plan for dissociative episodes during sessions: remain calm, speak softly, do not restrain, position safely, allow
the episode to resolve, and debrief gently afterward
PATIENT EDUCATION — KEY MESSAGES
"FND is not a psychiatric disorder — it is a neurological one, now recognised in the same classification systems as other neurological conditions.
Your brain's networks are the issue, not your mind or your character."
"Because the brain learns and adapts, these circuit patterns can change. Rehabilitation is about retraining the networks — not pushing through the
symptoms by force, but finding ways to help the brain rediscover how to move normally."
"Your symptoms will probably fluctuate — better some days, worse on others. This variability is actually characteristic of FND and is a positive sign
that your brain's control is potentially changeable."
"Gradually increasing activity — with the right pacing — is important for recovery. Completely avoiding movement to protect yourself can maintain and
entrench the pattern. We will build this together, carefully and at your pace."

LONG-TERM PLAN — WEEKS 4–12+
MDT approach is the gold standard for FND and is now reflected in international clinical guidelines. I advocate for active collaboration with a
clinical psychologist or psychiatrist skilled in FND — therapeutic modalities including CBT adapted for FND, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy

University of Edinburgh) and the FND Hope patient charity network. Regular physiotherapy review every 6–8 weeks as Chloe develops self-
management skills, with the explicit goal of reducing dependence on formal therapy as recovery progresses.
