Case 6 — Mr. George Miller
75 years old · Male Parkinson's Disease · 12 years duration Hoehn & Yahr Stage IV (Advanced) Motor fluctuations ·
Two falls (turning) · Shuffling gait · FOG
Neurological Physiotherapy
I SUBJECTIVE PROBLEM LIST
• Physical Impairments
• Freezing of gait (FOG) — turning-triggered, confirmed by falls
context
• Shuffling gait — reduced step length, increased cadence, poor
heel clearance
• On-off motor fluctuations — sudden loss of mobility unpredictably
• No ROM deficits — impairment is central/neurological, not
musculoskeletal
II RED FLAGS
• 1. Recurrent injurious falls — already present
• 2. Severe orthostatic hypotension or syncope
• 3. Signs of aspiration or significant dysphagia
• Significance: Bulbar dysfunction in advanced PD impairs
swallowing coordination; aspiration pneumonia is the leading cause
of death in advanced PD. Reduced cough reflex may mean
aspiration is silent.
• 4. Acute cognitive deterioration, confusion, or delirium
• Action: Suspend assessment; notify medical team; basic cognitive
screen (abbreviated MMSE); check for signs of infection; do not
attempt any exercise or mobility work until medically cleared.
• 5. Signs of respiratory compromise (reduced chest expansion, low
SpO2, tachypnoea)
• Significance: Thoracic rigidity, kyphosis, and potential aspiration
reduce respiratory reserve in advanced PD; acute decompensation
is life-threatening.
I PATHOPHYSIOLOGY (KEY POINTS)
• This explains why FOG is most dangerous in confined, complex environments.
• Motor fluctuations — the "on-off" phenomenon — arise because after 12 years of levodopa therapy, the remaining dopaminergic neurons have lost their
capacity to buffer and gradually release dopamine.
• Dopamine availability in the striatum now mirrors levodopa plasma levels directly, oscillating sharply with each dose cycle.
• When levodopa levels fall (wearing-off) or fluctuate unpredictably (on-off), the striatum loses dopaminergic tone abruptly, and the patient transitions
from functional mobility to near-immobility within minutes.
• This sudden transition — combined with FOG and impaired postural reflexes — is what makes advanced PD so dangerous from a falls perspective.
• Miller's 12-year disease duration and Hoehn & Yahr IV status are consistent with the degree of dopaminergic depletion and Lewy body spread expected
at this stage.

I OBJECTIVE ASSESSMENTS & HOW TO PERFORM
Key Neurological Observations (Before Formal Testing)
Gait observation: Observe during entry to clinic — note shuffling pattern, heel clearance, step length, cadence; does FOG occur at the doorway? How does
he manage the turn to sit down? Is there festination (involuntary acceleration)? Postural stability: Standing posture — degree of thoracic kyphosis and
forward head position; sway; does he need to hold furniture? Centre of mass position relative to base of support Tone:
Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (FOG-Q) + Timed 360° Turn Test
What it measures: How to perform (360° Turn): Ensure a safety spotter is present — stand close enough to catch without interfering Patient stands on a
marked spot, feet shoulder-width apart Instruct: "Turn a full circle to the right, then a full circle to the left. Walk at your usual pace."
Pull Test (Postural Instability — MDS-UPDRS Item 3.12)
What it measures: Automatic postural righting responses — whether Mr. Miller can recover his balance from an unexpected backward perturbation. At
Hoehn & Yahr IV, I expect severely impaired postural reflexes. This test directly determines the severity of postural instability, guides walking aid
prescription, informs carer training in safe assistance, and is required for accurate UPDRS staging. How to perform: Perform with a second person present
as a safety catcher at the front — do NOT perform alone in advanced PD Patient stands with feet shoulder-width apart; warn them: "I'm going to pull your
shoulders backwards firmly — try to stay balanced, you may take steps"
I TREATMENT PLAN
• Does Mr. Miller understand the relationship between medication timing and safe mobility? Lack of understanding leads to dangerous unsupervised
activity during "off" periods

• Single task: ask patient to walk at comfortable pace end-to-end; record time and step count
