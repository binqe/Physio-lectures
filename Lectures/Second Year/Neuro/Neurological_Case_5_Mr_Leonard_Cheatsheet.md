Case 5 — Mr. Leonard Vance
66 years old · Male Parkinson's Disease · Diagnosed 12 months ago Hoehn & Yahr Stage I–II (Early) Resting tremor
R hand · Micrographia · Hypokinetic gait
Neurological Physiotherapy
I SUBJECTIVE PROBLEM LIST
• Physical Impairments
• Bradykinesia — slowed, reduced-amplitude movement
• Akinesia — difficulty initiating movement (chair)
• Reduced right arm swing during gait
• Decreased bilateral hip and knee flexor ROM
II RED FLAGS
• 1. Sudden or rapid cognitive decline
• Significance: May indicate Parkinson's Disease Dementia (PDD),
Lewy Body Dementia, or medication toxicity. Atypically rapid onset
suggests misdiagnosis — possible Parkinson's-Plus syndrome.
• Action: Suspend physical assessment; complete MoCA cognitive
screen; notify GP/neurologist urgently; do not proceed with exercise
unsupervised.
• 2. Severe orthostatic hypotension or syncopal episodes
• Significance: Autonomic dysfunction in PD — a drop in systolic BP
>20 mmHg on standing increases falls risk dramatically and can
mimic worsening gait.
• Action: Measure lying-to-standing BP before any mobility work;
cease exercise if symptomatic; refer to neurologist for medication
review (levodopa and dopamine agonists can worsen hypotension).
• 3. Dysphagia or aspiration symptoms (coughing after
eating/drinking)
• Significance: Bulbar involvement affects swallowing safety;
aspiration pneumonia is a leading cause of mortality in PD. Even in
early stages this warrants urgent attention.
I PATHOPHYSIOLOGY (KEY POINTS)
• Parkinson's Disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterised by the selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra
pars compacta (SNc).
• This thalamic suppression dampens motor cortex excitation, producing the cardinal features of hypokinesia, bradykinesia, and akinesia.
• Vance's diagnosis, the disease process was likely well established for years before his resting tremor became the presenting complaint.
• Resting tremor (4–6 Hz, "pill-rolling"): Arises from pathological oscillations in the thalamo-cortical and basal ganglia-thalamic circuits when normal
dampening by the dopaminergic system is lost.
• Suppressed during voluntary movement because cortical volitional drive temporarily overrides the oscillation — this is why the tremor disappears when
he reaches for a cup.
• Bradykinesia and akinesia: Direct consequence of reduced dopaminergic facilitation through the direct pathway — the motor cortex receives less
thalamic excitation, meaning movement initiation requires much greater cortical effort.

I OBJECTIVE ASSESSMENTS & HOW TO PERFORM
Key Neurological Observations (Before Formal Testing)
Tone: Passive movement of wrist and elbow bilaterally — assess for cogwheel (intermittent catches) or leadpipe (uniform) rigidity; note asymmetry (right
worse expected); Froment's manoeuvre (asking patient to swing contralateral arm) can enhance subtle rigidity Coordination: Finger-nose test (absence of
dysmetria distinguishes from cerebellar pathology); rapid alternating movements — pronation/supination (dysdiadochokinesia may be present due to
bradykinesia, but should not have the irregular timing of cerebellar dysfunction) Posture:
Timed Up and Go (TUG)
What it measures: A composite measure of functional mobility including sit-to-stand, walking, turning (180°), walking back, and returning to sitting. It
captures gait initiation speed, walking velocity, turning ability, and sit-to-stand — exactly the functional problems Mr. Vance reports. It also serves as a fall
risk screening tool. How to perform: Use a standard chair with armrests (height ~46 cm); patient seated fully back in the chair Place a cone or mark on the
floor exactly 3 metres in front of the chair
I TREATMENT PLAN
• 2. "Can you describe your walking — has it changed? Your wife mentioned you trail behind her; can you tell me more about that?"
• 3. "Tell me about getting out of a chair — when it's difficult, what exactly happens? Does it feel like your body won't start moving?"
• 4. "How are your medications working? Do you notice times of day when you feel more in control of your movement — or times when it feels harder?"
• 5. "Has your handwriting changed? Can you show me how you'd sign your name or write a sentence?"
