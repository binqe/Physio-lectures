## Slide 3

- What is Anatomy?:
- Definition:
- Anatomy is the branch of biology concerned with the study of the structure of organisms and their parts.
- Etymology: Derived formthe Greek word ‘anatome’ meaning dissection
- Subdivisions of Anatomy:
- Gross Anatomy: Study of structures visible to the naked eye.
- Limbs, Thorax, Pelvis and Perineum, Head and Neck, Neuroanatomy
- Microscopic Anatomy: Study of structures at a microscopic level (histology).
- Developmental Anatomy: Study of the growth and development of an organism (Embryology).

- Why Study Anatomy?
- Understanding Human Structure:
- Foundational knowledge for medical practice (physiotherapy).
- Basis for understanding physiology and disease.

## Slide 6

- Let’s Start at the Very Beginning?
- Anatomical Positions and Planes:
- Anatomical Position:
- Standing upright, arms at sides, palms facing forward,
- feet slightly apart.
- Body Planes:
- Sagittal Plane: Divides the body into right and left parts.
- Coronal (Frontal) Plane: Divides the body into anterior (front) and posterior (back) parts.
- Transverse Plane: Divides the body into superior (upper) and inferior (lower) parts.
- (Sagittal and Coronal run vertically to, and transverse runs parallel to the ground)

## Slide 7

- Cardinal Axes:
- (Imaginary lines that help us think about the body in 3-D)
- Useful for describing movements of the body
- Horizontal (x-axis): points Medial-lateral e.g. Flexion/Extension of hinge joints (elbow and knee)
- Longitudinal (y-axis): points Superior–inferior e.g. Movement of pivot joints AA joints, medial and lateral rotation
- Sagittal (z-axis): Points Anterior-Posterior e.g. Abduction/ Adduction of ball and socket joints (hip/shoulder)
- The arrows in this diagram represent the axes.

**Notes:** Directional terms and body planes allow us to describe the relationship between anatomical structures, for example, the wrist is distal to the elbow, the ears are lateral to the eyes, the nose is located in the midsagittal plane. They are a part of a universal language for understanding anatomy. Helps us avoid confusion and enhance communication between health professionals.

## Slide 8

- Anatomical Terminologies
- Directional Terms:
- Superior (Cranial): Toward the head.
- Inferior (Caudal): Away from the head.
- Anterior (Ventral): Front of the body.
- Posterior (Dorsal): Back of the body.
- Medial: Toward the midline.
- Lateral: Away from the midline.
- Proximal: Closer to the origin of the body part.
- Distal: Farther from the origin of the body part.

## Slide 10

- Regional Terminologies
- organizes the body into several body parts or regions:
- Body Regions:
- Cephalic (Head)
- Thoracic (Chest)
- Abdominal (Belly)
- Pelvic (Hip Area)
- Upper Limb (Arm and Forearm)
- Lower Limb (Thigh and Leg)

**Notes:** In Anatomy, we use specific terms to clearly communicate the location of structures within the human body. These are directional terms, regional terms and body planes. To avoid confusion and miscommunication, we always use a standard reference point for these terms; this reference point is the anatomical position. The anatomical position is when the body is standing erect, with the face looking forwards, the feet parallel, the arms hanging at the sides, the palms facing forwards and the thumbs pointing away from the body.

## Slide 11

- Body Cavities and Regions:
- Dorsal Cavity: Includes cranial and spinal cavities.
- Ventral Cavity: Includes thoracic and abdominopelvic cavities.
- Importance of Body Cavities: Provides space for organs and allows flexibility in movement.
- Abdominal Regions (used for clinical purposes):
- Epigastric, Umbilical, Hypogastric
- Right and Left Hypochondriac, Lumbar, and Iliac
- Example: The liver is primarily located in the right hypochondriac
- and epigastric regions. Useful for localising visceral and referred pain.

## Slide 12

- Movement Terminologies
- Examples:
- Flexion vs Extension: Bending vs straightening a joint.
- Abduction vs Adduction: Movement away from vs toward the midline.
- Supination vs Pronation: Rotation of the forearm to face palm up vs palm down.
- Example: Flexion of the knee occurs when bending the leg, and supination of the forearm happens when turning the palm upward.

## Slide 18

- Learning Strategies in Anatomy
- Active Learning: Engage in dissections for hands on experience with cadavers, lab practicals, and 3D anatomy tools.
- Visual Learning: Use diagrams, videos, and apps to understand body structures.
- Mnemonics: Employ mnemonic devices to remember anatomical terms (e.g., "Some Lovers Try Positions That They Can’t Handle" for carpal bones).
- Spaced Repetition: Regular review of terms and concepts over time.
- Case-Based Learning: Apply anatomical knowledge in clinical scenarios.

## Slide 19

- Study Tips for Anatomy
- Consistent, daily study sessions.
- Use mnemonics for memorization.
- Label diagrams repeatedly to reinforce knowledge.
- Attend practical lab sessions and ask questions.

## Slide 20

- Common Challenges in Learning Anatomy
- Volume of Information: Memorizing a large amount of data can be overwhelming.
- Understanding Spatial Relationships: Requires 3D thinking and practice.
- Clinical Application: Integrating anatomy with physiology and pathology.
- To succeed, come up with your own unique strategies to overcome these challenges.

## Slide 21

- Overcoming Challenges in Learning Anatomy
- Study Groups: Collaborate with peers to discuss and clarify difficult concepts.
- Regular Practice: Continually engage with materials through quizzes, apps, and lab sessions.
- Integration with Clinical Practice: Relate anatomical knowledge to case studies and real-world patient care.
