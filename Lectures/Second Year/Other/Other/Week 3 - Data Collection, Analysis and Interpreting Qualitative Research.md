## Slide 9

- Purposive Sampling in Research
- A non-probability sampling method where participants are deliberately selected based on specific characteristics relevant to the study

## Slide 10

- What is so special about it?
- Depth of Understanding
- Information-rich cases provide detailed and
- nuanced insights.
- Helps explore complex social, cultural, or behavioural phenomena.
- Diverse Perspectives
- Selection of participants from various backgrounds to capture multiple viewpoints.
- Enhances the study’s validity by including different
- experiences.

## Slide 11

- Probability Sampling in Quantitative Research
- A sampling technique where every individual in the population has an equal chance of being selected.
- Used in: Quantitative research for statistical
- generalization.
- Key Features:
- Random selection minimises bias.
- Results are statistically representative of the population.

## Slide 12

| Feature | Purposive Sampling (Qualitative) | Probability Sampling (Quantitative) |
| Selection Method | Based on specific characteristics | Random selection |
| Purpose | Deep, contextual insights | Generalizability |
| Data Collected | Rich, descriptive, exploratory | Numerical, statistical |
| Bias Risk | Higher (subjective selection) | Lower (randomization) |
| Example Study | Patient experiences with chronic illness | Disease prevalence in a population |

## Slide 13

- Importance of Defining the Study Population
- The study population consists of individuals relevant to
- the research question.
- Ensures relevance, accuracy, and generalisability of findings.
- A well-defined population helps reduce bias and improves research quality.
- Selection should be based on clear inclusion and
- exclusion criteria.

## Slide 14

- Research Question Guides Study Population
- A specific research question helps identify who should be studied.
- Examples:
- How do residents perceive public services in 	their community? → Study Population: Local 	residents using public services.

## Slide 15

- Key Factors in Defining Study Population
- Inclusion Criteria: Characteristics that participants must have.
- Exclusion Criteria: Characteristics that disqualify individuals.
- Study Scope: Define geographical, demographic, or behavioural limits.

## Slide 16

- Study Population Considerations
- Qualitative Research: Small, purposefully selected sample for in- depth insights.
- Quantitative Research: Larger, randomly selected sample for statistical generalization.
- Sample Size: Should be large 	enough to provide meaningful 	conclusions.
- Ethical Considerations: Ensure fair selection and avoid biased sampling.

## Slide 17

- Importance of Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
- Define who can and cannot participate in a study.
- Ensures relevance, accuracy, and validity of
- research findings.
- Helps reduce bias and improve data consistency.
- Consider demographic, behavioural, and health
- factors before selection.

## Slide 18

- Key Considerations for Defining Study Population
- Inclusion Criteria: Characteristics participants must have.
- Example: Adults aged 40+ with Type 2
- diabetes in a study on diabetes management.
- Exclusion Criteria: Characteristics that disqualify participants.
- Example: Pregnant women or those with Type 1 diabetes in the same study.
- Criteria should be clear and specific
- to ensure meaningful results.

## Slide 22

- Participant Recruitment Strategies
- Strategies vary based on study design, target population, and accessibility.
- Common recruitment methods:
- Gatekeepers
- Registers
- Formal & Informal Networks
- Snowball Sampling
- Advertisements
- Mixed-Method Recruitment

## Slide 23

- Gatekeepers & Registers
- Gatekeepers (hospital administrators, employers, community leaders) Benefits:
- Provides access to restricted or specialised
- groups.
- Builds trust through existing relationships.
- Challenges:
- May limit access or influence selection,
- leading to bias.
- Requires ethical approvals and negotiation.

## Slide 24

- Registers (e.g., health records,
- membership lists)
- Benefits:
- Large, pre-screened population
- for efficient recruitment.
- Allows for targeted participant selection.
- Challenges:
- Access restrictions (data privacy laws).
- Potential outdated or incomplete information.

## Slide 25

- Networks & Snowball Sampling
- Formal & Informal Networks (e.g., academic groups, social
- circles) Benefits:
- Uses trusted connections, increasing willingness to
- participate.
- Useful for qualitative research and building rapport.
- Challenges:
- May introduce selection bias if participants are too
- similar.
- Hard to ensure diverse representation.

## Slide 26


## Slide 27

- Advertisements & Mixed-Method Recruitment
- Mixed-Method Recruitment (combining multiple strategies) Benefits:
- Enhances diversity and improves recruitment success.
- Reduces limitations of single-
- method approaches.
- Challenges:
- Requires more time, resources, and coordination.

## Slide 28

- Advertisements & Mixed-Method Recruitment
- Advertisements (social media, flyers, online platforms)
- Benefits:
- Fast, broad reach for large and diverse audiences.
- Flexible across multiple media (digital & print).
- Challenges:
- Self-selection bias – Only specific
- individuals may respond.
- May not target the right population effectively.

## Slide 29

- Questions to Consider:

- How can you minimize bias in
- participant selection?
- What challenges might you face, and how can you overcome them?

## Slide 30

- Evaluating Quality in Participant Recruitment
- Six key evaluation criteria:
- 1.	Appropriate (suitability of recruitment strategy).
- 2.	Coherent (justification of study population).
- 3.	Transparent (clear inclusion/exclusion
- criteria).
- 4.	Interpretive (use of purposive sampling).
- 5.	Saturated (recruitment until data
- saturation).
- 6.	Ethical (adherence to research ethics).

## Slide 31

- Appropriateness & Coherence
- Appropriateness: Are recruitment strategies suitable for the study population?
- Effective: Strategies match the 	target population and research 	goals.
- Ineffective: Mismatched strategies lead to bias or low participation.

## Slide 32

- Appropriateness & Coherence
- Coherence: Is the study population well justified for the research topic?
- Effective: Selection aligns with
- research objectives. Ineffective: Poor justification weakens conclusions.
- Example: A study on teacher workload should recruit teachers, not students.

## Slide 33

- Transparency & Interpretive Sampling
- Transparency: Are inclusion/exclusion criteria clearly defined?
- Effective: Criteria are clear, 	public, and replicable.
- Ineffective: Vague selection 	process reduces study 	credibility.
- Interpretive: How was purposive sampling used?
- Effective: Sampling ensures 	rich and diverse data.
- Ineffective: Inconsistent 	selection reduces data quality.

## Slide 34

- Saturation: Were participants recruited until no new insights emerged?
- Effective: Data collection stops when themes repeat.
- Ineffective: Stopping too early limits
- findings.
- Ethical Considerations: Was recruitment conducted ethically?
- Effective: Informed consent, confidentiality, and voluntary participation maintained.
- Ineffective: Ethical lapses harm participants and study credibility.

## Slide 35

- Reflection & Discussion
- Which recruitment strategies best suit your research?
- What ethical issues should be considered?
- How can bias be minimized in participant selection?
- What challenges might arise in ensuring transparency and coherence?
- When should recruitment stop to ensure saturation?

## Slide 40

- Structure of an Interview Guide
- Introduction – Provides important information about the interview.
- Opening Questions – Helps establish rapport and
- ease participants into the conversation.
- Key/Grand Questions – Focuses on the core research topics with probing follow-ups.
- Closing Questions – Broader, reflective questions to summarize the discussion.
- Each section ensures a smooth progression from general to in-depth discussion.

## Slide 41

- Introduction & Opening Questions
- Introduction (Before the Interview Begins)
- Explain purpose of the study and the role of the participant.
- Obtain informed consent and assure confidentiality.
- Set expectations for duration and format of the interview.
- Opening Questions (Building Rapport)
- General, non-threatening questions to make participants comfortable.
- Example: Can you tell me a little about your background?
- Avoid sensitive topics at this stage.

## Slide 45

- Establishing Rapport
- Builds trust and comfort, encouraging honest responses.
- Techniques to develop rapport: Active listening – Show genuine interest.
- Non-judgmental approach – Avoid influencing responses.
- appropriate body language – Maintain eye contact, nod, and use an open posture.
- A relaxed participant is more open and willing to share.

## Slide 46

- Asking Questions in an Open and Empathic Way
- Use open-ended questions to
- encourage detailed responses.
- Avoid leading or biased questions.
- Examples:
- Do you like working from home? (Closed-ended)
- Can you describe your experience of working from home? (Open-ended)
- Helps uncover thoughts, emotions, and motivations.

## Slide 47

- Probing to Motivate Storytelling
- Encourages participants to elaborate and clarify their responses.
- Effective probing techniques: Silence – Give participants time to expand.
- Clarification questions – Can you
- explain what you mean by that? Encouragement – Tell me more about that experience.
- Helps gather rich, in-depth insights
- beyond surface-level answers.

- Introduction to Thematic Analysis
- Thematic analysis identifies patterns (themes) within qualitative data.
- It is a flexible method not tied to a specific theoretical perspective.

## Slide 55

- Inductive (Data-driven) Thematic Analysis
- Themes emerge from the data without predefined categories.
- No prior theory or framework guides theme identification.
- Commonly used in exploratory research.
- Deductive (Theory-driven) Thematic Analysis
- Themes are based on a pre-existing theoretical framework or research 	questions.
- The data is coded according to predetermined themes.

## Slide 60

- Framework Analysis
- Developed for applied policy research, Framework Analysis provides a structured method for managing and analysing qualitative data.
- It involves systematic coding,
- categorization, and interpretation.
- Often used in health, education, and social policy research.

## Slide 61

- Grounded Theory
- Developed by Glaser and Strauss (1967), Grounded Theory (GT) is an inductive method focused on developing theories from data rather than applying existing theories.
- Themes emerge from constant comparison 	and iterative coding rather than predefined 	categories.
- Uses open coding, axial coding, and selective coding to refine themes and build theoretical constructs.

## Slide 62

- Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA)
- IPA focuses on understanding individual lived experiences and how people make sense of those experiences.
- It involves deep interpretation rather than just categorizing data.
- Used in psychology, healthcare, and social
- research.

## Slide 63

- Narrative Analysis
- Narrative analysis seeks to unearth the deeper meanings behind the stories people tell about their lives.
- This analysis examines the structure and content of narratives, providing insights into how they shape identity and understanding.
- Context plays a vital role in narrative analysis, influencing how stories are constructed and understood.

## Slide 64

- Importance of Thematic Analysis
- First qualitative method to 	learn
- Core skills useful for 	other analyses
- Widely used in research 	and teaching
