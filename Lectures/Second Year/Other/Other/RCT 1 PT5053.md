ABSTRACT
Objective  To compare the short-­term, mid-­term 
and long-­term effects between three interventions 
(education only, education and strengthening 
exercises, education and motor control exercises) 
for rotator cuff-­related shoulder pain (RCRSP) on 
symptoms and function.
Methods  123 adults presenting with RCRSP took 
part in a 12-­week intervention. They were randomly 
assigned to 1 of 3 intervention groups. Symptoms and 
function were evaluated at baseline and at 3 weeks, 6 
weeks, 12 weeks and 24 weeks using the Disability of 
Arm, Shoulder and Hand Questionnaire (QuickDASH) 
(primary outcome) and Western Ontario Rotator Cuff 
Index (WORC). Linear mixed modelling was used to 
compare the effects of the three programmes on the 
outcomes.
Results  After 24 weeks, between-­group differences were 
−2.1 (-­7.7 to 3.5) (motor control vs education), 1.2 (-­4.9 
to 7.4) (strengthening vs education) and −3.3 (-­9.5 to 
2.8) (motor control vs strengthening) for the QuickDASH 
and 9.3 (1.5 to 17.1) (motor control vs education), 1.3 
(−7.6 to 10.2) (strengthening vs education) and 8.0 (−0.5 
to 16.5) (motor control vs strengthening) for the WORC. 
There was a significant group-­by-­time interaction (p=0.04) 
with QuickDASH, but follow-­up analyses did not reveal 
any clinically meaningful between-­group differences. There 
was no significant group-­by-­time interaction (p=0.39) for 
the WORC. Between-­group differences never exceeded the 
minimal clinically important difference of QuickDASH or 
WORC.
Conclusion  In people with RCRSP, the addition of 
motor control or strengthening exercises to education 
did not lead to larger improvements in symptoms 
and function compared with education alone. Further 
research should investigate the value of providing 
stepped care by identifying individuals who may only 
need education and those who would benefit from the 
addition of motor control or strengthening exercises.
Trial registration number  NCT03892603.
INTRODUCTION
Rotator 
cuff-­related 
shoulder 
pain 
(RCRSP) 
accounts for approximately 70% of shoulder pain 
consultations.1 It is associated with reduced function, 
decreased muscle performance, altered shoulder 
kinematics, and maladaptive pain behaviours 
such as kinesiophobia and catastrophisation.2–4 
Management commonly involves education, exer-
cise,5 6 medication, injections7 and surgery.8 9 
However, 30% of individuals do not report signifi-
cant improvement.10
WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
	
⇒Rotator cuff-­related shoulder pain (RCRSP) 
accounts for approximately 70% of shoulder 
pain consultations and it may lead to 
pain, reduced function, decreased muscle 
performance, altered shoulder kinematics, and 
maladaptive pain behaviours like kinesiophobia 
and catastrophisation.
	
⇒Education and exercises are two of the most 
frequently used interventions for RCRSP with 
evidence supporting their effectiveness.
	
⇒No study has directly compared education, 
strengthening exercises and motor control 
exercises for the management of RCRSP to 
better highlight recovery over time as well as 
the choice of intervention provided.
WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
	
⇒All three interventions (education only; 
education and motor control exercises; 
education and strengthening exercises) were 
effective in improving symptoms and functional 
limitations in individuals with RCRSP, but the 
addition of motor control or strengthening 
exercises did not lead to significantly better 
outcomes than education alone.
	
⇒A high load/low repetition strengthening 
exercises regimen did not lead to additional 
strength improvement compared with 
education alone or education combined with 
motor control exercises.
HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, 
PRACTICE OR POLICY
	
⇒Clinicians could consider using education as a 
stand-­alone intervention for the management 
of patients with RCRSP, as the addition 
of exercise to education did not improve 
outcomes.
	
⇒More research is needed to determine when 
including motor control or strengthening 
exercises in the rehabilitation of RCRSP will 
provide the most benefit.

Education is used to increase knowledge of the condition 
and present pain management options and their anticipated 
benefits.11 Education is also used to reassure, improve adher-
ence, confidence and self-­efficacy, and reduce kinesiophobia 
and pain catastrophising.12 However, education alone may not 
help improve the impaired movement and muscle performance 
observed in individuals with RCRSP, and resolve pain and func-
tional limitations.3 4 Thus, exercises may be indicated to address 
such impairments.3 4
Two of the most widely recommended types of exercises for 
RCRSP are strengthening and motor control exercises (MCE).13 
Strengthening aims to increase a muscle force production and 
the capacity of its tendon to sustain load.14 In contrast, MCE are 
used to facilitate movement that may be impeded by pain inhi-
bition, central nervous system reorganisation, muscle guarding 
and kinesiophobia.15 Both strengthening and MCE have been 
shown to be effective for decreasing pain and disability in indi-
viduals with RCRSP,16–18 although some uncertainty remains as 
to the added value of exercise compared with placebo.19
Education, combined with exercise therapy should be prior-
itised as the initial management for people seeking care with 
RCRSP.20–22 No specific exercise intervention has demonstrated 
superiority over another.13 The aim of this randomised clinical 
trial (RCT) is to compare the short-­term, mid-­term and long-­
term effects between three different interventions (education 
only, education and strengthening exercises, and education and 
MCE; comparing all three groups at once23) for people diag-
nosed with RCRSP on symptoms and functional limitations. The 
hypotheses were that both exercises groups would demonstrate 
a better outcome in pain and function compared with the educa-
tion only group, with the MCE leading to greater improvement 
in the short term.13
METHODS
This RCT adheres to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting 
Trials (CONSORT) multiarm extension23 24 and the Consensus 
on Exercise Reporting Template (CERT)25 guidelines (online 
supplemental file 1) and its protocol has been published.26 All 
participants provided written consent.
Participants
Adults presenting with RCRSP were recruited through the elec-
tronic mailing list of Université Laval (convenience sampling out 
of >52 000 individuals) using the criteria presented in table 1. A 
positive cluster of criteria 3, 4 and 5 was required for inclusion, 
which is similar, although not identical, to the one proposed by 
Michener et al.27
Study design
This multiarm RCT included five evaluation sessions over 24 
weeks (T0, T3, T6, T12, T24), six intervention sessions over 12 
weeks for both exercise groups and two education sessions 
over 12 weeks for the education group. During baseline eval-
uation, participants answered questions on sociodemographics 
and symptomatology, and completed self-­administered ques-
tionnaires on pain, functional limitations, kinesiophobia and 
pain catastrophising. Then, ultrasonographic measurements 
of the acromiohumeral distance (AHD) were conducted28 and 
isometric strength (peak force) was assessed. Thereafter, partici-
pants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: education, 
education and strengthening, education and motor control. All 
study outcomes (self-­administered questionnaires, ultrasono-
graphic and strength measurements) were re-­evaluated at T12, 
while only the self-­administered questionnaires were completed 
at T3, T6 and T24 using internet-­based questionnaires. Partici-
pants were instructed to declare to the assessors the use of any 
co-­intervention and adverse events (eg, pain exacerbation for 
more than 48 hours following exercises).
Randomisation and blinding
A randomisation list was generated by an independent research 
assistant using a random number generator. Allocation was 
concealed in sealed and opaque envelopes that were sequen-
tially numbered. Randomisation was stratified for gender (male/
female) and age groups (18–55/55–75 years), and blocked 
randomisation was used to obtain three equal groups of 41 
participants (random blocks of 3, 6 or 9). Only the assessors and 
statistician were blinded to group allocation.
Interventions
1.	 Education group (see online supplemental file 2): During two 
education sessions, participants were provided with informa-
tion by a physiotherapist pertaining to shoulder (anatomy 
and function), basic pain science, advice on pain manage-
ment (night and day) and activity modification (when to in-
crease and decrease),29–31 and were directed to watch a series 
of six educational videos on shoulder pain and function, per-
sistent pain, stress, and the importance of physical activity, 
sleep and healthy eating habits. After watching each video, 
participants were asked two questions: (1) What was the 
most important message you took from the video? (2) Was 
there anything you did not understand in the video? During 
the second education session, responses were reviewed, and 
all additional questions raised by the participants regarding 
the videos were answered.
2.	 Strengthening group:26 In addition to same advice and edu-
cation the education group received, participants were given 
a progressive shoulder strengthening exercises programme 
based on the maximum number of repetitions at 90% of 1 
repetition maximum (RM) that involved concentric and ec-
centric contractions with free weights and resistance elastic 
tubes. To determine 90% of 1 RM, the treating physiother-
apist first determined the 1 RM by finding the weight with 
which the participant could only complete one repetition 
of the exercise. Then, the weight was adjusted to 90% 1 
RM. Exercises targeted humeral internal/external rotators, 
abductors and the scapular muscles. Number of repetitions 
was one set of the maximum number of repetitions until 
muscular fatigue. If the pain level reached 3/10 or more at 
rest, participants were asked to start with a lower number of 
repetitions and increase or decrease depending on their pain

3.	 Motor control group:26 Participants received the same ad-

with different shoulder symptom modification procedures32

through three key areas: thoracic ‘finger on sternum tech-
nique’, scapular procedures, ‘humeral head’ procedures. If 
a technique reduced pain, that technique was then used dur-
ing elevation exercises in three planes (flexion, abduction, 
scaption) and incorporated into the participant’s everyday 
functional movements. MCE during arm elevation were pro-
gressed through a standardised six-­phase retraining sequence, 
where external feedback was progressively decreased and ex-
ternal load was slowly increased (without the load exceeding 
50% of 1 RM (>15 reps)).33 Once participants had reached 
pain free execution, the programme was progressed into ex-
ercises involving the whole body and mirroring the different 
functions performed by the shoulder. Number of repetitions 
varied from one to three sets of 15 repetitions.
Participants in both exercise groups were asked to complete 
the exercises at home every day and completed a daily diary 
of their exercise adherence (see online supplemental file 1 
for the CERT table). Participants were asked to report any 
adverse events in the exercise diary.
Outcomes
A blinded assessor performed all evaluations according to stan-
dardised procedures. Symptoms and functional limitations were 
evaluated using the Disability of Arm, Shoulder and Hand Ques-
tionnaire (QuickDASH) (minimal detectable change (MDC): 
11; minimal clinically important difference (MCID): 11),34 
the primary outcome, as well as with two other validated self-­
reported questionnaires: Western Ontario Rotator Cuff Index 
(WORC) (MDC: 12; MCID: 12)35 and short form of Brief Pain 
Inventory (BPI-­SF).36 Pain-­related fear and catastrophising were 
evaluated using the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK) and the 
Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), respectively.37 38
Since one intervention targeted motor control, ultraso-
nographic measurements of AHD at rest and at 60° of active 
abduction were performed to estimate the humeral head posi-
tion in relation to the acromion using a 12-­MHz linear array 
probe (Logic e9, GE Healthcare, USA).39 For each position, 
three measurements were taken (intraclass correlation coeffi-
cient (ICC): 0.98; MDC: 0.7 mm).28
Since one intervention targeted strengthening, maximum 
voluntary isometric contraction was assessed for shoulder abduc-
tors and external rotators using a handheld dynamometer (Atlas-­
Medic, Canada) as per validated methods (online supplemental 
file 3),40 which are similar to the ones suggested by Cools et al 
(ICC: 0.93–0.99).41 The peak force value (N) from three trials 
was used and normalised to lever arm length (Nm).
Sample size
Based on our sample size calculation, calculated for a between-­
group difference for our primary outcome, 41 participants were 
required per group (G*Power 3.1.9; effect size: 0.80, α=0.05, 
power=0.90, SD=13 QuickDASH points, MCID=11 Quick-
DASH points, expected lost at follow-­up=15%).34 42
Data analysis
Analyses were performed using linear mixed models (LMMs) 
with a random effect by participants to account for the correla-
tion present in repeated measures within individuals (R V.4.1.1). 
These models allowed us to analyse and compare (all three 
groups at once) the effects of the three programmes on symp-
toms and function, kinesiophobia and catastrophising (three 
interventions X 5 Time (T0, T3, T6, T12, T24)), as well as on 
isometric strength and ultrasonograph-­based outcomes (three 
interventions X 2 Time (T0, T12)). The two-­sided alpha level 
(0.05) was not adjusted for the various analyses. Overall inter-
action values of p were the primary focus of our analyses. If 
interactions were revealed during analyses, post hoc analyses 
were used to identify where the differences were. Using LMM 
allowed us to include baseline values in the model and adjust for 
age and gender.43 All available measurements were included in 
the model. LMM does not require a complete case approach and 
only needs to assume that missing values are missing at random. 
Effect sizes derived from the model are presented as the mean 
weekly improvement in questionnaire score over 12 weeks and 
24 weeks for all participants.

and protocol. They were added to determine if a high load/low 
repetitions strengthening programme led to greater increases 
in strength than the other interventions of interest. Second, 
our choice of statistical analyses differed from the initial trial

RESULTS
Participants
Between May 2019 and November 2020, 123 individuals with 
RCRSP (table  2) were recruited out of 245 potential partici-
pants (figure 1). Five participants dropped out before T12, and 
eight additional participants failed to return their questionnaire 
at T24 (follow-­up rate=89.4 %). Sixty-­three appointments were 
missed (attendance rate: 89%) with half of those related to 
COVID-­19. At week 12, self-­declared adherence to the MCE 
and strengthening exercises was 86% and 82%, respectively. No 
participant declared the use of a co-­intervention (ie, injection, 
surgery, concomitant management by a health professional) and 
there were no adverse events reported. Assessors declared not 
knowing group allocation for any participant.
Main outcomes
There was a significant group-­by-­time interaction (p=0.04) 
for QuickDASH, but follow-­up analyses did not reveal clini-
cally meaningful between-­group differences at any individual 
time point. There was no significant group-­by-­time interaction 
(p=0.39) for WORC. Although some of the pairwise group 
comparisons at individual time points were statistically signifi-
cant, none of them exceeded the clinically important thresholds 
of 11% (QuickDASH) or 12% (WORC), and these group differ-
ences were not observed across the entire study period (tables 3 
and 4, figure 2).
Secondary outcomes
There were significant time effects (p<0.001) for QuickDASH 
and WORC. According to our model, participants presented 
an overall mean weekly improvement of 0.8 and 1.2 points on 
the QuickDASH and the WORC over the 24-­week follow-­up 
period. When calculated for the 12-­week intervention period,

DISCUSSION
Contrary to our first hypothesis, the addition of MCE or 
strengthening (high load, limited repetitions) exercises did not 
offer additional benefits in reducing symptoms and functional 
limitations of individuals with RCRSP over education alone.

Education and advice related to pain and activity management 
could represent an interesting stand-­alone intervention for indi-
viduals with RCRSP and this needs to be further investigated by 
comparing education to an actual control group. In the context 
of a crying need for access to high-­value rehabilitation services, 
an education and advice session could lay a good foundation for 
low-­cost care that is accessible to all, regardless of socioeconomic 
background.44 In a qualitative study, Cridland et al reported that 
for patients with RCRSP, adequate individualised education was 
an important component in the management of their condition 
and patients felt it was best delivered by a trusted source, such 
as a healthcare professional.30 Still, we cannot confirm that the 
statistically and clinically significant improvement in symptoms 
and limitations observed in our study was solely due to educa-
tion since there was no control group.
In a systematic review Lafrance et al concluded that MCE 
probably led to a small but greater, positive short-­term effect 
compared with strengthening exercises.13 However, in the 
current study, neither education and MCE nor education and 
strengthening exercises outperformed the other, and neither 
outperformed education by itself. This finding led to the rejec-
tion of our second hypothesis. In addition to not providing 
additional benefits on symptoms and functional limitations, 
the combination of either MCE or strengthening with educa-
tion did not lead to significant biomechanical changes regarding 
AHD compared with education alone. MCE and education may 
operate on similar mechanisms, and like education, MCE might 
decrease kinesiophobia and increase pain self-­efficacy because of 
the progressive nature of the exercises preventing pain flare-­ups. 
For many patients, education may therefore be sufficient to 
promote those changes.45 46
While our strengthening exercise parameters were different 
than those usually prescribed in the literature, there is 
currently no consensus on the best parameters for exercise 
prescription. Fundamentally, strengthening aims to improve 
muscle strength by inducing neuromuscular adaptations.47 
As the effectiveness of lower loads to produce physiological 
changes has been questioned,48 we targeted higher loads to 
ensure that the physiological loads applied to the muscu-
lotendinous unit were sufficient to promote physiological 
adaptations.48 Our results should therefore only be gener-
alised to the exercises prescribed as other parameters (ie, 
repetitions, resistance) may have led to different results. The 
1 RM obtained with our sample of participants could have 
been skewed by the presence of pain or kinesiophobia. Thus, 
90% of 1 RM might not have been as high as 90% of 1 RM 
in the same individual without pain. Our results are similar 
to those obtained by Hopewell et al where the addition of 
progressive resisted/strengthening exercises of varying loads 
did not result in additional benefits when compared with best 
practice advice.49 Recent systematic reviews reported uncer-
tain clinical benefits associated with higher-­dose and higher-­
volume exercises as well as progressively loaded exercises.50 51
Both of our exercise groups included an external load 
component. However, we believe that the difference in load 
between the two is sufficient to distinguish them, as one 
group had a high load/low repetitions strategy, and another 
had a strategy with a much lower load (ie, <50% of 1 RM) 
and focused mainly on movement quality. High load/low 
repetitions strengthening exercises did not lead to signifi-
cantly greater strength improvement as all three groups 
improved their peak abduction force value. This overall 
strength improvement is most likely explained by a decrease 
in inhibition, initially caused by pain.52–54 These lower-­than-­
expected improvements following strengthening exercises 
have also been observed in other trials.55 Recently, Clausen 
et al concluded that adding a large dose of shoulder strength-
ening to usual care does not lead to better results in disability 
and strength after 4 months, compared with usual care alone, 
in individuals with persistent RCRSP.55 Strengthening exer-
cises still remain an important component to include towards 
the end of rehabilitation to optimise long-­term physical 
capacities.56 57
Limitations
First, we used a convenience sample where participants volun-
teered to take part in the study after seeing advertisements. 
Participants may have believed that the interventions proposed 
may help their symptoms, which may impact the external validity 
of our study. We did not consider the longitudinal design of our 
study or the presence of three groups in sample size calculation. 
For the longitudinal design, we would have needed to provide 
the variance and the correlation within repeated measurements. 
These are often difficult to obtain or estimate. This might have 
led us to overpower our study, which is not ideal but arguably 
better than underpower. As for the presence of three groups and 
of multiple comparisons, we believed that the approach used to 
calculate the sample size was one of the best in the absence of a 
single calculation that could have included all three groups and 
reflect our primary analysis of a single global test. The study was 
thus designed to be sufficiently powered for pairwise compari-
sons, without adjusting for the various possible analyses. Also,

CONCLUSION
Results from this study indicate that MCE or strengthening exer-
cises did not offer additional benefits in reducing symptoms and 
functional limitations of individuals with RCRSP over educa-
tion alone. Future research could look at the potential value of 
providing stepped care where education and advice could be 
delivered to patients before deciding if they truly need the addi-
tion of an exercise programme.
