## Slide 5

- Statistics Analysis
- A powerful tool that helps identify patterns, differences, and relationships between variables in data, using appropriate significance tests based on the data's nature and study design. It uses mathematical models to summarise data (descriptive statistics) and make predictions or inferences about a larger population based on a smaller sample (inferential statistics).

## Slide 6

- Why run statistical analysis?
- Make Sense of Data: Turns numbers into clear information you can understand.
- Check What Works: Shows if a treatment, method, or idea is really effective.
- Avoid Guesswork: Helps you make decisions based on evidence, not assumptions.
- Spot Patterns: Finds trends and relationships you might not see otherwise.
- Example:In physiotherapy, you might collect data on patients’ pain levels before and after a new exercise program. Statistical analysis helps you see if the improvement is real or just due to chance.

## Slide 7

- Description vs. Inferential
- Two types of statistics:
- Descriptive: Talking exclusively about the data and evidence you have.
- Inference: Drawing broader inferences about the world by talking also about the data you do not have (i.e., the missing data).

## Slide 8

- Mean, Median and Mode (i.e. Measures of Central Tendency)
- Measures of central tendency estimate a dataset's average or centre, finding the result using three methods: mean, mode, and median.

## Slide 9

- Range, IQR, standard deviation (i.e. measures of variability)
- Range: difference between highest and lowest value
- Interquartile Range (IQR): The range of the middle 50% of the data (difference between 75th and 25th percentiles).
- Standard Deviation: The square root of the variance, expressing the average distance from the mean in the original units.

## Slide 28

- Inferential Statistics
- A statistical test tells us whether a finding is likely to be real (significant) or just due to chance (nonsignificant).
- We begin with the assumption that there is no difference or association (like ‘innocent until proven guilty’) (null hypothesis).
- If the evidence is strong enough, we reject this assumption and conclude the finding is statistically significant. 	Statistical power is vital.
- The systematic procedure for deciding whether the results of a research study support a particular theory which applies to a population is referred to as hypothesis testing
- Task – Look at the RCT paper you have chosen for your assessment, what did the researchers hypothesise?

## Slide 29

- Simply
- If p < 0.05 then there is a significant difference/association/relationship
- If p > 0.05 then there is no significant difference/association/relationship

## Slide 31

- Common Statistical Values
| Statistic | Explanation |
| p | p‑value tells you how likely it is that your results occurred by chance. A small p‑value (e.g., p < 0.05) suggests the result is statistically significant, meaning the observed effect is unlikely to be due to random variation. |
| 95% CI | A confidence interval shows the range of values within which we are 95% confident the true effect lies. Wide CI = less certainty. Narrow CI = more precision. If a CI crosses 0 (for mean difference) or 1 (for ratios), the effect is usually not statistically significant. |
| mean difference | the difference between the average value of a variable in two groups. Example: mean pain score in intervention group − mean pain score in control group. |
| Relative Risk (RR) | compares the probability of an event happening in one group to another.
RR = 1 → no difference
RR < 1 → event is less likely in the treatment group
RR > 1 → event is more likely |
| Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) | ICC measures reliability — how consistent measurements are when repeated:
By the same person (intra‑rater)
By different people (inter‑rater) |
| χ² | tests whether there is a relationship between two categorical variables. |
| t‑value | comes from a t‑test and reflects how big the difference between two groups is relative to the variability within the groups. |
| r | correlation coefficient r measures the strength and direction of the relationship between two continuous variables. Values range from –1 to +1 |

## Slide 32

- Common statistical errors and misinterpretations
- 1) Failing to check parametric assumptions
- Parametric tests like t‑tests, Pearson correlation, ANOVA etc. have assumptions
- Violating assumptions can make results misleading.
- 2) Using the wrong statistical test for the study design and hypothesis
- 3) Small sample size problems (statistical power and power analysis)
- 4) Multiple Comparisons Without Correction
- Testing many outcomes increases the chance of false positive.
- 5) Selectively reporting only significant outcomes

## Slide 33

- Statistical significance vs clinical significance
- Is the result unlikely to have happened by chance?
- vs
- Is the result big enough to make a meaningful difference to a patient’s life or function?
| Concept | Meaning | Key Question | Example |
| Statistical Significance | Unlikely due to chance | Is it real? | p < 0.05 |
| Clinical Significance | Meaningful improvement | Does it matter? | Exceeds MCID |

**Notes:** MCID: Minimal Clinically Important Difference

## Slide 34

- Interpreting tables
- Start With the Basics

- Columns → Measurements, statistics (mean, SD, N, %)?
- Identify Key Variables
- What outcome is being measured? (e.g., pain, ROM, strength)
- What is being compared? (e.g., control vs intervention, timepoints)

## Slide 36

- Interpreting Graphs
- Start With the Basics
- Title → What is the graph about?
- Type of Graph:
- Bar chart → comparing groups
- Line graph → changes over time
- Scatter plot → relationships/correlations
- Box plot → spread and medians
- Axes:
- X‑axis = what is being measured over time, groups, or conditions.
- Y‑axis = the outcome variable (e.g., pain score, ROM, strength).

## Slide 37

- Interpreting Graphs
- Look for Key Patterns
- Is the trend increasing, decreasing, or flat?
- Are groups moving in different directions?
- Is there overlap between groups?
- Check Variability and Precision
- Error bars = standard deviation, standard error or confidence intervals
- Wide bars → more variability / less certainty
- Narrow bars → more consistent data
- Does the graph match the conclusions made by the authors of the study?
