## Slide 3

- Outcome Measures
- Most RCTs preselect a single primary outcome measure
- Followed by one or more secondary outcomes
- This is to evaluate treatment efficacy.
- Statistically significant = P < 0.05
- An outcome (or end point) is a direct or indirect measurement of the effect of an intervention on a participant's clinical function or status
- Ideally the results of an RCT should be generalised to the whole population

## Slide 4

- Patient Reported Outcome Measures (PROM)
- PROM are assessments made directly by the patients themselves rather than observers
- This includes symptoms of severity, of functional health status, health related quality of life
- Most PROMs are recorded in self completed questionnaires with validated scales, that are standardised and scored

## Slide 5

- Patient Reported Outcome Measures (PROM)
- PROM include collecting information on symptoms, health-related quality of life and functional status.
- Symptoms: (pain) – Visual Analog Scale (VAS)
- Functional status: The Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire, Barthel Index
- HRQoL: SF-36
- Health behaviour: The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)
- (Quel De Oliveira et al.)

## Slide 6

- Primary Outcome Measures
- Primary outcome measures: represent the main reason the trial is being conducted
- Normally an RCT has only ONE primary outcome measure

## Slide 7

- Secondary Outcome Measures
- RCT often have additional important outcomes
- These outcomes may not be statically conclusive
- They can be useful to generate further hypotheses and guide future trials and studies
- Because of their importance in justifying future studies, these additional outcomes need careful consideration

## Slide 8

- Bias in outcome measures
- Need to look for: outcome reporting bias
- This is a particular issue for PROM where multiple health domains are measured within a single questionnaire and not always fully reported on
- (Macefield et al.)

## Slide 9

- Bias in outcome measures
- Performance Bias: difference in the way patients in two treatment arms are managed post intervention
- Ascertainment Bias: collectively observed bias
- Reporting Bias: how the data is reported by assessors
- Both performance and observation bias can occur on a conscious or subconscious level and may arise from preexisting expectations about the efficacy of the treatment, knowledge or treatment allocation
- (Macefield et al.)

## Slide 10

- Reducing Bias in Outcome Measures
- One method that can be used to minimise the risk is blinding.
- Hide treatment allocation from the research team or trial participants
- (Macefield et al.)

## Slide 11

- Thoughts when critically appraising?
- Do these outcome measures match the population of this research?
- Can these outcome measures be replicated?
- Do they report on all the findings?
- Do the outcome measures link to the condition / intervention?

- To determine confidence in clinical tools

## Slide 13

- Validity
- The tools' ability to measure what it is supposed to measure
- Is the tool measuring the construct it is intent to?
- Implies that the tool is relatively free from error?
- If research has high validity, that means it produces results that correspond to real properties, and variations

## Slide 14

- Types of Validity
- Content Validity: Establishes that the multiple items that make up a questionnaire, inventory, or scale adequately sample the universe of content that defines the construct being measured.
- Construct Validity: Establishes the ability of an instrument to measure the dimensions and theoretical foundation of an abstract construct

## Slide 16

- Reliability
- Refers to the extend to which a measurement is consistent and free from error  how consistently a method measures something
- Test-retest reliability: the stability of scores obtained by a patient when they are evaluated on two separate occasions
- Intra-rater: the same evaluator over time (same tester over two separate occasions)
- Inter-rater: Different evaluators usually within the same time period

## Slide 19

- Responsiveness
- Sensitivity to change
- The ability of an instrument to measure small changes that are clinically important
- Where participants or patients respond to effective therapeutic interventions
- The responsiveness of a measuring instrument is its ability to detect change over time
- (Portney)

## Slide 20

- Minimal Detectable Difference / ChangeMDD / MDC
- The minimum amount of measured change needed before we can rule out measurement error as being solely responsible
- Minimal Clinically Important Difference MCID: is the smallest difference in a measured variable that signifies an important difference in the patient’s condition
- The MCID is a reflection of the test’s validity and can be helpful when choosing an instrument, setting goals for treatment, and determining whether treatment is successful.
- (Portney)

## Slide 21

- Ethical Considerations in Research
- Researchers must inform the potential participants when a study is planned that includes a control group (RCT)
- When the study involves randomisation, subjects should know that there is a chance they could be assigned into either control or intervention
- Confidentiality of all information should be maintained
- Subjects must be free to withdraw consent at any time
- (Portney)

## Slide 22

- Ethical Considerations in Research
- Is the study truly representing the patient group / representation
- Informed consent: The informed consent process begins with an invitation to participate. A statement of the purpose of the study permits potential subjects to decide whether they believe in or agree with the worth and importance of the re-search.
- (Portney)

## Slide 23

- Ethical Considerations in Research
- An important aspect of informed consent is the description of all reasonably foreseeable risks or discomforts to which the patient will be subjected, directly or indirectly, as part of the study. The researcher should detail the steps that will be taken to protect against these risks and the treatments that are available for potential side effects
- (Portney)

## Slide 24

- Research Misconduct
- Fabrication: making up data or results or recording them wrong
- Falsification: manipulating research materials, equipment or processes or results so that the data is not accurately represented
- Plagiarism: appropriation of another person’s idea, process, results or words without giving credit or representation
- (Portney)

## Slide 26

- Visual Analogue Scale
- The pain VAS is a unidimensional measure of pain intensity, used to record patients’ pain progression, or compare pain severity between patients with similar conditions
- A mean reduction in VAS of 3 points represents a clinically meaningful importance in pain severity.

## Slide 27

- The Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire
- The Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire is most sensitive for patients with mild to moderate disability due to acute, sub-acute, or chronic low back pain

## Slide 28

- SF-36
- An outcome measure that is often used, well-researched, self-reported measure of health.
- It comprises 36 questions that cover eight domains of health
- 1) Limitations in physical activities because of health problems.
- 2) Limitations in social activities because of physical or emotional problems
- 3) Limitations in usual role activities because of physical health problems
- 4) Bodily pain
- 5) General mental health (psychological distress and well-being)
- 6) Limitations in usual role activities because of emotional problems
- 7) Vitality (energy and fatigue)
- 8) General health perceptions
- The SF-36 is often used as a measure of a person or population’s QoL

## Slide 32

- Power Calculation
- A power calculation is made before the study to calculate the sample size necessary to detect a true difference between outcomes in the CONTROL and INTERVENTION groups
- You should ALWAYS see a power calculation reported, usually in the methods section
- It is based on the primary outcome measure – only the outcome for which research has been set up to measure
- Secondary outcomes are only there for guidance and future research

## Slide 33

- Power calculation
- Power of 80-90% is standard
- If authors do not manage to recruit enough people (or do but have a large drop out) then we say the study is UNDER POWERED
- This means it is harder to truly say the findings are significant

## Slide 34

- A p-value is calculated to assess whether results are likely to have occurred simply through chance
- P-values simply provide a cut off beyond which we assert that the findings  are statistically significant (p<0.05)
- 0.05 = 5% chance the result is a fluke between groups
- P Value

## Slide 35

- Confidence Intervals
- A confidence interval are calculated to measure the treatment effect
- It shows the range within the true treatment effect is likely to be
- Confidence intervals aid interpretation of clinical trial data by putting upper and lower bounds on the likely size of any treatment effect

- Critical Appraisal
- The success of evidence-based practice will depend on how well we incorporate research findings into our clinical judgments and treatment decisions. We have a responsibility to evaluate research reports to determine whether the findings provide sufficient evidence to support the effectiveness of current practices or offer alternatives that will improve patient care
- (Portney)
