🧑‍⚕️ Role of the Pelvic Health Physiotherapist

Holistic assessment (history, internal/external exam, functional movement)

Patient education and empowerment

Development of individualised rehab plans

Use of biofeedback, manual therapy, exercise prescription

Signposting or referring for red flags or surgical/medical care

Participation in MDT (Multidisciplinary Teams)


Did You Know?

Up to 1 in 3 women experience pelvic floor dysfunction in their lifetime (NICE NG123).

Pelvic health issues can occur at any age, not just during or after childbirth.

Evidence supports physiotherapy as the most effective non-surgical management option for pelvic floor dysfunction.



✅ Take-Home Messages:

Women’s health physio is not just postnatal care—it spans the lifespan.

It addresses intimate, sensitive conditions with clinical and empathetic care.

Recognised by NICE and professional bodies as first-line, essential treatment.

- Objective Assessment - Internal examiantion

**Notes:** Slide 6: Common Women’s Health Conditions Managed by Physiotherapists

Urinary Incontinence

Most common condition seen in women’s health physio.

Stress incontinence: leakage with exertion (cough, sneeze, exercise). due to an increase in intra-abdominal pressure, overwhelms the PF

Urge incontinence: sudden, strong urge with little warning.

Mixed: both stress and urge.

Management: pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), bladder training, lifestyle advice.

Pelvic Organ Prolapse (POP)

Descent of bladder, uterus, or rectum into vaginal space.

Symptoms: heaviness, bulge, dragging sensation, incomplete emptying.

Prevalence: up to 50% of parous women on examination, though not all are symptomatic.

Physio role: PFMT, education on load management, exercise modification; referral for pessaries/surgical consult if needed.

Pelvic Pain Syndromes

Includes dyspareunia (pain with sex), vulvodynia, chronic pelvic pain, and pain linked to endometriosis.

Complex biopsychosocial presentation — influenced by muscle tension, central sensitisation, trauma, hormonal changes.

Physio management: pelvic floor down-training, relaxation, manual therapy, desensitisation, pain education, pacing strategies.

Pregnancy-Related Musculoskeletal (MSK) Issues

Low back pain and pelvic girdle pain affect 45–70% of women during pregnancy.

Caused by hormonal laxity + increased load + altered posture.

Can limit mobility, sleep, work capacity.

Physio role: exercise prescription, belts/supports, manual therapy, activity modification.

Postnatal Dysfunction

Diastasis recti abdominis (DRA): widening of linea alba, impaired abdominal wall tension → affects trunk stability and load transfer.

Pelvic floor weakness: contributes to urinary leakage, prolapse, reduced sexual function.

Management: graded core and PFMT, functional rehab, education on safe return to activity.

Data & Impact

Urinary incontinence: up to 1 in 3 women; increases with age and childbirth.

Prolapse: ~50% of women post-vaginal delivery show some degree on exam.

Pregnancy-related MSK pain: affects nearly 2/3 of pregnant women, with ~20% experiencing severe disability.

These conditions significantly impact quality of life, mental health, return to work, and participation in exercise/sport.

**Notes:** Slide 5: Anatomy & Physiology – Abdominal Wall & Reproductive System


1. Layers of the Abdominal Wall

“When we talk about the abdominal wall, we’re really looking at several layers that work together for support, stability, and pressure regulation.

On the outside we’ve got skin and subcutaneous fat.

Then the muscle layers: the external oblique on the outside, the internal oblique just beneath it, and the transversus abdominis — the deepest, horizontal muscle that’s really important for core stability and managing intra-abdominal pressure.

We also have the rectus abdominis running vertically at the front, enclosed in its sheath, with the linea alba down the middle. This linea alba is the structure that stretches and can separate during pregnancy, causing diastasis recti.

Beneath the muscles, there’s the fascia layers and then the peritoneum lining the abdominal cavity.

For physiotherapists, the key point is that the abdominal wall works in close coordination with the pelvic floor and the diaphragm. Together they act like a pressure system — essential for continence, stability, and efficient movement.”

2. Changes During Pregnancy & Postpartum

“Pregnancy brings both hormonal and mechanical changes that affect the abdominal wall.

Hormones like relaxin and progesterone soften connective tissue and increase ligament laxity. This is necessary for childbirth, but it also means greater risk of instability and pain, particularly around the pelvis and lower back.

Mechanically, the growing uterus stretches the abdominal wall. The linea alba widens and thins, which can lead to diastasis recti. As the abdominal wall stretches, it becomes less effective at stabilising the trunk.

Posturally, women often develop an increased lumbar lordosis due to the forward weight shift, and the ribs flare outward, which changes breathing patterns.

Postpartum, the abdominal wall and linea alba don’t immediately return to their pre-pregnancy state. Collagen remodeling can take up to 12–18 months. Early postpartum rehab often focuses on restoring deep core and pelvic floor activation, not just surface strength.”

3. Reproductive Organs Relevant to Physio Care

“The reproductive system also undergoes huge changes that matter to us as physios.

The uterus grows massively in both size and weight, which compresses nearby organs — the bladder, intestines, and stomach — leading to urinary frequency, constipation, and reflux during pregnancy.

The ovaries continue their hormonal role, producing estrogen and progesterone, which affect circulation, tissue elasticity, and overall pelvic stability.

The vagina is a distensible muscular canal that supports the pelvic organs and stretches during childbirth. Trauma to this tissue or scarring from stitches can impact pelvic floor function and sexual health postpartum.

The pelvic floor muscles themselves are critical — particularly the levator ani group (puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus). They provide continence, organ support, sexual function, and pressure regulation. These are directly affected during pregnancy and birth, especially with vaginal delivery.

Supporting structures include the pelvic ligaments — broad, round, and uterosacral — which hold the uterus in place but become more lax with hormones, increasing strain on both pelvic floor and abdominal wall.

Finally, the bladder and rectum sit in very close relationship to the pelvic floor. That’s why pelvic floor dysfunction can lead to both urinary and bowel symptoms, which are central concerns in women’s health physiotherapy.”

✨ Summary point for delivery:
“As physios, understanding these anatomical layers and the physiological changes in pregnancy and postpartum gives us the foundation for assessing and treating issues like diastasis recti, incontinence, prolapse, and pelvic pain. The abdominal wall and reproductive system don’t function in isolation — they’re part of a dynamic pressure and support system that we can retrain through targeted rehab.”

**Notes:** Slide 7: Recognising Red Flags & Urgent Referral

Obstetric Emergencies

Pre-eclampsia: A potentially life-threatening condition marked by high blood pressure, severe headaches, visual disturbances (blurred vision, flashing lights), and swelling of face/hands. As physios, if we see these symptoms in a pregnant client, it’s an immediate referral to obstetric care.

Placenta previa or placental abruption: Both present with vaginal bleeding during pregnancy (with or without pain). Placenta previa is painless, abruption is often painful with uterine tenderness. Any bleeding in pregnancy is a red flag requiring urgent referral.

Gynaecological Malignancies

Early detection is crucial — physios may be the first health professional a woman discloses symptoms to.

Red flag symptoms: unexplained or abnormal vaginal bleeding (especially post-menopause or between periods), persistent bloating, pelvic or abdominal pain, and unexplained weight loss.

Our role: we do not diagnose but must recognise when symptoms are concerning and escalate appropriately.

Severe Infections

Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID): can progress rapidly to sepsis or infertility if untreated.

Symptoms include pelvic pain, fever, unusual discharge, painful intercourse, or pain on urination.

Any suspicion of acute infection with systemic features → urgent medical review.

When to Escalate Care

Physiotherapists should always be alert to:

Sudden onset of severe or worsening pain

Progressive neurological symptoms (e.g., bladder/bowel dysfunction, saddle anaesthesia → think cauda equina)

Systemic illness signs (fever, night sweats, unintended weight loss)

Obstetric emergencies (bleeding, hypertension, seizures, fluid loss)

If any of these are present → immediate referral to emergency services, GP, or specialist team depending on severity.

**Notes:** Slide 8: Biopsychosocial Impact of Women’s Health Conditions

“This slide brings together the biopsychosocial impact of women’s health conditions, which is central to physiotherapy practice.

Effects on Quality of Life

Conditions such as urinary incontinence, prolapse, or pelvic pain have a huge effect on everyday living. Women may avoid leaving the house, socialising, or sexual activity due to symptoms or embarrassment.

Work and caregiving roles are often disrupted. Even simple activities like lifting children, exercising, or travelling can become restricted.

Physical Function Limitations

Physically, these conditions reduce ability to participate in sport, exercise, and even daily household tasks.

Pain syndromes, fatigue, or incontinence can all lead to reduced mobility and deconditioning. This creates a vicious cycle — symptoms cause inactivity, inactivity worsens physical and emotional health.

Mental Wellbeing

The psychological impact is significant.

Many women experience anxiety, depression, embarrassment, or loss of confidence.

There is also stigma — symptoms like urinary leakage or prolapse are often hidden, leading to isolation.

This makes addressing the psychological and social context just as important as addressing the physical symptoms.

Research Evidence

Nygaard et al. (2010) showed that urinary incontinence is associated with a significant reduction in quality of life and an increased risk of depression.

Broader literature shows up to 50% of women with pelvic floor dysfunction report negative impacts on mental wellbeing, self-esteem, and relationships.

Visual (Biopsychosocial Model)

On the slide, you’ll see a simple diagram with three circles: Biological, Psychological, and Social factors, overlapping into the central “Patient Experience.”

This model helps us remember that symptoms don’t exist in isolation. For example, urinary incontinence (biological) may cause exercise avoidance (social), which leads to embarrassment and low mood (psychological). Our physiotherapy care must consider all of these layers.”

**Notes:** A 31-year-old woman has noticed urine leakage when she runs or jumps. She used to run regularly but has stopped because of embarrassment and frustration. She worries that her symptoms mean something is “wrong” with her body. She feels self-conscious about exercise and has noticed her mood declining since she stopped being active.

Biopsychosocial Mapping

1. Biological (physical/medical factors)

Stress urinary incontinence (leakage with running/jumping)

Possible pelvic floor weakness or dysfunction

Changes in connective tissue or muscle tone postpartum (if relevant)

Impact on fitness, cardiovascular health, and musculoskeletal system due to reduced activity

2. Psychological (thoughts, emotions, beliefs)

Embarrassment and shame associated with leakage

Anxiety about symptoms indicating something “wrong”

Reduced self-efficacy or confidence in exercising

Possible low mood or frustration from being less active

3. Social (environmental/relationship factors)

Avoidance of running or group exercise due to fear of leakage

Possible withdrawal from social interactions or exercise groups

Influence of societal stigma around incontinence

Impact on relationships or family life if activity was shared with partner/friends

Discussion / Reflection Questions for Students

How do her biological, psychological, and social factors interact to influence her behavior?

What might be the long-term consequences if she does not receive support or treatment?

What interventions could address each domain (e.g., physiotherapy, cognitive support, social encouragement)?

How could you approach goal setting to restore confidence in exercise?
