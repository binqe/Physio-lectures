## Page 12

Ontology – Relativism
• Reality is subjective and shaped by human experiences, beliefs, 
and culture.
• No single truth—reality varies across individuals and societies.
• Key Features:
• Knowledge is shaped by context and social interactions.
• Associated with interpretivism and qualitative research

## Page 13

Ontology –
Pragmatism
Reality is fluid and defined by what 
works best in a given situation.
Rejects strict divisions between 
objective and subjective reality.
Key Features:
• Reality is problem-oriented and changeable.
• Associated with mixed-methods research 
(qualitative + quantitative).

## Page 14

Ontology – Critical Realism (Constructivism)
• Reality is real but influenced by historical, political, and social 
structures.
• Bridges realism and relativism—reality exists, but perception is 
shaped by power structures.
• Key Features:
• Focuses on underlying mechanisms and hidden social structures.
• Used in social sciences, critical theory, and political studies.

Reality is entirely shaped by individual consciousness, and there

Used in phenomenological and narrative research, where 
researchers explore personal stories and emotions.

## Page 23

Epistemology – Positivism/ Objectivism
• Positivism assumes that knowledge is objective, measurable, and 
independent of human perception.
• Reality consists of facts that can be observed and quantified using 
scientific methods.
• Key features
• Objectivity
• Quantitative methods
• Causality

## Page 24

Epistemology – Interpretivism/ Constructivism
• Knowledge is constructed through human experiences, interactions, and 
cultural context.
• Unlike positivism, interpretivism does not seek objective facts but rather aims
to understand how individuals perceive and experience reality.
• Key features
• Lived experiences
• Qualitative methods
• Researcher involvement
• No absolute truth

## Page 26

Use Positivism when:
Seeking objective, measurable data.
Using experiments, surveys, or statistical methods. 
Aiming to establish causal relationships.
Example: Studying the impact of new medication on blood pressure.
Use Interpretivism when:
Seeking in-depth understanding of human experiences. 
Using interviews, case studies, ethnography.
Exploring social or cultural influences on behavior.
Example: Investigating how patients emotionally cope with chronic illness.
Choosing the right approach ensures research validity and relevance.

•

## Page 51

Ethnography 
(Understanding

•
Study of cultures, behaviours, and 
social practices through immersion in

•
Key Characteristics:
•
Conducted through participant 
observation, interviews, and field

•
Uses thick descriptions (detailed 
cultural accounts).

## Page 52

Phenomenology (Lived 
Experiences)
•
Focuses on how individuals

•
Key Characteristics
•
Uses in-depth interviews and
personal narratives.
•
Involves bracketing (suspending 
researcher biases).
•
Examines subjective meanings of 
lived experiences.

## Page 53

Symbolic Interactionism 
(Meaning Through 
Symbols)
•
Examines how people create meaning
through language, gestures, and social
cues.
•
Key Characteristics:
•
Meaning is socially constructed
through interactions.
•
Symbols are interpreted based on 
social context.
•
Focuses on micro-level interactions 
(e.g., conversations, identity 
formation).
