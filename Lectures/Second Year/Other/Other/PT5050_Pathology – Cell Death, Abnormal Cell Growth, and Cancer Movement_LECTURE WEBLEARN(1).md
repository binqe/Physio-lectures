## Slide 3

- What is Cell Injury?
- Cell injury occurs when a cell is exposed to stress it cannot adapt to.
- It represents the earliest stage of disease — before visible tissue changes occur.
- Injury can be:
- Reversible → the cell recovers when the stress is removed.
- Irreversible → the cell passes the “point of no return” and dies.
- The type, duration, and intensity of the insult determine the outcome.

## Slide 4

- Cell response to stress
- Cells respond to stress in stages:
- Normal function
- → Adaptation
- → Reversible injury
- → Irreversible injury (Death)
- → Cell death (necrosis, apoptosis, or necroptosis)
- Mild stress → adaptation and recovery
- Persistent or severe stress → cell death (necrosis/apoptosis)
- Outcome depends on the strength and duration of the insult and the cell’s capacity to adapt.
- Skeletal muscle cells adapt to repeated loading (hypertrophy), but heart cells under excessive pressure eventually die (infarction).

## Slide 5

- Many disease processes begin with cellular injury
- The environment around cells is dynamic and constantly changing.
- Cells are exposed to numerous stimuli and stresses, which may result in cell injury.

## Slide 10

- Reversible vs Irreversible Cell Injury
| Feature | Reversible Injury | Irreversible Injury |
| Definition | Early damage that can be repaired if the stress is removed | Permanent damage leading to cell death |
| Cell Morphology | Cell swelling, fatty change, chromatin clumping | Membrane rupture, nuclear fragmentation, lysed organelles |
| Mitochondria | Mild swelling, function restored if oxygen returns | Severe swelling, rupture → no ATP recovery |
| Plasma Membrane | Intact or slightly damaged | Irreversibly damaged → leakage of contents |
| Outcome | Recovery and normal function | Necrosis or apoptosis (cell death) |
- Whether a cell recovers or dies depends on:
- Severity and duration of the injury
- Type of cell and its ability to regenerate
- Availability of oxygen and nutrients

## Slide 12

- Necrosis – Uncontrolled Cell Death
- Necrosis is the uncontrolled, premature death of cells in living tissue.It results from severe or irreversible injury and is always pathological.
- Key Characteristics:
- Loss of membrane integrity → leakage of cell contents
- Triggers inflammation in the surrounding tissue
- Energy-independent process (no ATP required)
- Affects groups of cells, not single cells
- Common causes: ischemia, trauma, toxins, infection
- Necrosis = “messy death” — the cell bursts, spills its contents, and inflames nearby tissue.

## Slide 13

- Molecular Control
- of Necrosis
- Traditionally, necrosis was considered a purely accidental or uncontrolled process caused by severe injury (e.g. trauma, toxins, ischemia).
| Type of Cell Death | Trigger / Cause | Key Molecular Players | Outcome / Features |
| Classical Necrosis | Severe ischemia, trauma, toxins | None (unregulated) → ion pump failure, lysosomal enzyme release | Accidental cell rupture; tissue inflammation and scarring |
| Necroptosis | Death receptor activation (e.g. TNF) when caspases are inhibited | RIPK1, RIPK3, MLKL | Programmed necrosis; cell swelling and rupture via MLKL pore formation |
| Ferroptosis | Iron overload + oxidative stress (ROS accumulation) | Fe²⁺, GPX4 inhibition, lipid peroxidation enzymes | Iron-dependent lipid damage → membrane rupture; seen in ischemia-reperfusion injury |
| Pyroptosis | Infection, immune activation, inflammasome signaling | Caspase-1, Gasdermin D, IL-1β, IL-18 | Inflammatory death; membrane pore formation → cell lysis and cytokine release |

## Slide 14

- Types of Necrosis
- Different types of necrosis reflect the cause of injury and the tissue involved.
- Each type has a characteristic microscopic appearance and clinical relevance.
| Type of Necrosis | Description / Appearance | Typical Location / Example |
| Coagulative | Tissue architecture preserved; cells appear “ghost-like” | Most solid organs (esp. heart, kidney) after ischemia |
| Liquefactive | Tissue becomes soft, liquid; due to enzyme digestion | Brain infarcts, abscesses |
| Caseous | “Cheesy,” crumbly appearance; mix of dead cells and debris | Tuberculosis (TB) granulomas |
| Fat | Chalky white deposits (saponification of fats with calcium) | Pancreatitis, trauma to fat tissue |
| Fibrinoid | Immune-mediated; deposition of immune complexes in vessel walls | Vasculitis, hypertension-related vessel injury |

- Physiotherapy Implications:

## Slide 17

- Apoptosis is a regulated, energy-dependent process in which cells die without causing inflammation.It removes unwanted, damaged, or aged cells in a tidy, physiological way.
- Key Features:
- Controlled process – initiated by specific cellular signals
- Requires ATP – active, energy-dependent
- Cell shrinks and fragments into apoptotic bodies
- Membrane remains intact → no leakage
- No inflammation – cell contents safely phagocytosed
- Can be physiological or pathological
- Apoptosis – Programmed Cell Death (“Good Death”)
- Apoptosis = a built-in cellular “self-destruct” mechanism that maintains tissue health and prevents inflammation.

## Slide 18

- Molecular Control
- of Apoptosis
- Apoptosis is tightly regulated by caspases — enzymes that break down cell components in an orderly way.
- Two main pathways can trigger it:
- Intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway:Damage inside the cell (e.g. DNA damage, stress) causes mitochondria to release cytochrome c, which activates caspases.
- Extrinsic (receptor) pathway:Signals from outside the cell (e.g. death ligands binding to death receptors) activate caspases through the cell membrane.
- Both pathways lead to controlled cell dismantling without inflammation.
- Intrinsic: internal damage → mitochondria → caspase-9 → apoptosisExtrinsic: external death ligands → receptor → caspase-8 → apoptosisBoth end in activation of executioner caspases → cell death

## Slide 20

- Apoptosis vs Necrosis
| Feature | Apoptosis (“Clean Death”) | Necrosis (“Messy Death”) |
| Type of process | Controlled, programmed (regulated) | Uncontrolled, accidental |
| Energy (ATP) | Requires energy (active) | Energy failure (passive) |
| Cause | Normal development, DNA damage, stress | Severe injury, toxins, ischemia |
| Cell size | Shrinks (condensation) | Swells (oncosis) |
| Membrane integrity | Intact until the end | Ruptures early → leakage |
| Inflammation | None | Strong |
| Cells affected | Individual cells | Groups of cells |
| Nucleus | Condenses → fragments neatly | Swells → disintegrates |
| Outcome | Clean removal by phagocytes, no scarring | Tissue damage, fibrosis, scarring |

## Slide 21

- Autophagy – The Cell’s Recycling System
- Autophagy (from Greek “auto” = self, “phagy” = eating) is a cellular recycling process that breaks down and reuses damaged organelles and proteins to maintain energy and survival during stress.
- Key Points:
- Lysosomes digest unwanted or damaged components.
- Triggered by nutrient deprivation, stress, or ageing.
- Helps cells survive mild stress by cleaning and renewing themselves.
- If excessive → may lead to cell death.
- Examples:
- Active during fasting, exercise, or low oxygen conditions.
- Helps remove damaged mitochondria (“mitophagy”).
- It is important in muscle maintenance and in preventing degeneration.

## Slide 22

- Why Understanding Cell Death Matters in Physiotherapy
- Cell injury and death determine how tissues heal, adapt, and respond to rehabilitation.
- Physiotherapists use this knowledge to design safe, effective treatment plans based on tissue biology.
| Pathological Process | Effect on Tissue | Physiotherapy Implication |
| Reversible injury | Temporary dysfunction, swelling | Early movement & gentle exercise enhance recovery |
| Apoptosis | Controlled removal → regeneration | Supports tissue remodelling post-exercise or injury |
| Autophagy | Recycling → cell survival | Triggered by exercise and metabolic stress; aids recovery |
| Necrosis | Inflammation, fibrosis, scarring | Avoid load on necrotic/healing tissue; prevent further damage |
| Chronic cell injury | Fibrosis, atrophy, dysfunction | Long-term rehabilitation to restore or compensate function |

## Slide 34

- Neoplasia – When Growth Becomes Uncontrolled
- Neoplasia (from Greek “neo” = new, “plasis” = growth) is the autonomous, unregulated proliferation of cells that persists even after the original stimulus is removed.

## Slide 35

- Neoplasia Key Features:
- Growth is independent of normal controls (e.g., growth factors, checkpoints).
- Cells divide continuously, ignoring regulatory signals.
- Forms a mass of abnormal tissue — called a neoplasm or tumour.
- It can be benign (localised) or malignant (invasive and metastatic).
- Often arises from dysplasia — the “point of no return” in abnormal growth.

## Slide 36

- Tumour Characteristics – When Cells Go Rogue
| Feature | Description | Molecular / Cellular Mechanism | Analogy / Key Idea |
| Autonomy – Self-Sufficient Growth | Tumour cells no longer depend on external growth signals; they keep dividing even without stimulation. | - Activation of oncogenes (growth drivers)- Loss of tumour suppressor genes (p53, Rb) → no “brakes” on cell cycle | “They make their own rules.” |
| Invasiveness – No Respect for Boundaries | Tumour cells lose contact inhibition and spread into nearby tissues. | - Loss of cell adhesion molecules- Secretion of proteases that degrade the extracellular matrix | “They push, invade, and spread.” |
| Loss of Control – Abnormal Differentiation & Immortality | Cells lose normal structure and function (anaplasia) and evade death. | - Activation of telomerase (infinite division)- Apoptosis pathways switched off | “They forget who they are — and never stop.” |

## Slide 37

- Benign vs Malignant Tumours
| Feature | Benign Tumour | Malignant Tumour (Cancer) |
| Growth rate | Slow, limited, may stop | Rapid, uncontrolled |
| Growth pattern | Localised, often encapsulated | Invasive, infiltrates surrounding tissue |
| Cell differentiation | Well-differentiated; resembles normal tissue | Poorly differentiated (anaplasia) |
| Mitotic activity | Low, normal mitoses | High, abnormal mitoses |
| Invasion | No invasion; stays where it started | Invades nearby tissues |
| Metastasis | Absent | Present (via blood or lymph) |
| Effect on host | Minimal (unless compressing organs) | Severe; tissue destruction, cachexia |
| Recurrence after removal | Rare | Common |
| Example | Uterine fibroid, skin papilloma | Carcinoma, sarcoma, melanoma |

## Slide 38

- Cancer develops when the balance between cell growth and cell death is lost due to genetic mutations in key regulatory genes.
- Molecular Basis of Cancer – The Balance Lost
| Gene Type | Normal Function | When Mutated… | Example | Analogy |
| Oncogenes | Promote normal cell growth and division (as proto-oncogenes) | Become overactive → cells divide uncontrollably | RAS, MYC, HER2 | Gas pedal stuck on |
| Tumour Suppressor Genes | Stop cell cycle or trigger apoptosis when DNA is damaged | Become inactivated or deleted→ no “brakes” | p53, Rb, APC | Brakes fail |
| DNA Repair Genes | Fix mutations during replication | Mutation = genetic instability→ more errors | BRCA1/2, MLH1 | Mechanic missing |
- p53: “Guardian of the Genome”
- Detects DNA damage and halts cell division for repair.
- p53 mutations are found in >50% of human cancers.

## Slide 39

- Hallmarks of Cancer – The Six Core Behaviours
- Cancer cells share a set of biological capabilities that allow them to grow, survive, and spread — known as the Hallmarks of Cancer (Hanahan & Weinberg, 2000; updated 2011).
| Hallmark | Description | Example / Mechanism | Icon / Visual Cue |
| Sustaining proliferative signalling | Continuous self-signalling for growth | Overactive RAS, HER2 | Gas pedal stuck on |
| Evading growth suppressors | Ignore stop signals (e.g. p53, Rb loss) | Mutated p53, RB1 | Broken brakes |
| Resisting cell death | Avoid apoptosis despite DNA damage | Overexpression of Bcl-2 | Refuse to die |
| Enabling replicative immortality | Activate telomerase to keep dividing | Infinite cell divisions | Endless life |
| Inducing angiogenesis | Stimulate new blood vessel growth | Release VEGF | New blood supply |
| Activating invasion & metastasis | Invade tissues, spread via blood/lymph | Loss of adhesion (E-cadherin) | Spreading footprint |

## Slide 41
