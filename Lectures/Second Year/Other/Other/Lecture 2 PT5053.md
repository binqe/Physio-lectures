## Slide 2

- Purpose of Quantitative Research
- Quantitative research is about measuring, counting and testing relationships using numerical data.
- Its purpose includes:
- Describing phenomena numerically (e.g. prevalence of LBP)
- Testing hypotheses and identifying cause-effect relationships
- Measuring change over time
- Predicting outcomes
- Generalising findings to wider populations

## Slide 7

- Randomised Controlled Trials RCTs
- Definition: Are prospective studies that measure the effectiveness of a new treatment or intervention (Hariton and Locascio)
- Key Features:
- Randomisation – balances participants characteristics between groups
- Control or comparison group
- Prospective Design
- Often blinded (single / double) = participants, clinicians, researchers
- The RCT should have a very well defined, clear RCT protocol

## Slide 8


## Slide 9

- Randomised Control Trials RCTs
- Researches must carefully define the:
- Population
- Inclusion and exclusion criteria
- Process of randomisation
- Allocation
- Blinding of intervention
- Treatment and control
- Outcome of interest (outcome measures)
- Sample size required using power calculation
- (Hariton and Locascio; Bhide et al.)

## Slide 10

- RCT – Random Allocation
- Each of the eligible participants should have an equal chance to be allocated the intervention or not.
- Simplest way = parallel group design (intervention and control)
- If a particular baseline characteristic is of importance as will have an outcome on the results; it can be considered at randomisation
- Participants with or without baseline characteristics are randomly separated
- (Hariton and Locascio; Bhide et al.)

## Slide 11

- Types of Randomisation in RCT
- 1. Simple Randomisation: participants are assigned to treatment groups purely at random (random number generators). It can lead to imbalances in group sizes or characteristics – potentially affecting the validity of results.

## Slide 12

- Types of Randomisation in RCT
- 2. Stratified Randomisation: Accounts for specific variables such as age, gender, or baseline health conditions – that could influence treatment outcomes.
- Participants are grouped into strata based on these variables and randomised.
- This method reduces the risk of confounding and ensures a balance distribution of key characteristics across groups.

## Slide 14

- Types of Randomisation in RCT
- 3. Blocked Randomisation: ensures equal number of patients in each study group

## Slide 15

- RCT -  Blinding
- To eliminate BIAS
- Single blinded – participants blinded
- Double blinded – participants AND investigators blinded
- To eliminate unconscious information bias
- It is not always possible to blind the participants or investigators due to the nature of the RCT
- (Hariton and Locascio; Bhide et al.)

## Slide 16

- RCT Blinding
- Single blinded: Participants do not know which group they have been placed in until the experience has finished
- Very difficult in physiotherapy research
- Most commonly used for medicine

## Slide 17

- RCT Blinding
- Double Blinding – the group assignment is hidden from both the participant and the researchers

## Slide 18

- RCT Blinding
- Triple Blind Studies: Occurs when group assignment is hidden from the participants, researchers and data analysts

## Slide 19

- RCT – Collection of Outcome Measures
- Primary and secondary outcomes should be collected by independent observers who are unaware of the allocation and treatment arms of the participants.
- Outcome measures taken at baseline, post intervention and follow up (long term if able)
- (Hariton and Locascio; Bhide et al.)

## Slide 20

- RCT – Sample Size
- Aim: conduct a study that has adequate sample size and power so that the conclusions generated from the experiment can be applied with confidence
- The required sample size to test a hypothesis is governed by the effect size
- (Hariton and Locascio; Bhide et al.)

## Slide 21

- RCT – Power of a study
- If there are significant differences in the primary outcome between the two groups, one can conclude that the difference is likely to be due to the intervention
- Significant = probability is <0.05 (p) value
- (Hariton and Locascio; Bhide et al.)

## Slide 22

- Factors to consider
- Bias: systematic tendency of any factors associated with the design, conduct, analysis, evaluation and interpretation of the results of a study to make the estimate of the effect of a treatment or intervention deviate from its true value.
- In Physiotherapy:
- Selection bias – non representative groups?
- Information bias – inaccurate measurement?
- Financial incentive?
- Treatment bias?
- Detection bias – lack of blinding?
- (Hariton and Locascio; Bhide et al.)

## Slide 23

- Factors to consider
- Cofounding variable: a third factor related to both the EXPOSURE and the OUTCOME that distorts the true relationship
- E.g. Studying physio exercise & recovery; but age affects both adherence and recovery (age is a confounder)
- Only way to eliminate these differences is to perform stratified randomisation to everyone
- Contextual Factors: home based bs clinic? Therapeutic alliance?

## Slide 24

- Factors to consider
- Ethical: unethical to expose participants to any risk or burden from the research
- Gaining voluntary informed consent
- Participant drop out? Participant adherence
- Baseline characteristics?
- Population? Diverse population with the condition of interest required – reflect heterogeneous of population
- Adverse Events?

## Slide 25

- Factors to Consider
- Relevance of the outcome measures are they assessing the important factors that are relevant to this study?
- Do they have?
- Clinical relevance
- Good reliability
- Uniqueness
- Availability for all patients

## Slide 26

- Factors to consider
- Validity: Internal – can I trust the results?
- ‘How confident can we be that the observed effect is due to the exposure / intervention?’
- E.g. Selection bias; measurement error; loss to follow up
- Validity: External
- ‘Can the findings be generalised to the wider population?’
- A value or quality that can vary between subjects and / or over time
- Participants characteristics; clinical setting
- (Mann)

## Slide 27

- Factors to consider
- Conflict of interest:
- The authors report if there are any conflict of interest from the results of the study

## Slide 28

- Summary:
- Patient assignment to groups of treatment must be characterised by unpredictability: allocation randomised and concealed until patients are assigned to a group.
- Blinding is the best way to avoid performance and detection bias. Studies should clearly state who was blinded (care providers, patients, outcome assessors and/or data analysts)
- The primary outcome must be unique, be clinically relevant, be available for all patients, have a good reliability and reproducibility and be used for sample size calculations.
- (Estellat, Torgerson and Ravaud, 2009)

## Slide 30

- Qualitative Research – Observational
- Case control, cohort and cross-sectional studies are often referred to as OBSERVATIONAL studies
- This is because the investigator simply observes
- No interventions are carried out by the investigator
- Objective: Determine: prevalence, incidence, cause, prognosis, effect of treatment
- (Mann)

## Slide 31


## Slide 32

- Cohort Studies
- The best method for determining the incidence and natural history of a condition
- The studies may be prospective or retrospective
- Prospective Cohort Studies: A group of people are chosen who do not have the outcome of interest (e.g. diabetes). The investigator then measures a variety of variables that might be relevant to the development of the condition
- Over a period, the people in the sample are observed to see whether they develop the outcome of interest (e.g. diabetes)
- (Mann)

## Slide 33


## Slide 34

- Retrospective Cohort Study
- Historical longitudinal study in which researches look BACK to a certain point to analyse a particular group of subjects who have already experienced an outcome of interest
- Use data already collected for other purposes.
- The cohort is followed up retrospectively
- The study period may be many years to complete
- For example, a researcher might identify a group of people who have smoked and a group who never smoked and look back to determine the rate of lung cancer between the groups
- (Mann)

## Slide 36

- Cross sectional studies
- Used to determine prevalence
- Prevalence = the number of cases in a population at a given point in time
- Important as it influences the likelihood of any particular diagnosis
- Can be taken at one point of time or over time (longitudinal)

## Slide 37


## Slide 38

- Case Control Studies
- Usually retrospective
- People with the outcome of interest are matched with a control group who do not
- Retrospectively the researcher determines which individuals were exposure to the outcome of interest
- Case control studies determine the relative importance of a predictor variable in relation to the presence or absence of the disease
- (Mann)

## Slide 40

- Experimental vs Observational Designs
| Feature | Experimental (e.g., RCT) | Observational (e.g., cohort, case‑control, cross‑sectional) |
| Manipulation of exposure | Yes | No |
| Randomisation | Yes | No |
| Ethical constraints | Often high | Usually low |
| Real‑world applicability | Sometimes limited | Often high |

## Slide 41

- Strengths and Limitations:
- When RCTs are ideal:
- You need to test causal impact of an intervention
- Ethical and practical to assign treatments
- When observational studies are ideal:
- Studying long term prognosis
- When randomisation is not ethical or possible
- Investigating multiple exposure of outcomes
- Real world context / effects
