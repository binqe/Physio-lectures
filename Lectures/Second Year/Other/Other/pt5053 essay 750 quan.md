PT5053 – Evidence Based Practice
Critical Appraisal of the Efficacy of heel lifts versus calf muscle eccentric exercise for mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy (HEALTHY): a randomised trial
Word Count: 775
Introduction
Evidence based practice in physiotherapy requires physiotherapists to appraise research critically to determine whether the findings are trustworthy and appropriate for practice. To apply this, a randomised controlled trial (RCT) by Rabusin et al in 2021, which compared heel lifts with eccentric calf muscle exercises for mid portion Achilles tendinopathy, has been critically appraised using the CASP RCT checklist. This examines the study’s methodological quality, such as design, sampling, data collection, analysis, strengths, limitations and clinical relevance. As Achilles tendinopathy is prevalent in physiotherapy, evaluating the effectiveness of commonly prescribed interventions is important for deciding interventions.
Study Design
The study was a parallel group randomised superiority trial, which is a rigorous design or evaluating intervention effectiveness. The study followed the CONSORT guidelines, enhancing the transparency of the study. The study was randomised through a computer using permuted blocks, and allocation concealment was maintained by an independent online service. This aligns with the CASP as it minimises selection bias. Blinding of participants and research staff (CLR and JMG) was not feasible due to the nature of the treatments (Rabusin et al., 2020). However, research staff analysing the data (CLR, HBM and JMG) were blinded to group allocations (Rabusin et al., 2020). This reduces the chances of detection bias.
Sampling and Recruitment
100 adults with diagnosed mid portion Achilles tendinopathy were recruited through advertisement. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were clear and appropriate; however, the sampling may introduce selection bias as the sample represented many patients with chronic symptoms. The median duration found in Table 1 was between 18 and 22 ½ months (Rabusin et al., 2020). This limits the study's applicability to the acute diagnosis of Achilles tendinopathy. The characteristics of the sample group such as age, sex, height and other baseline characteristics, were balanced between the groups. This supports the validity of the study.
Data Collection Method
There were multiple data collection methods that related to physiotherapy. The primary outcome measure was the VISA-A questionnaire, which is a measure of pain, function and activity. Secondary outcome measures included ultrasound, tendon thickness, visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain severity, EQ 5D 5L for quality of life, physical activity, calf muscle endurance and patient reported improvement (PIC). These measures captured subjective and objective aspects of tendon health.
Data was collected at the start, 2nd, 6th and 12th week to see the change over time. A limitation to self reported adherence is that patients may have social desirability bias. They may overreport positive symptoms and underreport negative ones to present themselves better. The 12 week follow up limits insight into long term outcomes, which is relevant to Achilles tendinopathy management.
Data Analysis and Findings
The data analysis aligned with the CASP criteria. To preserve the benefits of randomisation, Rabusin et al. used an intention to treat analysis and applied multiple imputation to address the missing data. This reduces the chances of attrition bias. ANCOVA was used to compare the continuous outcome measures while adjusting for the baseline differences. Relative risk and number needed to treat (NNT) were used for binary outcomes.
The VISA-A showed a significant improvement favouring heel lifts as the adjusted mean was 9.6, p = 0.016 (Rabusin et al., 2020). It missed the 10 points, the predetermined minimum important difference, which questions the clinical significance of the study. Secondary outcome measures showed more reductions in pain and small improvements in quality of life in the heel lift group, but tendon structure and calf function showed no difference between the groups. Adherence was better in the heel lift group which may have contributed to better outcomes.
Strengths, Limitation and Trustworthiness
The strengths of the study include relevant outcome measures, blinded data analysts, robust randomisation and allocation concealment. The limitations include the lack of participant blinding, short follow ups, self reported adherence, which potentially leads to selection bias. But the study demonstrates good internal validity and moderate external validity. However, the confidence in the findings indicates good evidence but possible uncertainties.
Relevance To Physiotherapy
Heel lifts have demonstrated that they can be better than short term improvements to pain and function with more adherence and less risk. They may serve as an intervention for patients experiencing exercise compliance compared to eccentric calf exercises. However, eccentric calf exercises remain a vital intervention due to the long term benefits. As there aren’t long term follow ups in the study, it limits the conclusions about the outcomes, which are significant in Achilles tendinopathy management.
Conclusion
This RCT provides credible evidence that heel lifts offer short term benefits over eccentric exercise for Achilles tendinopathy. There are limitations, however, the findings show that adding heel lifts promotes adherence alongside progressive loading.
