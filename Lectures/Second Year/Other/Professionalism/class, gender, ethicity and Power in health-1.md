## Slide 9

- Barriers to Access to Care
- Race
- Race can be a barrier to access to care due to factors such as implicit bias, lack of culturally responsive care, and discriminatory policies.
- Ethnicity
- Ethnicity can influence access to care due to language barriers, cultural beliefs.
- Geographic Location
- Geographic location can create barriers to access to care due to lack of healthcare facilities, transportation issues and rural/urban divide.

## Slide 10

- Social Determinants and Their Effects on Health Equity
- Social Determinants
- Social Determinants are the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age.
- Health Inequity
- Health inequities are differences in health outcomes and access to care between different groups of people.

## Slide 11

- Exploring the Concept of Power in Society

## Slide 12

- Defining Power in Sociological Terms
- Power is the ability to influence others and is shaped by social relations, norms, and institutions.

## Slide 13

- Manifestations of Power in Societal Institutions
- Power in Government Institutions
- Power is often concentrated in government institutions, which can perpetuate power imbalances and contribute to health inequity.
- Power in Educational Institutions
- Educational institutions can perpetuate power imbalances and contribute to health inequity by privileging certain groups over others based on factors such as race, ethnicity, and socio-economic status.
- Power in Healthcare System
- The healthcare system can continue power imbalances and contribute to health inequity by providing unequal access to healthcare services based on factors such as race, ethnicity, and socio-economic status.

## Slide 14

- Power Dynamics Within the Healthcare System
- The power dynamics within the healthcare system can significantly impact access to care, clinical decision-making, and patient-provider interactions, leading to disparities in health outcomes.

## Slide 15

- Intersections Between Healthcare and Power in Society

## Slide 16

- Healthcare Systems and Societal Power Dynamics
- Societal power dynamics can influence the healthcare system, resulting in disparities in access to care, quality of care, and health outcomes. Political and economic factors can affect healthcare policies and funding, which can lead to systemic inequalities.

## Slide 17

- How Power Operates Within Healthcare Systems
- Power Dynamics in Clinical Encounters
- Power differentials can influence the quality of patient-provider relationships and care delivery in clinical encounters.
- Healthcare Access
- Power imbalances can also affect healthcare access and utilization, particularly among marginalized communities.
- Patient Outcomes
- Power dynamics within healthcare systems can ultimately impact patient outcomes, including morbidity and mortality rates.

## Slide 18

- Power Imbalances and Healthcare Delivery
- Impact on Clinical Decision-making
- Power imbalances between providers and patients can impact clinical decision-making, leading to suboptimal treatment plans and poor health outcomes.
- Patient-Provider Interactions
- Power imbalances between providers and patients can negatively impact patient-provider interactions, leading to a lack of trust, poor communication, and ultimately, poor health outcomes.

## Slide 19

- Impact on Marginalized Groups
- Healthcare Disparities
- Marginalized groups, such as racial and ethnic minorities, LGBTQ+ individuals, and low-income populations, experience healthcare disparities due to power imbalances in the healthcare system.
- Social Determinants of Health
- Social determinants of health, such as poverty, discrimination, and lack of access to education and healthcare, contribute to healthcare disparities among marginalized groups.

## Slide 20

- Implications of Power Differentials in Healthcare for Clinical Practice

## Slide 21

- Power Differentials Between Providers and Patients
- Impact on Clinical Decision-Making
- Power differentials can impact clinical decision-making by influencing the information that is shared, the treatment options presented, and the care provided to the patient.
- Patient-Provider Interactions
- Power differentials can impact patient-provider interactions by affecting communication, trust, and patient satisfaction.
- Impact on Health Outcomes
- Power differentials can impact health outcomes by influencing the quality of care received, adherence to treatment, and patient outcomes.

## Slide 22

- Implications for Healthcare Providers
- Power differentials can negatively impact healthcare providers, leading to burnout and other negative consequences.

## Slide 23

- Influence on Clinical Interactions and Decision-Making
- Power differentials between providers and patients can significantly impact the quality of communication, trust, and shared decision-making, leading to suboptimal outcomes for patients.

## Slide 24

- Strategies for Equitable Care Practices
- Understanding Power Differentials
- Promoting equitable care practices requires understanding and addressing power differentials in the healthcare system.
- Cultural Humility
- Cultural humility is the practice of self-reflection and self-critique by healthcare providers to understand and respect patients' cultural beliefs and values.
- Patient-Centred Care
- Patient-centred care is an approach to healthcare that prioritizes the needs and preferences of the patient.
- Advocacy for Marginalized Populations
- Promoting equitable care practices also involves advocacy for marginalized populations, such as racial and ethnic minorities, LGBTQ+ individuals, and individuals with low socioeconomic status.
