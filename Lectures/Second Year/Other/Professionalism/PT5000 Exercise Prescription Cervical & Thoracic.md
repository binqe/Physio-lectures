## Page 2

Cervical Spine
●
Acute <6 weeks, sub-acute (6-12), chronic >12 weeks
●
Chronic neck pain has the potential to change the cervical region biomechanics by 
adversely affecting the muscle imbalance and head-neck posture. 
●
Forward head posture problems are commonly seen in persons with neck pain. 
●
Systematic Review Findings: Low to high certainty of evidence for positive effects 
on pain and disability of the various exercise types used in chronic neck pain 
compared to non-exercise interventions. Since no specific exercise type was 
found to be superior, the authors suggest that patient preference and therapist 
expertise should guide the choice of treatment. The most important factor is that 
the patient engages in some form of active movement rather than remaining 
sedentary or relying solely on passive treatments (like massage or heat). 
(Rasmussen-Barr et al., 2023)
Rasmussen-Barr et al., 2023

## Page 3

Cervical Spine
●
Buckets of activity found in the SR:
○
Motor Control Exercises (MCE): Focus on the deep neck flexor muscles and coordination.
○
Resistance Training: Strength training for the superficial muscles of the neck and shoulder girdle.
○
Pilates: Exercises focusing on core stability, posture, and flexibility.
○
Yoga: Mind-body practice involving specific poses and breathing.
○
Tai Chi / Qigong (TCE): Gentle flow and movement-based mind-body exercise.
●
Other Key Findings:
○
Everything is better than nothing: There is low to high certainty that MCE, Pilates, resistance training, 
TCE, and yoga all provide short-term relief for neck pain compared to doing no exercise at all.
○
Impact on Disability: Most exercises (except for resistance training in some instances) also showed 
positive short-term effects on reducing disability.
○
The "No Winner" Conclusion: When researchers compared different types of exercises against each 
other, they found no significant difference. No single exercise type emerged as the "gold standard" or 
"best" option.
○
Sustainability: Most evidence focused on short-term results (immediately after the program). Long-term 
data was less certain, particularly for yoga.
Rasmussen-Barr et al., 2023

Deep Cervical Neck Flexors
●
The deep cervical neck flexor muscles provide postural endurance support, 
and it is known that activation of these muscles is impaired in people with neck 
pain.  
●
The deep cervical flexor muscles support the cervical lordosis and are an 
important stabiliser of the head and neck posture.
●
In individuals with mechanical neck pain, there is a decrease in muscle 
activation of the deep cervical muscles, causing superficial muscles such as 
the trapezius to undertake postural functions. 
●
A low load program of craniocervical flexion exercise focusing especially on 
motor control of the deep neck flexors has been shown to reduce neck pain 
and headache.

## Page 5

Deep Cervical Neck Flexors
●
Focuses on training the deep flexor muscles: 
●
Longus Capitis and Longus Colli.
●
Cues: Tuck your chin in or hold your head up
●
Can be done with an inflatable cuff pressure sensor.
-
Baseline of 20mmHg. 
-
Gently and slowly nod the head to 2mmHg
-
Continue along: 2 → 4 → 6 → 8 → 10mmHg is reached
-
Hold for 10 seconds x 6 reps

## Page 7

Considerations:
●
It is important to consider what is occurring above and below the joint
●
E.G. neck - shoulder, scapular, thoracic spine, upper body (bicep, tricep)
●
Using specific strengthening exercises as a part of routine practice for chronic 
neck pain, cervicogenic headache and radiculopathy may be beneficial. 
Strengthening and endurance exercises for the cervico-scapulothoracic and 
shoulder may be beneficial in reducing pain and improving function. 
●
THINK:
1.
What stage of healing? Acute, subacute, chronic
2. What is the impairment? ROM, PAIN?
3. What type of exercise is suitable? ROM, Isometric, Isotonic, Mobility?
4. How can I get this person engaging in rehab?
