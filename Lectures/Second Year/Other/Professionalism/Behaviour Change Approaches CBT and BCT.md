## Slide 6

- Theoretical Foundations
- Self-Efficacy Theory
- Proposed by Albert Bandura,
- This theory suggests that an individual’s belief in their capability to execute behaviours necessary to produce specific performance attainments is crucial for behaviour change.
- Theory of Planned Behaviour
- This theory suggests that intention is the primary predictor of behaviour,
- attitudes toward the behaviour,
- subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control.

## Slide 7

- Theoretical Foundations
- Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change)
- behaviour change as a process that occurs in stages
- precontemplation,
- contemplation,
- preparation,
- action,
- maintenance
- Health Belief Model (HBM)
- The model suggests that health-related behaviours are influenced by a person’s perceptions of the threat posed by a health problem.

## Slide 8

- Theoretical Foundations
- Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
- SDT focuses on motivation behind choices people make without external influence and interference.
- Social Norms Theory
- what is considered normal or acceptable within their social group.
- Social Cognitive Theory
- Also developed by Bandura,
- this theory emphasizes the role of observational learning,
- imitation, and modeling in behaviour change.

## Slide 9

- The Importance of Theory in Behaviour Change Approaches
- They are crucial in the fields of psychology, health promotion, and public health because they provide structured methods for influencing behaviour.
- Role of Theories in Guiding BCT Selection
- Theories provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying behaviour change.

## Slide 38

- Scenario 1
- John is a 46-year-old warehouse worker recovering from a lumbar strain he sustained six months ago. Although his pain has reduced and he has regained good mobility, John remains very cautious about moving. He avoids bending, lifting, and even doing basic house chores because he believes any wrong movement could “damage his back again.” His wife now does most of the physical work at home, and John feels guilty and frustrated but insists he is “being careful.” He reports stiffness and constant tension in his lower back from keeping it rigid. As the physiotherapist, you need to explore his beliefs and help him understand the difference between pain and harm. Your goal is to encourage graded movement, rebuild his confidence, and help him re-engage in normal daily activity.

## Slide 39

- Scenario 2
- Mary is a 67-year-old retired teacher who experienced a mild stroke 10 weeks ago, leaving her with weakness in her left arm and leg. She attends physiotherapy sessions regularly but puts in minimal effort and shows little interest in her exercises. Mary often says, “I’ll never get back to normal, so what’s the point?” She lives alone, has few visitors, and seems to have lost confidence in her ability to recover. During therapy she appears withdrawn and tired, doing only the minimum required. As the physiotherapist, your challenge is to explore her thoughts and feelings, identify what matters most to her, and help her rediscover motivation through positive feedback, small achievable goals, and active involvement in planning her rehabilitation.

## Slide 40

- Scenario 3
- Aisha is a 39-year-old office worker being treated for shoulder impingement. She has attended three physiotherapy sessions and understands her home exercise programme but rarely completes it. When asked, she says she “forgets most days” or feels “too tired after work.” She expresses frustration about her lack of progress, commenting that “these exercises don’t seem to make much difference anyway.” Aisha juggles a demanding job and family responsibilities, often putting her own needs last. As her physiotherapist, you need to explore her barriers, identify what is realistic for her schedule, and help her develop a simple, sustainable plan that fits her lifestyle. The focus is on improving adherence through collaborative goal setting and problem solving.
