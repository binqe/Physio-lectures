I PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
• Asthma is characterised by chronic eosinophilic airway inflammation and bronchial hyperresponsiveness (BHR).
• In sensitised individuals (atopic asthma), airway epithelium is primed to over-react to triggers.
• Rodriguez has two simultaneous triggers: viral URTI causes direct epithelial injury, releasing inflammatory mediators (histamine, leukotrienes,
prostaglandins) that amplify BHR; pollen allergens cross-link IgE on mast cells → degranulation → acute bronchoconstriction.
• Bronchoconstriction dramatically increases airway resistance.
• By Poiseuille's law, resistance ∝ 1/rI — halving the airway radius increases resistance 16-fold.
I DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES
1. Vocal Cord Dysfunction (VCD) / Inducible Laryngeal Obstruction (ILO)
• Young female — VCD is most prevalent in young women aged 20–40
• Episodic breathlessness — can be triggered by stress or exercise (postgraduate student, high pressure environment)
• Does not fully respond to bronchodilators
2. Anaphylaxis with Bronchospasm
• Atopic individual with acute onset severe breathlessness
• High-pollen allergen exposure on day of presentation
• Bronchospasm can be a component of a systemic allergic reaction

I TREATMENT PLAN
I Initial Phase — Acute Management (ED / Hours 1–48)
• Shake the inhaler; remove the cap
• Breathe out gently (not forcefully)
• Seal lips firmly around mouthpiece (or spacer mouthpiece)
• Begin slow inhalation — press canister down once at the very start
• Continue slow inhalation over 3–5 seconds

I Patient Education — Key Messages
• Preventer vs. reliever — the most important distinction: "Your preventer inhaler [ICS] must be taken every day — even when you feel completely well.
It reduces the inflammation that causes these attacks. Your blue inhaler is for relief in the moment — it does not treat the underlying inflammation."
• Written Asthma Action Plan: Traffic-light system — Green (well, take preventer as usual); Amber (increasing symptoms — take extra reliever, start
oral steroid if prescribed, contact GP); Red (severe attack not responding — call 999 immediately). Every patient should leave hospital with a written
plan.
• Trigger avoidance: Check daily pollen count (high pollen days — keep windows closed, shower after being outdoors, wear wrap-around sunglasses).
For viral illness — begin reliever use early at first sign of symptoms; contact GP proactively.
• When to call emergency services: "If your blue inhaler is not working after 4 puffs in 10 minutes, your breathing is getting worse, or you cannot speak
— call 999. Never wait to see if it improves on its own in that situation."
I Long-Term — Weeks 1–4 Post-Discharge and Self-Management
• GP/Asthma nurse follow-up within 48 hours of discharge: Review medication step; ensure written action plan provided; consider specialist referral if
this is a second ED attendance within 12 months.
• Breathing retraining programme: Buteyko or Papworth method — evidence supports ↓ reliever use and ↑ quality of life in chronic dysfunctional
breathing. Particularly appropriate given the likely anxiety-asthma overlap.
• Psychological support: Screen for health anxiety and panic disorder (both share positive feedback loops with breathlessness). Refer for CBT if
indicated — stress management also reduces exacerbation frequency.
• Discharge criteria: Demonstrates correct inhaler technique independently; has personalised written action plan; peak flow ≥80% personal best; ACT
score ≥20 (well-controlled); follow-up appointment within 1 week confirmed.
