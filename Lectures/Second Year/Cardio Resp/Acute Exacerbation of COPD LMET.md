## Slide 5

- What is an exacerbation?
- An exacerbation is a sustained worsening of the patient's symptoms from their usual stable state which is beyond normal day-to-day variations, and is acute in onset. Commonly reported symptoms are worsening breathlessness, cough, increased sputum production and change in sputum colour. The change in these symptoms often necessitates a change in medication.
- (NICE, 2018)

**Notes:** What is an exacerbation? A flare up of systems e.g. cough, increased SOB that worsens in < 14 days. Can be caused by a virus, bacteria or pollution thus it can be infective or non-infective. Can be mild, moderate or severe.

## Slide 12

- Question 3
- Nebulisers
- Antibiotics and steroids
- Oxygen – target saturations 88-92%
- O
- Answers

**Notes:** Goal of treatment: To minimise the impact of the current exacerbation and prevent the development of subsequent events
B2 agonists work directly on smooth muscle – sympathetic pathway
Anti-cholinergics block channels and prevent acetylcholine binding – parasympathetic pathways
No evidence in favour of nebulisers over MDIs but nebulisers tend to be easier for sicker patients 
If a person with COPD is hypercapnic or acidotic the nebuliser should be driven by compressed air rather than oxygen
Antibiotics: Should be considered in the presence of increased volume of sputum and increased purulence + SOB. Shorten recovery time, reduce the risk of early relapse, treatment failure and hospitalisation duration – local bacterial resistance pattern
Steroids: Reduce inflammation, shorten recovery time,  improve lung function, oxygenation,  reduce risk of early relapse, treatment failure and the length of hospitalisation – note increased risk of pneumonia. Osteoporosis prophylaxis to be considered

## Slide 16

- Type 1 Respiratory Failure
- Decreased PaO2
- Normal or low PaCO2
- Treated with supplementary oxygen

## Slide 17

- Type 2 Respiratory Failure
- Decreased PaO2
- Increased PaCO2
- Alveola hypoventilation
- Can be acute or chronic

## Slide 18

- Acute Type 2 Respiratory Failure
- Decreased PaO2
- Increased PaCO2
- Normal HCO3-
- Kidneys take 2-3 days to compensate for raised PaCO2
- Decreased pH – respiratory acidosis

## Slide 19

- Chronic Type 2 Respiratory Failure
- Decreased PaO2
- Increased PaCO2
- Raised HCO3-
- Kidneys have now compensated
- Normal pH
- Example - COPD

## Slide 20

- Acute on Chronic Type 2 Respiratory Failure
- Decreased PaO2
- Increased PaCO2
- Raised HCO3-
- Decreased pH – acidotic
- PaCO2 normally high but due to sudden deterioration has risen further so kidneys no longer able to compensate
- E.g. acute exacerbation of COPD
