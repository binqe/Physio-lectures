## Slide 3

- Sternotomy - Specific Respiratory Consequences
- Chest wall splinting:
- Post-operative pain causes patients to adopt shallow, rapid breathing patterns, reducing tidal volume and preventing effective coughing. This is the single most important physiotherapy target in the early post-operative period.
- Reduced lung compliance:
- Altering the pressure-volume relationship. Can impair gas flow and therefore mucociliary clearance
- Phrenic nerve injury:
- Occurs in up to 10–15% of cardiac surgical cases. Causes ipsilateral diaphragmatic palsy - a significant contributor to post-operative hypoxaemia and prolonged weaning.
- Pleural breach:
- Both pleural spaces are routinely entered during cardiac surgery; even if pleural drains are placed, residual effusions and pneumothorax risk persist and impair lung expansion.
- Sternal instability (rare):
- Presents as paradoxical chest wall movement, palpable 'click', and significantly impairs coughing and deep breathing. Requires surgical review and modification of physiotherapy techniques (avoid resistive breathing techniques; use hands-over-sternum support for all coughing).

## Slide 4

- Thoracotomy - Specific Respiratory Consequences
- Lateral thoracotomy (used for lung resection, transplantation, and oesophageal surgery) involves division of chest wall muscles and rib spreading, with distinct physiological consequences.
- Intercostal nerve damage: thoracotomy causes direct trauma to intercostal nerves at the level of rib spreading. This produces significant post-operative pain and sensory loss, severely impairing the ability to take deep breaths and cough.
- Muscle division: division of serratus anterior, latissimus dorsi, and intercostal muscles reduces the mechanical efficiency of the respiratory pump on the operative side.
- Pneumothorax and pleural space management: post-thoracotomy chest drains are placed to evacuate air and blood. Drain management is critical to physiotherapy - malpositioned or blocked drains delay lung re-expansion.
- Loss of lung tissue (post-resection): lobectomy or pneumonectomy reduces total lung parenchyma and requires significant adaptation of the remaining lung. Post-pneumonectomy, the contralateral lung must compensate for the entire gas exchange load.
- VATS (Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery): compared to open thoracotomy, VATS is associated with less pain, fewer pulmonary complications, and faster physiotherapy progression - but does not eliminate the risk of atelectasis or secretion retention.

## Slide 6

- 2.2  Systemic Inflammatory Response and Implications for Physiotherapy
- Major surgery - particularly cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB/ blood pumping machine) - activates a profound systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), which has direct implications for physiotherapy timing and intensity
- Pathophysiology of SIRS Post-Cardiac Surgery
- CPB exposes blood to non-endothelialised circuit surfaces, activating complement, neutrophils, platelets, and the coagulation cascade. This generates a 'whole-body' inflammatory state.
- Cytokine surge peaks within 2–4 hours post-CPB and can persist for 24–72 hours. Consequences include capillary leak, oedema, myocardial depression, and multi-organ dysfunction in severe cases.
- Reperfusion injury: restoration of coronary blood flow after ischaemia generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), contributing to myocardial stunning, endothelial damage, and systemic inflammation.
- Coagulopathy (clotting issues or DVT) is near-universal post-CPB - heparinisation, haemodilution, and platelet dysfunction all contribute. Chest drain losses must be closely monitored.

## Slide 7

- Physiotherapy Implications
| SIRS Feature | Physiotherapy Consideration |
| Myocardial stunning - reduced cardiac output | Strict haemodynamic monitoring during any activity; low-intensity early mobilisation; avoid isometric exercises |
| Capillary leak / fluid shifts - third-spacing | Significant oedema may impair range of motion and comfort; respiratory muscle function may be compromised |
| Coagulopathy - drain losses | Confirm coagulation status before invasive physiotherapy (e.g., manual techniques over wound sites); ensure drain patency. What is their INR levels |
| Fever / pyrexia | Pyrexia increases metabolic demand - increased VO2 and VCO2. High fever (>38.5°C) should prompt medical review before active exercise |
| Delirium / reduced consciousness | SIRS contributes to cerebral hypoperfusion and ICU delirium. Assess cognitive status before exercise; prioritise orientation and communication |
| Pulmonary inflammation - increased alveolar permeability | ARDS risk in severe cases. Monitor for worsening P:F ratio, CXR changes. Early positioning (semi-recumbent) and secretion management remain priorities |
- The inflammatory response typically resolves by 48–72 hours post-operatively in uncomplicated cases. This is the key window where physiotherapy should shift from low-intensity supportive care (positioning, passive exercises, breathing techniques) to active mobilisation.
- Progressing too early (within the first 12–24 hours in a haemodynamically unstable patient) risks cardiovascular compromise; delaying beyond 48–72 hours risks deconditioning, atelectasis, and prolonged ICU admission.

## Slide 8

- Atelectasis
- Partial or complete collapse of the alveoli
- Most common post-operative pulmonary complication, occurring in up to 70–80% of cardiac surgical patients to some degree.
- Mechanism: airway closure below FRC (due to anaesthesia, pain, diaphragmatic dysfunction, and increased closing volume), secretion plugging, and compression from pleural effusion.
- Radiographic findings: basal linear opacities, elevated hemidiaphragm, mediastinal shift towards the collapse.
- Physiotherapy is the primary treatment:
- ACBT, incentive spirometry, positioning (sitting upright, lateral positioning towards the unaffected lung), and supported coughing.
- Severe lobar collapse may require suction if secretion plugging is severe - this is a medical decision, but the physiotherapist's assessment is critical in identifying those who are failing to respond.

## Slide 9

- Pleural Effusion
- An abnormal build-up of fluid between your lungs and chest wall (Pleura)
- Near-universal post-cardiac surgery, particularly left-sided following CABG (from pleural breach and pericardial fluid).
- Small effusions resolve spontaneously and do not require physiotherapy modification. Significant effusions (compromising lung expansion, producing dyspnoea, or causing mediastinal shift) require drainage.
- Physiotherapy role:
- Positioning to optimise drainage and ventilation to non-effused areas; avoid placing the patient in side-lying with the effusion-side down if it causes significant hypoxaemia.
- Post-thoracentesis or drain insertion: monitor for pneumothorax; re-assess breath sounds and encourage deep breathing to re-expand the lung.
- Liaise with medical team to confirm appropriate plans for pleural effusion

## Slide 10

- Atrial Fibrillation (AF)
- Rapid irregular heart beat
- The most common arrhythmia post-cardiac surgery, occurring in 20–40% of CABG patients and up to 60% of valve surgery patients. Peak incidence is Day 2–3 post-operatively.
- Mechanism: surgical trauma to atrial tissue, pericardial inflammation, autonomic imbalance, electrolyte disturbances (hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia), and adrenergic surge all contribute.
- Physiotherapy implications:
- New AF requires prompt medical notification. Assess rate ventricular rates >110 bpm at rest significantly reduce cardiac output and exercise tolerance. Do not commence or continue active mobilisation during uncontrolled rapid AF.
- Haemodynamically stable AF (controlled rate, no hypotension) does not necessarily preclude mobilisation -  discuss with the medical team and use close monitoring.
- AF increases stroke risk significantly: ensure anticoagulation is commenced or confirmed before any vigorous activity.

## Slide 11

- Hospital Acquired Pneumonia
- Pneumonia after surgery (post-operative pneumonia) is a serious, often hospital-acquired infection occurring when bacteria infects the lungs, frequently due to reduced breathing, immobility, or intubation
- Key Causes and Risk Factors
- Anesthesia/Sedation: Affects the ability to breathe deeply and clear mucus from the lungs.
- Immobility: Bed rest leads to fluid accumulation in the lungs.
- Intubation: Ventilation during surgery raises the risk of bacteria entering the lungs.
- Pain: Pain from the surgical site may limit deep breathing or coughing.
- Prevention
- Incentive Spirometry: Using a device to encourage deep breathing.
- Early Movement: Walking or moving around as soon as permitted.
- Deep Breathing/Coughing Exercises: Taking deep breaths and coughing frequently (using a pillow to support the abdomen, known as splinting).
- Positioning: improved FRC and therefore mucociliary clearance

## Slide 12

- Deep Vein Thrombosis:
- A blood clot that forms in a deep vein, usually the leg or pelvis. If left untreated can lead to seriously life-threatening pulmonary embolism (PE)
- Symptoms to Watch For:
- Pain, swelling, or tenderness in one leg (usually the calf).
- Redness or discolouration of the skin.
- Veins that are hard or warm to the touch.
- Chest pain, breathlessness, or collapse, seek emergency care immediately
- Preventive Measures
- Early Mobilization: Getting up and walking as soon as possible after surgery.
- Ankle Pumps: ankles up and down, mobilising
- Compression Stockings: Elastic stockings to improve circulation.
- Medication: Blood thinners (e.g., heparin) to prevent clotting.
- Mechanical Devices: Devices that squeeze the legs to encourage blood flow.
- Hydration: Maintaining good fluid intake.
