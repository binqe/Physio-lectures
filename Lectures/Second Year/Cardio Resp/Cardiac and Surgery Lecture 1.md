- Common Heart Surgeries
- Coronary artery Bypass Grafting (CABG):
- Diverts blood around blocked arteries.

- open blocked or narrowed heart surgeries (Coronary artery disease) to improve blood flow

## Slide 6

- Procedure Types
- Open Heart Surgery
- Traditional Method. Involves sternotomy/ mini- sternotomy and incision is made through your sternum
- 2. Minimally invasive Heart Surgery/ Robotic Surgery:
- Small incisions between the ribs of the side of the chest
- 3. Transcatheter Heart Surgery
- A catheter is inserted through a small incision, usually in the groin (femoral artery), and guided to the heart.

## Slide 7

- Post-Surgical Rehabilitation
- Cardiac Surgery Rehabilitation Pathways
- Phase 1
- Inpatient
- Days 1–7
- A pre-op prior to surgery to prepare. Confirming HPC, PMHx, Shx, Mobility etc.
- ICU: Initial Hx, Respiratory Ax, Bloods, Chest PT, ACBT, SOEOB / SOOBIC, passive AROM pain management
- Cardiac Ward:
- Day 1–2: Neck, shoulder and trunk exs, SOOBIC, ACBT, sternal precautions, mobilise
- Day 3–5: Ongoing chest PT, progress mobility
- Day 5–7: Stairs assessment & discharge education
- Phase 2
- Outpatient
- Weeks 6-8
- Commences 6-8 weeks post-discharge
- Structured exercise program: Aerobic ex: 50–85% HRmax or Borg 11–14
- UL exercise program from weeks 6–8 (sternal healing)
- Risk factor modification + psychosocial support
- Phase 3
- Maintenance
- Months 3–12+
- Community or home exercise programme
- Sustained participation ↓ re-hospitalisation
- Annual cardiology review
- Ongoing PT for deconditioning or complications

## Slide 8

- The precautions apply for:
- 8 weeks for a full sternotomy.
- 4-6 weeks for a mini-sternotomy.
- You may push or pull yourself up using both hands together, putting equal weight and stress through each arm.
- No lifting anything heavier than approximately 5kg for the first 4-6 weeks after surgery (all heart surgeries).
- Light housework and light gardening only from 6 weeks. Mowing the lawn, digging or hoovering can be resumed 12 weeks post-surgery.
- No smoking: impedes healing and increases risk of infection
- Sternal Precautions:
- Walking Advice:
- Aim to build up to longer walks of 30-45 minutes, 5 days a week within 4-6 weeks. Remember to always incorporate a warm up and cool down
- Start with walking on level ground, building up to walks on inclines after a few weeks.
- Walking and talking pace is the goal
- GOAL: 30 minute walks, 5 times a week, within 1 month after  surgery.

## Slide 9

- Lung transplant
- A lung transplant is a surgical procedure for end-stage respiratory failure, replacing damaged lungs with healthy donor lungs
- Procedure: A surgeon makes a large incision—either a "clamshell" incision (across the chest) or on the side (thoracotomy)—to access the lungs.

## Slide 10

- Lung Transplantation Rehabilitation Pathway
- Rehabilitation Pathway
- Inpatient
- ICU (1-7 days)→Ward
- (2-3 weeks)
- More consistent and intensive PT respiratory management and assessment
- Chest PT, SOEOB, mobility (avoid excessive forced exhalation)
- Denervated lung = no distal cough reflex (ACBT important)
- Passive → active limb exercises within 24–48h
- Early Outpatient
- (1–6 months)
- Pulmonary rehab from 4–8 weeks post-transplant
- 6MWT as key outcome measure
- Aerobic: start at Borg 10–12, progress incrementally
- Long-term
- (6 months+)
- Community or home exercise maintenance
- Osteoporosis prevention (steroids → bone loss)
- Monitor for Chronic Lung Allograft Dysfunction (CLAD) : declining FEV1 = urgent review
- Key Transplant Precautions
- Infection prevention
- Strict hand hygiene, PPE for all chest physio, avoid shared gym spaces
- Rejection
- Declining FEV1 >10%, fever, malaise → suspend exercise, urgent review
- Osteoporosis
- Avoid high-impact loading; weight-bearing exercise + DEXA surveillance caused by immunosuppressants
- - Similar to cardiac.
- - However, unlike heart transplants, which monitor for coronary vasculopathy, lung care prioritizes airway health and rejection.
- - Focus is on pulmonary management.
- - Immunosuppressants for the remainder of their life .
- - Activity Restrictions: For the first 6–8 weeks, patients must avoid pushing, pulling, or lifting more than 5kg
- Monitoring
- Frequent follow-ups include blood tests, chest X-rays, and pulmonary function tests to monitor for signs of acute or chronic rejection.

## Slide 11

- Cardiovascular instability
- Resting HR >100 bpm with haemodynamic compromise, or new/uncontrolled arrhythmia (e.g. rapid AF, sustained VT, new heart block)
- Systolic BP <90 mmHg or MAP <65 mmHg not responsive to fluid or vasopressor titration
- New or evolving myocardial ischaemia (ECG changes, rising troponin, chest pain)
- Active haemorrhage or coagulopathy
- Chest drain output >200 mL/hour (active surgical bleeding)
- INR or coagulation profile indicating uncontrolled coagulopathy - particularly relevant in the early post-CPB period
- Respiratory failure
- SpO2 <88% on current oxygen therapy despite optimisation, or FiO2 requirement >0.6 at rest
- Acute tension pneumothorax or large untreated pneumothorax
- Cardiac surgical specifics
- Sternal instability  confirmed or strongly suspected (palpable click, paradoxical movement): all active physiotherapy halted until surgical review
- Tamponade - rising drain output followed by sudden cessation, hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds (Beck's triad): immediate surgical emergency
- Systemic
- Uncontrolled pyrexia >38.5°C with haemodynamic compromise (simple post-operative fever without instability is a relative precaution, not absolute)
- Severe uncorrected electrolyte disturbance - particularly hypokalaemia <3.0 mmol/L or hypomagnesaemia (arrhythmia risk with exertion)
- Altered consciousness or acute agitation precluding safe participation and command-following
- Vs                 Lung Transplant Contraindications
- Lung specific Contraindications
- - Active, uncontrolled infection (bacterial, viral - particularly CMV, fungal - Aspergillus) - exercise during acute infection significantly increases rejection risk and systemic compromise.
- - Acute rejection episode (confirmed or suspected) -  evidenced by declining FEV1 (>10% from personal best), fever, malaise, radiographic infiltrates.
- - Haemodynamic instability: resting HR >120 bpm, systolic BP <90 or >180 mmHg, new or worsening arrhythmia.
- - Active haemorrhage, significant anaemia (Hb <70 g/L), or uncontrolled coagulopathy.
- - Severe electrolyte disturbance (especially hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia)

## Slide 12

- Relative Precautions for transplants:
- Osteopenia/Osteoporosis (universal in long-term steroid/immunosuppressents users): avoid high-impact loading, contact activities, or exercises with significant fall risk. Tailor resistance training to bone health.
- Skin fragility and wound healing impairment: monitor incision sites carefully; avoid prolonged pressure or friction.
- Denervated heart (cardiac transplant): heart rate response to exercise is blunted and delayed — do not use HR alone for exercise intensity guidance. Use Borg RPE scale (12–14 target) or power output/speed as primary guides.
- Absent cough reflex distal to bronchial anastomosis (lung transplant): patients cannot sense or clear secretions in transplanted segments normally — proactive secretion management protocols are essential.
- Calcineurin inhibitor neurotoxicity: tremor, peripheral neuropathy, and rarely encephalopathy can affect exercise performance and safety.
- Immunosuppression-induced diabetes (NODAT — New Onset Diabetes After Transplant): monitor blood glucose around exercise; hypoglycaemia risk with sulphonylureas.
