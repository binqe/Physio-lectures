WHAT IS CHRONIC PAIN?
2026
CHRONIC PAIN
3
•
Chronic Pain - Pain that persists or recurs for >3 months. It outlasts the normal healing time for tissues.
•
Chronic pain is not a "symptom" of a broken part; it is a malfunction of the nervous system itself.
•
Nociplastic Pain – Pain that arises from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of actual or 
threatened tissue damage.
•
Functional Neurological Disorder (FND)- A condition where the brain sends/receives signals incorrectly (e.g., 
functional tremors, paralysis, or seizures) despite the "hardware" (nerves/muscles) being intact.
•
Both Chronic Pain and FND are "Software" problems. The brain’s predictive processing is "glitched," creating 
a real physical experience (pain or movement loss) without a structural "break."
•
In both cases, the nervous system has become hyper-vigilant. It is reacting to "threats" that aren't actually 
there, similar to a car alarm that goes off when a leaf touches the windshield.
•
While both are 'software' issues, they are classified by their primary output: Nociplastic Pain is a disorder of 
sensation (how the brain feels), whereas FND is a disorder of function and control (how the brain moves or 
regulates the body). In modern coding (ICD-11), Chronic Pain is now recognized as a primary disease entity, 
while FND remains classified under neurological/psychobiological processing disorders.
•
A patient can have Chronic Low Back Pain (the location) caused by Nociplastic mechanisms (the "how"), 
presenting with FND symptoms (the "expression").

HOW MIGHT PATIENTS PRESENT?
2026
CHRONIC PAIN
8
•
1. Secondary Hyperalgesia: 
•
The patient reports pain in a much larger area than the original injury.
•
Pain spreads to areas that weren't even injured (The "Volume" is so loud it leaks into neighboring nerves).
•
If a patient had a right-sided L5 disc issue but now has pain across both hips and up into their mid-back, you might 
be seeing Secondary Hyperalgesia.
•
2. Cognitive-Emotional Link: 
•
The patient becomes anxious or depressed or "catastrophizing" (the feeling that the pain is a disaster that will never 
end).
•
This isn't "just a reaction" to pain—it's part of the Pillar 3 rewiring. The brain’s emotion centers and pain centers are 
now physically connected.
•
3. Cortical Map Degradation
•
The patient may have weakness, "giving way" of limbs, tremors, or a loss of coordination that doesn't match nerve 
distribution.
•
The brain becomes so focused on protection that it forgets how to move the area normally, leading to the functional 
tremors or weakness seen in FND.

## Page 35

NOCIPLASTIC PAIN
Nociplastic pain as part of a continuum.  
•
Nociplastic pain as a unique category devoid of actual or threatened tissue or somatosensory 
damage, there is evidence for the overlap of nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic pain, indicating 
that nociplastic pain is not a distinct entity, but part of a chronic pain continuum.
•
The biopsychosocial model is particularly relevant for nociplastic pain disorders. Although often 
sought out, a causal factor is usually not evident. Common predisposing factors include a family 
history of pain, a history of pain experiences, and psychosocial factors such as psychological, 
emotional, sexual, or physical abuse, or a combination of these.
•
We used to think of Nociceptive (tissue), Neuropathic (nerve), and Nociplastic (brain) pain as three 
separate islands. Most chronic pain patients live on a continuum. A patient might start with a 
nociceptive injury (a disc bulge), develop neuropathic features (sciatica), and eventually transition 
into a dominant Nociplastic state (central sensitization).

The theory is that, when we massage or articulate an area, we are activating large myelinated fibers that cause 
a dampening effect on the neurological C- pain fibres.
This changes the pain threshold of the nervous system and a ‘closing’ of the gating system. 
Another theoretical part of the pain gate theory is the descending pathway inhibition via the periaqueductal 
grey (PAG). This suggests that stimulation of the periaqueductal grey matter of the midbrain causes a response 
in the descending pathways to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord that stimulates inhibiting interconnecting 
neurons situated in the substantia gelatinosa. 
References: Bialosky, J.E., Beneciuk, J.M., Bishop, M.D., Coronado, R.A., Penza, C.W., Simon, C.B. and George, S.Z., 2018. Unraveling the mechanisms of manual therapy: 
modeling an approach. journal of orthopaedic & sports physical therapy, 48(1), pp.8-18.

EFFECTS OF MANUAL THERAPY
2026
CHRONIC PAIN

Manual therapy is a sensory input used to create a neurological output. It modulates pain via three primary mechanisms:
1.
The Spinal Level (Gate Control)
A.
MT activates large-diameter, myelinated A-beta fibers (mechanoreceptors).
B.
These fibers provide excitatory input to inhibitory interneurons in the substantia gelatinosa.
C.
This "closes the gate," preventing nociceptive C-fiber signals from reaching the second-order projection neurons.
2.
The Supraspinal Level (The Top-Down Brakes)
A.
The rhythmic and novel sensory input of MT is processed by the brain as "safe.”
B.
This activates the Periaqueductal Gray (PAG) and the Rostroventral Medulla (RVM).
C.
Triggers the Descending Inhibitory Pathway, releasing endogenous opioids and serotonin to dampen systemic pain 
sensitivity.
3.
 The Peripheral Level (Chemical Washout)
A.
Physical movement of the tissues and improved circulation.
B.
Reduces the concentration of inflammatory mediators (the "chemical soup") around the nerve endings.
C.
Decreases Peripheral Sensitization, making the "alarm" (nociceptors) less twitchy and harder to trigger.
4.
*BONUS* The Psychological Level (Placebo & Context)
A.
The therapeutic alliance and the "human touch.”
B.
Positive expectations and a reduction in threat perception.
C.
Modulates the Neuromatrix, reducing the brain’s overall "danger" evaluation.

EVIDENCE BASED STRATEGIES FOR CHRONIC PAIN
2026
CHRONIC PAIN
48
Since chronic pain is a multisystem "software" glitch, our treatment must address the biological, psychological, and social 
drivers simultaneously.
1.
Pain Neuroscience Education 
•
Hurt does not equal Harm (The Tissue-Brain Gap):
•
Educate patients that pain is a protective output from the brain, not a reliable measure of tissue damage. Just 
because it feels "worse" doesn't mean the tissues are tearing further. Understanding this reduces the "threat value" 
and allows the brain to turn down the descending "gas."
•
The Over-Protective Alarm (Sensitization):
•
Explain that in chronic pain, the nervous system has become hyper-vigilant. Much like a home security system that 
goes off when a leaf touches the window, their nerves have lowered their firing threshold. The goal of therapy is to 
"re-train" the alarm to be less twitchy, not just to fix a "broken" part.
•
"Motion is Lotion" (Graded Exposure):
•
Help patients move through the "Edge of Discomfort" without fear. If the patient understands that the pain is a 
"false alarm" from a sensitized system, they can engage in Graded Exposure. This proves to the brain that 
movement is safe, which eventually triggers neuroplastic changes to "un-smudge" the brain's body maps.
•
The Bio-Plastic Brain (Hope for Change):
•
Emphasize that the nervous system is malleable. Just as it "learned" to be in pain through neuroplasticity (The 3 
Pillars), it can "un-learn" pain through new experiences, better sleep, stress management, and safe movement. The 
system is not "stuck"; it is simply in a state of temporary adaptation.
•
A great reference: Moseley, G. L., & Butler, D. S. (2017). Explain Pain Supercharged. Noigroup Publications.

EVIDENCE BASED STRATEGIES FOR FUNCTIONAL 
NEUROLOGIAL DISORDER (FND)
2026
CHRONIC PAIN
51
Because FND is a disorder of voluntary control and agency (the brain is "glitching" the movement), the goal of 
treatment shifts from "desensitization" to "retraining the map.”
1. Pain Neuroscience Education  → Education on FND
1. Instead of focusing on why it hurts, you focus on the "Software vs. Hardware" analogy.
2. The most evidence-based first step for FND is clear communication of the diagnosis. The patient must 
understand that their nerves are intact (Hardware), but the brain’s signaling is blocked or hijacked by a 
"functional" loop (Software).
2. Graded Exposure & Exercise → Redirecting Attention
1. In FND, the brain is "over-monitoring" the limb. If an FND patient focuses too hard on moving their leg, the 
tremor or weakness often gets worse.
2. We use distraction or external focus of attention.
3. Cognitive Functional Therapy (CFT) & CBT
The current "best" practice for FND (based on the Consensus Recommendations by Nielsen et al., 2015) is Physiotherapy based 
on the retraining of movement through distraction.
We use movements that the patient can do (like walking backward or side-stepping) to show the brain it is still capable of 
movement. This breaks the "learned" pattern of dysfunction.
