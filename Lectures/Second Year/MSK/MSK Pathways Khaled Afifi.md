## Slide 10

- MSK triage:
- The goal of the diagnostic triage for MSK patients  is to exclude non-MSK causes of pain. A focused history and a physical examination of the patient form the cornerstone to the diagnostic triage classification. Part of the consultation should be used to gauge the patient’s understanding of their pain (ICE) (Bardin et al., 2017).
- Triage systems are implemented to reduce waiting times and ensure rapid access to effective assessment, advice and treatment. As a result, the effects of triage on waiting list time , satisfaction, diagnostic agreement , appropriateness of referral , clinical measures  and cost  have been evaluated (Joseph et al., 2014).

## Slide 11

- MSK triage (continued):
- Evidence suggests that advanced practice physiotherapists (APPs) demonstrate good to very good agreement with physicians in both diagnosis and surgical triage of musculoskeletal disorders. Moreover, patients managed within an APP model of care report clinical outcomes—such as reductions in pain and disability—that are comparable to, or in some cases better than, those achieved with usual medical care (Lafrance et al., 2023).

## Slide 15

- Red flags:
- "positive answers to key questions about medical history, that indicate the possibility of a serious underlying condition" (Underwood & Buchbinder 2013).
- Not intended to be clinical decision-making tools in their own right
- Inadequate evidence to support the use of red flags as a screening tool to decide whether or not further investigations are needed
- Need a better understanding about which red flags are predictive of serious pathology in order to reduce unnecessary investigations
- Single red flags are not diagnostic, clusters of red flags may have better prognostic utility and should be used in context of a thorough subjective patient history and physical examination
- Need to think in terms of how a serious pathology may present clinically, and which red flags would be relevant for that specific pathology

## Slide 19

- clinical practice pathways (CPWs):
- structured multidisciplinary care plans which detail essential steps in the care of patients with a specific clinical problem (Rotter 2010). They support the translation of clinical guidelines into local protocols and clinical practice (Campbell 1998). Whilst clinical guidelines provide generic recommendations, CPWs detail the local structures, systems, and time frames to address these recommendations.
- CPWs have been proposed as a strategy to optimize resource allocation in a climate of rising healthcare costs, as they may improve the efficiency with which healthcare is delivered

## Slide 20

- Benefits of clinical practice pathways (CPWs):
- Stand-alone CPWs are likely to reduce in hospital complications (moderate certainty) but it is very uncertain if they make any difference to hospital readmissions (up to 6 months) (very low certainty). Stand-alone CPWs are likely to reduce length of hospital stay (moderate certainty). Costs and charges were generally lower in CPWs in nine out of ten studies included in this comparison (very low certainty). Stand-alone CPWs may also slightly increase adherence to recommended practice (low certainty) (Rotter et al., 2025).

## Slide 22

- Musculoskeletal: Osteoarthritis (OA) clinical guidance- hip and knee within primary and community care
- Red Flags
| Initial presentation in primary care | Confirmation of diagnosis/initiation of treatment in primary and community care | Conservative management in primary and community care | Initial presentation for clinical triage and assessment by integrated tier 2 MSK service/SPOA | Review in primary and community care | MSK Specialist review | Surgical review and assessment in secondary care |
- Consider alternative diagnosis if features do not fit OA diagnosis:
- Atypical features such as a history of trauma, prolonged morning joint-related stiffness, rapid worsening of symptoms, presence of a hot swollen joint may indicate alternative or additional diagnoses.
- Differential diagnoses includes gout, other inflammatory arthritides, septic arthritis and malignancy (bone pain).
- 1st presentation in primary care:
- Over 45, activity related joint pain, No morning joint-related stiffness >30 mins, impacts on daily activities and quality of life
- When to refer
- Imaging is not indicated at this stage unless symptoms are unusually severe.  Consider using iRefer
- Engage with wider primary care team such as care coordinator, clinical pharmacist, health and wellbeing coach and social prescriber as needed
- Consider paracetamol, topical NSAIDs, or if unsuccessful, NSAIDs and highly selective COX-2 inhibitors with PPI
- Consider addition of opioids  if  the above unsuccessful
- Consider Intra articular corticosteroid injections  as an adjunct to core treatments for the relief of moderate to severe pain
- Holistic assessment including mental health, social and work status, sleep, weight, comorbidities, pain and function and collection of PROM and PREM data
- Clinical assessment and screening for serious pathologies. Exclude red flags
- Clinical triage at SPOA
- MDT management for complex cases
- Conservative non-surgical management for period of up to 3 months
- Review investigations and progress
- Trauma & Orthopaedic, rheumatology, spine or pain referral with full patient medical history
- SPOA/Tier 2 MSK service referral
- Refer to pain management/Combined Physical and Psychological Programme (CPPP) / MDT as appropriate
- Consider referral to primary/community care and 3rd sector/voluntary sector for ongoing management rehabilitation and support
- Timing of regular reviews for people with OA based on:
- severe pain, more than one joint affected, comorbidities, on regular medication for pain
- Optimise patient for surgery and manage comorbidities:
- Consider prehab referral, psychological support, pain management
- Evidenced based investigations
- Refer for injection/surgery if indicated
- Refer for further opinion if indicated
- Refer for further conservative treatment if indicated
- Discharge if indicated
- Shared decision making should occur at each decision point across the pathway
- Self referral
- Primary Care:
- GP, MSK Specialist Doctor, FCP
- ED/111
- Other non-MSK/ MSK specialist HCP
- Link to GIRFT pathways, NICE guidance and best practice resources
- Refer back to primary/community as needed
- Consider Patient Initiated Follow Up (PIFU)
- Consider onward referral to other specialities if symptoms not MSK in origin
- Key:
- Decision points
- As part of shared decision making conversation:
- Discuss options for onward referral. Consider use of decision support tools.
- Discuss referral/self referral for conservative treatment /physiotherapy if not improving. If the patient is ready, consider a trial of appropriate conservative treatment, including strengthening and aerobic exercise, but consider referral if symptoms worsening despite treatment
- Offer tailored written and verbal advice on the following core treatments to all people with clinical osteoarthritis:
- Assess patient’s knowledge, skills and confidence: if low consider signpost/referral to health and well-being coach
- Deliver consistent and concise health and wellbeing information and encourage patients to engage in conversations about  their health
- Access to quality self-management education and appropriate information to enable self-management
- Tailored advice about how to: stay active, how to achieve weight loss if the person is overweight or obese, advice on smoking and alcohol consumption, use of appropriate footwear
- Consider directing social prescriber to access voluntary/third sector, quality self-management resources, emotional/mental health wellbeing resources
- Shared decision making conversations (including holistic assessment, understanding what matters to the person, discussion of appropriate treatment options and associated harms, benefits and consequences, values and preference) to take place at each decision point on the pathway, using relevant in consultation decision support  tools. Discuss options for onward referral.

## Slide 29

- Personalised care:
- Personalised care means people have choice and control over the way their care is planned and delivered. It is based on ‘what matters’ to them and their individual strengths and needs.
- This happens within a system that makes the most of the expertise, capacity and potential of people, families and communities in delivering better outcomes and experiences.

## Slide 30

- Shared Decision Making:
- is a collaborative process that involves the patient/carer and their healthcare professional working together to reach a joint decision about care. To achieve this the patient/carer/family member needs to understand the risks, benefits and any consequences of the care management options available.

## Slide 31
