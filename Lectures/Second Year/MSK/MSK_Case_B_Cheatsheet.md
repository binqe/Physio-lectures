Case B
Mr. O'Shea — 72-Year-Old Retired Librarian | Working Diagnosis: Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fracture (T7–T8)
Target: 70%+ | Musculoskeletal (MSK) Physiotherapy
I PROBLEM LIST
• Mid-thoracic pain VAS 4/10 — worse with prolonged
standing/loading
• Progressive thoracic kyphosis (vertebral body wedging)
• Tenderness and percussion pain at T7–T8 (fracture site)
• Height loss ("shrinking") — loss of vertebral body height
• Likely reduced thoracic and hip extension ROM
• Significant secondary osteoporosis (corticosteroid-induced)
• Likely reduced back extensor and core muscle strength
• Inability to stand for prolonged periods (shopping, standing at
kitchen)
• Difficulty with activities requiring thoracic loading (lifting, carrying)
• Possible difficulty with dressing (bending forward)
• Reduced walking tolerance and exercise capacity
• Potential social isolation secondary to pain and reduced mobility
I RED FLAGS
• Bilateral lower limb weakness, paraesthesia, gait disturbance, or
bladder/bowel dysfunction
• Severe, constant, unremitting back pain — worse lying down,
disturbing sleep
• Fever, rigors, unexplained weight loss (>5 kg in 3 months), night
sweats
• Sudden escalation in pain severity or new neurological symptoms
following assessment
• Absent or decreasing reflexes in lower limbs + upper motor neuron
signs (clonus, Babinski)
I PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
• Osteoporosis is characterised by reduced bone mineral density (BMD) and disrupted trabecular microarchitecture, resulting in decreased bone
strength and increased fracture susceptibility.
• The thoracic vertebrae (T7–T8) are vulnerable sites for osteoporotic VCF because the natural thoracic kyphosis directs axial compressive load
predominantly onto the anterior vertebral body — the region with the least cortical bone support.
• Localised periosteal nerve endings and the sinuvertebral nerve at the fracture site generate deep, aching nociceptive pain, which is exacerbated by
axial loading (standing, extension) and relieved by offloading (lying down).
I DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES
1. 1. Spinal Metastases / Pathological Fracture (Malignancy)
• 72-year-old male — age of peak malignancy incidence
• Mid-thoracic pain is a common site for metastatic deposits (lung, prostate, renal)
• Vertebral body destruction leading to pathological fracture can mimic VCF
2. 2. Scheuermann's Disease / Thoracic Disc Pathology
• Scheuermann's kyphosis presents with progressive thoracic kyphosis and mid-back pain
• Typically younger onset but may be longstanding, undiagnosed, and symptomatic in old age
• Disc degeneration contributes to thoracic pain and kyphosis in older adults

I TREATMENT PLAN
I Initial Phase — Weeks 1–4 (Pain Management, Safety, Stabilisation)
• Pain understanding and pacing: "Your pain is coming from the weakened vertebrae in your mid-back. The good news is that with the right exercises
and activity management, we can reduce your pain, strengthen your back muscles, and prevent further fractures. Movement is important — too much
rest will make your bones and muscles weaker."
• Fall prevention — the most critical long-term message: "Because of your osteoporosis, preventing a fall is our top priority. We will work on your
balance and core strength. Please remove trip hazards at home (rugs, trailing wires), ensure good lighting, use a grab rail in the bathroom, and wear
non-slip footwear. Consider a referral for a home safety assessment."

• Osteoporosis management — GP liaison: "I want to make sure you are on the right medication for your osteoporosis. Long-term steroids significantly
reduce bone density. Are you taking a bisphosphonate (e.g., alendronate) or calcium and Vitamin D? If not, I would recommend we flag this to your GP
as part of your management."
• Posture and spinal hygiene: "Avoid bending and twisting simultaneously — this is the highest risk movement for your spine. Instead: 'hip hinge' to pick
items up (push hips back, keep spine tall). Use a firm chair with good lumbar support. Sleeping on your back or side with a pillow between your knees is
safest."
I Progression Criteria (Before Advancing to Phase 2)
• VAS ≤2/10 during daily activities (from baseline 4/10)
• Able to stand comfortably for ≥15 minutes (from the reported limitation)
• Completing prone extension exercise with 10-second hold × 10 reps pain-free
• TUG score ≤12 seconds (reduced fall risk threshold)
• No deterioration in kyphosis index or neurological screen
I Long-Term Plan — Weeks 4–12 (Function, Fall Prevention & Independence)
• Progressive strengthening: Advance thoracic extensor programme — increase resistance, reps, and functional positions (standing, integrated with
daily activities). Standing resistance band rows and scapular retraction to counteract kyphosis.
• Balance and fall prevention programme: Progress from seated balance exercises → standing on one leg (support available) → tandem stance →
stepping exercises. Consider referral to community falls prevention programme (e.g., Otago Exercise Programme — RCT evidence for fall reduction in
>65s).
• Functional independence training: Practice sit-to-stand technique, safe bending mechanics (hip hinge), lifting technique, safe stair use. Focus on
activities identified in PSFS (e.g., shopping, cooking, dressing).
• Hydrotherapy consideration: Pool-based therapy reduces gravitational load on the spine while allowing progressive strengthening — particularly
useful if pain remains a barrier to land-based exercise at this stage.
• Multidisciplinary team involvement: Liaise with GP regarding bone protection medication optimisation; consider occupational therapy for home
assessment and assistive devices; dietitian review for calcium/vitamin D intake.
