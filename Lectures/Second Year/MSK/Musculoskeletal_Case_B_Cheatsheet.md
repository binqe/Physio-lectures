• Physical Impairments

standing/loading

• Likely reduced thoracic and hip extension ROM
• Likely reduced back extensor and core muscle strength
• Difficulty with activities requiring thoracic loading (lifting, carrying)
• Possible difficulty with dressing (bending forward)
• Reduced walking tolerance and exercise capacity

• Clinical Significance
• Action if Present
• Bilateral lower limb weakness, paraesthesia, gait disturbance, or

from fracture fragment migration. EMERGENCY — can cause

• Severe, constant, unremitting back pain — worse lying down,
disturbing sleep

I PATHOPHYSIOLOGY (KEY POINTS)
• M Structural & Biomechanical Mechanism Osteoporosis is characterised by reduced bone mineral density (BMD) and disrupted trabecular
microarchitecture, resulting in decreased bone strength and increased fracture susceptibility.
• The thoracic vertebrae (T7–T8) are vulnerable sites for osteoporotic VCF because the natural thoracic kyphosis directs axial compressive load
predominantly onto the anterior vertebral body — the region with the least cortical bone support.
• Localised periosteal nerve endings and the sinuvertebral nerve at the fracture site generate deep, aching nociceptive pain, which is exacerbated by
axial loading (standing, extension) and relieved by offloading (lying down).
• Mid-thoracic pain worsening with prolonged standing: Upright posture loads the anterior wedged vertebral body — fracture site becomes compressed,
stimulating periosteal nociceptors.
• Pain on spinous process percussion: Vibration transmitted to the fractured vertebra triggers periosteal nerve activation at the fracture site — highly
specific for bony pathology.
• Progressive kyphosis / "shrinking": Anterior wedge collapse reduces vertebral body height — cumulative across one or more levels results in
measurable height loss and visible kyphotic deformity.

I OBJECTIVE ASSESSMENTS & HOW TO PERFORM
Key Observation Findings
Posture: Increased thoracic kyphosis (flexed posture of thoracic spine) — visible as a "hump" or "dowager's hump." Compensatory forward head posture
and increased lumbar lordosis are common secondary adaptations. Reduced lumbar extension range. Movement: Thoracic extension will be restricted and
painful (loads the fracture site). Thoracic flexion may be less provocative but also limited. Lateral flexion and rotation may be preserved but guarded.
Observe for paraspinal muscle guarding/splinting bilaterally at the T7–T8 level. Gait:
TEST 3
Lower Limb Neurological Screen (Reflexes, Myotomes, Dermatomes) + Babinski Sign What it Measures Thoracic spinal cord integrity — screens for cord
compression (myelopathy) from posterior VCF wall retropulsion or thoracic canal stenosis. How to Perform 1. Lower limb reflexes: Patellar (L3–L4), Achilles
(S1–S2) — compare bilaterally with reflex hammer. 2. Myotome screen: Hip flexion (L1–L2), knee extension (L3), dorsiflexion (L4), great toe extension (L5),
plantarflexion (S1). 3. Dermatome light touch: medial calf (L4), dorsum foot (L5), lateral foot (S1). 4. Babinski sign: Stroke lateral plantar surface of foot from
heel to MTP joints. Upward (extensor) great toe response = positive UMN sign.
