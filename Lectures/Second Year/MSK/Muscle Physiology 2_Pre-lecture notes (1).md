## Slide 5

- Skeletal Muscle
- Striation (sarcomeres)
- Effector of somatic nerve system
- Voluntary contraction
- Neuromuscular junction-specific
- Multi-nuclei (fusion)

**Notes:** Striation (Sarcomeres):
Muscle tissue is composed of repeating units called sarcomeres, which give skeletal muscles their characteristic striped appearance (striation).
Sarcomeres are the functional units of muscles, delimited by Z-discs. They consist of overlapping actin (thin) and myosin (thick) filaments, enabling muscle contraction.
Effector of Somatic Nervous System:
Skeletal muscles are the effectors of the somatic nervous system, which controls voluntary movements. It's under conscious control and responsible for activities like walking, talking, and typing.
Voluntary Contraction:
Contractions of skeletal muscles are typically under conscious control. When you decide to move a limb or perform an action, your brain sends signals via the somatic nervous system to stimulate the relevant muscles.
Neuromuscular Junction-Specific:
The neuromuscular junction is a specialized synapse where a motor neuron communicates with a muscle fiber. It's highly specialized for rapid and efficient transmission of nerve signals to initiate muscle contraction.
Multi-Nuclei (Fusion):
Skeletal muscle fibers are unique in that they are multinucleated, meaning they have multiple nuclei within a single cell. This is a result of the fusion of multiple precursor cells (myoblasts) during development.
This multi-nucleation contributes to the size and strength of skeletal muscles, as each nucleus can control a portion of the muscle fiber.

## Slide 6

- Smooth Muscle
- No striation (filaments not form sarcomeres)
- Effector of autonomic nerve system
- Involuntary contraction
- Varicosites-diffuse
- Gap junctions

**Notes:** No Striation (Filaments not Form Sarcomeres):
Smooth muscle lacks the distinct striations seen in skeletal muscle. Instead of organized sarcomeres, smooth muscle cells have actin and myosin filaments scattered throughout the cell, allowing for a more uniform, smooth appearance.
Effector of Autonomic Nervous System:
Smooth muscles are primarily regulated by the autonomic nervous system, which controls involuntary bodily functions. This includes activities like digestion, blood vessel constriction/dilation, and organ function.
Involuntary Contraction:
Contractions of smooth muscles are largely involuntary and not consciously controlled. They occur as a result of signals from the autonomic nervous system or local stimuli, such as changes in pH or stretching of the muscle tissue.
Varicosities - Diffuse:
The autonomic nervous system communicates with smooth muscle cells through varicosities, which are swollen, bead-like nerve endings. These varicosities release neurotransmitters in a more diffuse manner compared to the precise synaptic cleft seen in neuromuscular junctions of skeletal muscle.
Gap Junctions:
Smooth muscle cells are connected by gap junctions, which are specialized channels that allow for direct electrical and chemical communication between adjacent cells. This allows for coordinated contractions across a large area of smooth muscle tissue.

## Slide 7

- Cardiac Muscle
- Striation (sarcomeres)
- Effector of autonomic nerve system
- Involuntary contraction
- Gap junctions
- Intercalated disks

**Notes:** Striation (Sarcomeres):
Cardiac muscle, like skeletal muscle, also exhibits striations due to the organized arrangement of sarcomeres. Sarcomeres are the repeating units of contraction, composed of overlapping actin and myosin filaments.
Effector of Autonomic Nervous System:
The autonomic nervous system influences cardiac muscle activity. However, unlike smooth muscle, cardiac muscle has an intrinsic rhythm, and the autonomic system modulates rather than initiates the heartbeat. It helps adjust the rate and force of contractions in response to physiological needs.
Involuntary Contraction:
Cardiac muscle contractions are involuntary, meaning they occur without conscious control. The heartbeat is regulated by specialized cardiac conduction system cells that initiate and propagate electrical signals, leading to rhythmic contractions.
Gap Junctions:
Cardiac muscle cells are connected by specialized junctions called gap junctions. These channels allow for rapid electrical communication between adjacent cells, ensuring that the heart contracts as a coordinated unit.
Intercalated Disks:
Intercalated disks are unique structures found in cardiac muscle. They are specialized regions where adjacent cardiac muscle cells physically connect. Intercalated disks contain gap junctions and desmosomes, which provide both electrical continuity and mechanical stability to the tissue.

## Slide 8

- ”On-off switch” – Skeletal and Cardiac
- Interaction of actin and myosin depends on regulatory proteins on the thin filament:
- Tropomyosin – blocks myosin binding sides on actin
- Troponin C – binding site for Ca2+
- Troponin T – binds to tropomyosin
- Troponin I – binds to actin
- Ca2+ binds to troponin-C then a conformational change occurs which moves tropomyosin
- This then revelas the myosin binding site and a cross-bridge can form

**Notes:** Tropomyosin:
Tropomyosin is a long, filamentous protein that lies along the groove of the actin filament. In its resting state, tropomyosin physically blocks the myosin binding sites on the actin filament.
When a muscle is at rest, tropomyosin acts as a regulatory "gatekeeper," preventing myosin heads from attaching to actin.
Troponin C:
Troponin is a complex protein consisting of three subunits: Troponin C, Troponin T, and Troponin I. Troponin C is the subunit that binds to calcium ions (Ca2+).
When calcium levels increase in the sarcoplasm (the fluid inside muscle cells), calcium ions bind to specific sites on Troponin C. This binding triggers a conformational change in the troponin complex.
Troponin T:
Troponin T is the subunit of troponin that binds to tropomyosin. When calcium ions bind to Troponin C, Troponin T undergoes a conformational change, which assists in moving tropomyosin away from the myosin binding sites on actin.
In essence, Troponin T helps facilitate the exposure of myosin binding sites on the actin filament.
Troponin I:
Troponin I is the subunit of troponin that binds to actin. When Troponin I binds to actin, it helps stabilize the position of the troponin-tropomyosin complex.
Additionally, Troponin I plays a crucial role in preventing unwanted muscle contractions by inhibiting the interaction between actin and myosin when calcium levels are low.

## Slide 12

- Twitch contraction

**Notes:** Nervous Stimulation: A twitch occurs when a nerve impulse (action potential) stimulates a motor neuron, which in turn signals a specific muscle fiber to contract.
Phases of a Twitch:
Latent Phase: This is the brief moment between the nerve impulse and the onset of muscle contraction. It represents the time taken for the action potential to travel along the nerve and initiate the muscle response.
Contraction Phase: This is when the muscle fiber actively shortens and generates force.
Relaxation Phase: After the contraction, the muscle fiber returns to its original length, and tension decreases.
Strength and Duration: The strength and duration of a twitch are relatively constant and do not vary greatly under normal physiological conditions.

## Slide 14

- Frequency and Summation

**Notes:** Summation and tetanus are processes by which muscle fibers can produce sustained contractions. 
They occur when muscle fibers receive repeated nerve impulses in a short period of time.

Summation: This occurs when a single motor neuron repeatedly sends action potentials in quick succession to a muscle fiber. If the impulses are frequent enough, the muscle fiber doesn't have enough time to completely relax between contractions. As a result, the force of each contraction adds up, leading to a more sustained, stronger contraction.

Incomplete Tetanus: In this state, the muscle fiber is stimulated at a rapid rate, but there is still a partial relaxation between contractions. The result is a sustained but not entirely steady level of muscle tension.
Complete Tetanus (Fused Tetanus): When the stimulation frequency is so high that the muscle fiber doesn't have time to relax at all between contractions, a complete tetanus occurs. The contractions fuse together, resulting in a sustained, smooth, and maximal level of muscle tension.
Physiological Tetanus: This is a sustained muscle contraction that occurs during normal, everyday activities. It's the result of repeated motor neuron signaling to muscle fibers.
Pathological Tetanus: This is an uncontrolled, sustained muscle contraction often caused by the toxin produced by the bacterium Clostridium tetani. It leads to severe muscle rigidity.

## Slide 23

- Energy Production – Aerobic Pathways
- Krebs cycle (or citric acid cycle) consists of a series of reactions that result in a net production of (per molecule of glucose):
- 2 x ATP ( = 2 ATP from glycolysis) = 4 ATP
- 6 x NADH (+ 4 NADH from glycolysis) = 10 NADHs ≈ 30 ATP
- 2 x FADH2 ≈ 4 ATP
- 38 ATPs produced from complete oxidation of glucose (Krebs and Electron Transport Chain)

**Notes:** nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is involved in the electron transport chain, a series of chemical reactions that occur within the mitochondria of cells. During the process of cellular respiration, NADH donates high-energy electrons to the electron transport chain, which in turn produces ATP.

flavin adenine dinucleotide is a molecule that accepts electrons during various metabolic reactions. When FAD accepts two electrons and two protons (H+ ions), it becomes reduced to FADH2.
