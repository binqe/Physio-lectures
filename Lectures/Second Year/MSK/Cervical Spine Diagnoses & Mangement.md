## Slide 4

- Example: 7. Non-MSK Neck Pain (Slide 1)
- 1. Meningitis
- Subjective: Severe headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, fever, nausea, rapid onset of systemic illness.
- Assessment: Vital signs, neurological screen, Kernig/Brudzinski signs (if trained), check for rash, urgent medical referral.
- Treatment: Immediate emergency referral — this is a medical emergency.
- 2. Cervical Arterial Dysfunction (e.g., Vertebral Artery Issues)
- Subjective: Dizziness, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, drop attacks, nausea, nystagmus, unilateral neck pain or headache.
- Assessment: Vascular risk factor screening, cranial nerve exam, blood pressure, neurological screen. Avoid provocative testing.
- Treatment: Urgent medical referral; avoid cervical manipulation or end‑range movements.

**Notes:** Menigitis - van de Beek, D., de Gans, J., Tunkel, A. R., & Wijdicks, E. F. (2006). Community-acquired bacterial meningitis in adults. New England Journal of Medicine, 354(1), 44–53. 

Cerebral Arterial Dyfunction - Arnold, M., & Bousser, M. G. (2005). Cervical artery dissections: Predisposing factors, diagnosis, and outcome. The Lancet Neurology, 4(9), 668–678.

## Slide 5

- Example: 7. Non-MSK Neck Pain (Slide 2)
- 3. Tumour / Metastases
- Subjective: Unexplained weight loss, night pain, constant non‑mechanical pain, history of cancer, fatigue.
- Assessment: Full subjective red‑flag screen, palpation for masses, neurological exam.
- Treatment: Medical referral for imaging and oncology input.
- 4. Infection (Discitis, Osteomyelitis)
- Subjective: Severe constant pain, fever, recent infection, immunosuppression, IV drug use, night sweats.
- Assessment: Temperature, red‑flag questioning, neurological screen, palpation for local heat/swelling.
- Treatment: Urgent medical referral for blood tests, imaging, and antibiotics.

**Notes:** 3. Tumor - Henschke, N., Maher, C. G., Refshauge, K. M., et al. (2009). Red flags to screen for malignancy in patients with low‑back pain: Systematic review. BMJ, 339, b3365. 

4. Infection - Cottle, L., & Riordan, T. (2008). Infectious spondylodiscitis. Journal of Infection, 56(6), 401–412.

## Slide 8

- 3. Mechanical/Postural pain
- Subjective symptoms:
- Pain- dull, aching
- Stiffness- affect range of motion
- Tension headaches
- Poor posture
- Objective details:
- McKenzie method special test
- Forward head, kyphosis in observation
- Tenderness in palpation
- Reduced and pain at end of range of motion
- Weakness in muscle strength test
- Best treatments:
- Patient education on ergonomics and posture- eg take breaks from sitting down
- Strengthening exercises for neck and shoulder
- Manual therapy- active range of motion, stretches
- Expected timeline for healing:
- Few weeks- 6-8 weeks

## Slide 12

- 6. Inflammatory / Systemic
- Subjective Symptoms: (Red flags...)1 – Constant/unrelenting pain
- 2- Fever & chills, night sweats
- 3- Unexplained weight loss 4- Persistent fatigue Objective Symptoms: 1- Reduced ROM
- 2 – Neuro deficits: Myelopathy – gait instability, hand clumsiness, spasticity. Radiculopathy – Reduced reflexes, muscle weakness 3- Raised inflammatory markers (blood testing)
- Best treatment: 1- Physical therapy – isometrics (resisted lateral flexion) , stretching (lev scap stretch),  Mobility (chin tucks, rotations) 2- Postural Adjustment – education on postural alignment
- 3- Traction4- Heat and cold therapy – reduces stiffness and pain5- DMARDs (medical) aggressive tx – suppress overactive immune response Timeline:
- Acute – can see improvements in 1-7 days Subacute – 2-12 weeks
- Long-term recovery – 3-12 months
- Rheumatoid Arthritis > can lead to atlanto-axial joint subluxation ​Ankylosing Spondylitis > fusion and stiffening of cervical vertebrae
