Hoffmann's negative
Q1: Focused Subjective Assessment, Red Flags & Prioritised Problem List
Opening Statement (speak this aloud):
"Mrs. Gupta, I'd like to start by asking you some questions about your neck pain so I can
understand how it affects you day-to-day, identify anything that requires urgent attention, and then
set priorities for today's assessment and treatment."
Subjective Assessment — Key Areas to Cover
Chief Complaint & Body Chart: Dull, aching neck pain — ask the patient to point to the area.
Determine whether pain is local (cervical), referred (into trapezius, interscapular region), or
radiating distally into the arm (dermatomal pattern suggesting radiculopathy). Mrs. Gupta is a
graphic designer — prolonged static postures at a workstation are highly relevant.
•
Onset & Duration: 1-year history — likely a chronic/recurrent mechanical condition. Ask about
precipitating events: new desk setup, increased workload, ergonomic changes. Gradual onset
supports postural/degenerative aetiology.
•
Pain Characteristics: Dull quality suggests nociceptive mechanical pain. Rate severity on
NRS (0–10). Assess irritability: how easily provoked, how long to settle. Low irritability allows
fuller objective examination; high irritability requires a modified approach.
•
24-Hour Behaviour: Morning stiffness < 30 minutes suggests mechanical rather than
inflammatory. Aggravated by prolonged screen use, forward-head posture, overhead activity.
Eased by heat, rest, movement — typical of mechanical neck pain. Night pain unrelated to
position warrants red flag screening.
•
Aggravating / Easing Factors: Cervical extension or rotation aggravated → facet or foraminal
involvement. Sustained flexion aggravated → disc / deep cervical flexor fatigue. Eased by
position change → mechanical; constant regardless of position → red flag.
•
Neurological Symptoms: Ask specifically about paraesthesia, numbness, weakness in the
upper limbs — even though Spurling's is negative, radiculopathy cannot be excluded by history
alone. Bladder/bowel changes would indicate cervical cord involvement.
•
Psychosocial Screening (Yellow Flags): Graphic design work may involve work pressure,
repetitive strain concerns, fear-avoidance beliefs. Use STarT MSK or similar tool. Beliefs about
pain ("my neck is damaged") can perpetuate disability.

Primary Diagnosis
Cervicogenic Neck Pain secondary to Upper Crossed Syndrome / Postural Cervical
Dysfunction — mechanical nociceptive pain arising from sustained abnormal loading of cervical
joints, myofascial structures, and intervertebral discs.
Clinical Reasoning:
Differential 1: Cervical Radiculopathy (C5/C6 or C6/C7)
Radiculopathy involves nerve root compression producing dermatomal pain, paraesthesia, and
myotomal weakness. Although Spurling's is negative (sensitivity ~30%, specificity ~93%), it does not
fully exclude radiculopathy. Supporting features would include: unilateral arm pain in a dermatomal
pattern, reduced upper limb reflexes (biceps C5/C6; triceps C7), and positive neurodynamic testing
(Upper Limb Tension Test). Currently less likely given absence of distal symptoms, but must be
formally cleared with objective neurological screen.
Differential 2: Inflammatory Arthritis (e.g., Rheumatoid Arthritis)
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) affects the cervical spine in 30–40% of cases, particularly the atlantoaxial
joint (C1–C2), posing a risk of cord compression. Features that would support RA: morning stiffness
> 1 hour, bilateral joint swelling, extra-articular features (nodules, skin, eye involvement), elevated
ESR/CRP and positive rheumatoid factor. Currently less likely given the mechanical, unilateral,
posture-dependent nature of pain — but inflammatory markers and rheumatological history should
be clarified, particularly before any cervical manual therapy.
Q3: Objective Assessment Techniques & Interpretation
Technique 1: Cervical Active Range of Motion (AROM) with Overpressure
1-year history consistent with a chronic mechanical condition, not an acute red-flag
presentation.
•
Dull, aching quality — characteristic of nociceptive mechanical pain (myofascial or articular), not
neuropathic (burning/shooting) or nociplastic (widespread, disproportionate).
•
Protracted head posture and rounded shoulders increase cervical axial load by up to 27 kg per
inch of forward head displacement (Hansraj 2014) — directly mechanically plausible.
•
Occupation as a graphic designer involves prolonged static cervical flexion/rotation — a well-
established risk factor for chronic neck pain.
•
Negative Spurling's (no radiculopathy) and negative Hoffmann's (no myelopathy) reduce
likelihood of neurological origin.
•
Osteoporosis does not cause neck pain directly but must be considered in management
approach.
•
Rationale: Quantifies functional restriction and reproduces the patient's familiar pain —
essential for baseline and outcome measurement.

Procedure: Assess all 6 cervical movements: flexion, extension, left/right lateral flexion,
left/right rotation. Use a goniometer or inclinometer for accuracy. Apply gentle overpressure at
end range if AROM is full and pain-free.
•
Normal Values: Flexion 45–50°, extension 55–70°, lateral flexion 45°, rotation 60–80°.
•
Interpretation: Restriction in extension and ipsilateral rotation suggests facet joint involvement.
Restriction in flexion with end-range pain suggests discogenic or deep cervical flexor
involvement. Deviation on rotation suggests muscle guarding or unilateral facet hypomobility.
•
Rationale: DCF weakness (longus colli, longus capitis) is the hallmark of cervicogenic neck
pain and upper crossed syndrome. These muscles stabilise the upper cervical spine and
correct forward head posture.
•
Procedure: Patient supine. Inflate pressure biofeedback unit (Chattanooga stabiliser) to 20
mmHg baseline under cervical lordosis. Instruct patient to perform gentle nodding action
(craniocervical flexion — NOT gross neck flexion). Progressively target 22, 24, 26, 28, 30
mmHg in 2 mmHg increments, holding each for 10 seconds.
•
Interpretation: Inability to reach 26 mmHg without compensatory superficial flexor activity
(sternocleidomastoid, anterior scalene) indicates DCF weakness — common in chronic neck
pain. Normal is achieving 28–30 mmHg with smooth activation.
•
Rationale: In Janda's Upper Crossed Syndrome, upper trapezius and levator scapulae are
hypertonic (overactive/shortened), contributing to elevated and protracted shoulders and
increased cervical loading.
•
Procedure: Stabilise shoulder girdle (depress and laterally rotate scapula). Passively laterally
flex the cervical spine away from the side being tested. Measure range and assess for stretch
pain, tissue resistance, or asymmetry.
•
Interpretation: Reduced ipsilateral lateral flexion with reproduction of stretch pain/resistance
confirms tightness in upper trapezius/levator scapulae. Combined with DCF weakness,
confirms the upper crossed syndrome pattern — informing the priority to stretch and inhibit
these muscles while strengthening DCF and lower trapezius.
•
Short-term (4 weeks): Reduce neck pain from NRS 6/10 to ≤ 3/10 at work; improve cervical
rotation ROM to within 10° of normal.
•
Long-term (12 weeks): Patient independently manages posture at workstation; DCF
endurance to 28 mmHg on CCFT; return to full work productivity without pain.

Initial Management (Sessions 1–3)
Progressive Rehabilitation (Sessions 4–12)
⚠️ Safety Note: Given osteoporosis, avoid cervical axial loading, high-velocity thrust techniques,
and sustained end-range compression. All manual therapy must be graded gently with ongoing
reassessment.
Patient Education: Explain the postural mechanism — forward head posture increases
cervical load and compresses facet joints. Dispel catastrophising (pain does not equal
damage). Introduce self-management concepts: movement breaks every 30–45 minutes,
postural cues.
•
Pain Management: Heat/TENS for symptomatic relief. Heat over upper trapezius before
exercise to reduce muscle tone. TENS at conventional (80–100 Hz) for gate control analgesia.
•
Manual Therapy — Modified for Osteoporosis: Grade II–III posteroanterior (PA) cervical
mobilisation (Maitland) to restricted segments — gentle oscillatory technique appropriate with
osteoporosis. NO high-velocity thrust manipulation to cervical or upper thoracic spine. Soft
tissue massage / trigger point therapy to upper trapezius and levator scapulae to reduce
muscle hypertonicity.
•
Ergonomic Advice: Screen at eye level, monitor at arm's length, lumbar support, forearm
support to reduce scapular elevation, frequent movement breaks.
•
Therapeutic Exercise — DCF Strengthening: CCFT-based progressive programme — start
at 22 mmHg, progress by 2 mmHg per week as tolerated. Sets/reps: 3 × 10 holds at each
pressure level. Progress to functional patterns (supine → sitting → standing).
•
Scapular Stabilisation: Lower trapezius strengthening (prone Y, W exercises), serratus
anterior activation — to correct rounded shoulders and reduce upper trapezius overload.
•
Cervical Flexibility: Upper trapezius/levator scapulae stretching (30-second holds, 3 reps,
daily) — Muscle Energy Technique (MET) for active participation. Cervical retraction exercises
(chin tuck) to correct forward head posture.
•
Postural Taping: Kinesiotape or postural correction tape to facilitate scapular retraction and
reduce forward head posture during work — short-term adjunct while motor learning is
established.
•
Bone Health Liaison: Confirm liaison with GP regarding osteoporosis management (DEXA
monitoring, medication review, fall prevention strategies). Bone-loading exercises (upright
weight-bearing postures) are beneficial for bone density.
•
Discharge Planning: Home exercise programme (HEP) reinforced from session 1. App or
written record for exercise adherence. Flare-up management plan. GP referral if symptoms
worsen or neurological signs develop.

CASE B — Mr. O'Shea
72-year-old male  |  Retired librarian  |  10-month thoracic back pain  |  Osteoporosis  | 
Corticosteroids  |  Percussion pain positive at T7–T8
Q1: Focused Subjective Assessment, Red Flags & Prioritised Problem List
Opening Statement:
"Mr. O'Shea, before I examine your back, I'd like to ask you some important questions. Some of
your details — particularly your age, your bone condition, and your medication — mean I need to be
especially thorough about understanding your symptoms and ruling out anything that needs urgent
attention."
Subjective Assessment — Key Areas
Chief Complaint & Location: Thoracic back pain centred at T7–T8 (mid-thoracic). Determine
whether pain is localised (one segment) or diffuse. Ask about radiation: intercostal pattern
(nerve root), anterior chest (referred visceral), or down into the lower back/legs.
•
Onset: 10 months — gradual or sudden? A sudden onset after minimal trauma (bending, lifting
light object, coughing, sneezing) in a patient with osteoporosis is HIGHLY suspicious for a
vertebral compression fracture (VCF).
•
Increasing Kyphosis: A change in posture over the period of symptoms suggests progressive
vertebral collapse — hallmark of VCF. Ask if height has reduced.
•
Pain Quality: Sharp, knife-like thoracic pain that is worse on movement and weight-bearing is
consistent with a fracture. Dull, aching pain suggests muscular or degenerative aetiology. Night
pain that wakes from sleep or constant unremitting pain → red flag.
•
Aggravating / Easing Factors: VCF characteristically worsens with spinal loading (standing,
walking, transitional movements — lying to sitting) and eases with recumbency. Degenerative
disc pain is often eased by walking (extension-biased).
•
Corticosteroid History: Duration, dose, reason for prescription (COPD? Inflammatory
condition?). Long-term corticosteroids (> 3 months, > 5mg prednisolone/day) cause significant
bone loss — markedly increasing fracture risk.
•
Neurological Symptoms: Any lower limb weakness, numbness, bilateral leg symptoms, gait
disturbance, or bowel/bladder changes → EMERGENCY. A VCF can result in spinal cord
compromise (thoracic cord compression → paraparesis).
•
Systemic Symptoms: Unexplained weight loss, fatigue, fever, night sweats — screen for
malignancy (multiple myeloma, metastatic disease very common in 70+ male with thoracic
pain).

Clinical Reasoning:
Differential 1: Malignant Spinal Disease (Metastatic / Multiple Myeloma)
Skeletal metastases to the thoracic spine are common in men over 65, particularly from prostate,
lung, or renal primary cancers. Features distinguishing metastatic disease from osteoporotic VCF
include: pain at rest or night pain, systemic features (weight loss, fatigue), multiple levels affected on
imaging, bone scan hot-spots, elevated ESR/calcium/alkaline phosphatase. This differential
CANNOT be excluded clinically and requires urgent imaging. Multiple myeloma should also be
considered — it causes lytic (punched-out) lesions on X-ray and presents with bone pain, anaemia,
and elevated paraprotein.
Differential 2: Degenerative Thoracic Disc Disease / Facet Osteoarthritis
Degenerative changes are universal in this age group and may produce thoracic stiffness and
diffuse aching. However, degenerative disease alone does not explain: (a) percussion pain localised
to one segment, (b) progressive kyphotic deformity, or (c) the severity and 10-month progressive
course. Degenerative changes are likely co-existent but are a secondary rather than primary
diagnosis. This distinction is critical — degenerative disease may be managed with physiotherapy
while a fracture or malignancy requires a different pathway.
Q3: Objective Assessment Techniques & Interpretation
⚠️ Important: Objective assessment must be MODIFIED and CAUTIOUS given the high likelihood

Percussion pain directly over T7–T8 spinous processes is a sensitive indicator of vertebral

•

•

•

Long-Term Rehabilitation (Once Fracture Stabilises — 6–12 Weeks)
Key clinical message: This case is primarily a MEDICAL RED FLAG scenario. Demonstrating
thorough red flag reasoning and appropriate referral decision-making is the highest-priority clinical
skill being assessed here.
Pain Management (Short Term): Paracetamol (preferred in elderly — NSAID risk of GI bleed,
renal impairment). Heat therapy for paraspinal muscle spasm. TENS over thoracic paraspinal
muscles. Brief relative rest (not bed rest) for 1–2 weeks.
•
ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS: High-velocity thrust manipulation to thoracic spine; axial
loading exercises; extension-based exercises initially; Grade IV–V mobilisation; traction.
•
Early Mobilisation (within 1–2 weeks): Gentle supported walking to prevent deconditioning.
Supine and seated pain-free movements. Bed transfer and positional advice to reduce pain
during transitional movements.
•
Bone Health Optimisation: Liaise with GP/rheumatologist for bisphosphonate prescription,
calcium (1200 mg/day), vitamin D (800–1000 IU/day). Consider referral for
vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty if pain is refractory.
•
Postural Rehabilitation: Gentle thoracic extension exercises (prone on elbows progression) to
reduce kyphotic progression. Avoid flexion-dominant exercises — flexion increases anterior
vertebral body compression. Wall slides for scapular retraction.
•
Strength Training: Low-load paraspinal extensor strengthening (bird-dog, prone hip
extension). Avoid high spinal loading. Hip and knee strengthening to reduce falls risk.
•
Falls Prevention: Balance training (static → dynamic). Home hazard assessment. Referral to
occupational therapy for aids and adaptations. Footwear review.
•
Respiratory Physiotherapy: Increasing kyphosis compromises thoracic cage expansion —
breathing exercises, incentive spirometry if required.
•
SMART Goals: At 12 weeks: walking 20 minutes without rest; pain ≤ 3/10 NRS; independent
home exercise programme; no further falls.

CASE C — Ms. Taylor
32-year-old female  |  Yoga instructor  |  6 months postpartum  |  Right SIJ / buttock pain  |  Thigh
Thrust & Sacral Thrust positive  |  SLR & FADDIR negative
Q1: Focused Subjective Assessment, Red Flags & Prioritised Problem List
Opening Statement:
"Ms. Taylor, I understand you've been having right-sided low back and buttock pain since having
your baby six months ago. I'd like to ask you some detailed questions before examining you —
including some questions to make sure we're not missing anything important. I'll also want to
understand how this is affecting your work as a yoga instructor and your life at home."
Subjective Assessment — Key Areas
Chief Complaint & Body Chart: Right-sided LBP with buttock pain. Clarify exact location —
SIJ pain typically localises to the PSIS region and gluteal area, rarely below the knee. Draw
pain distribution: unilateral posterior pain with possible posterior thigh referral is characteristic of
SIJ dysfunction.
•
Onset & Context: 6 months postpartum — the pelvic girdle has undergone significant
hormonal and biomechanical change during pregnancy. Relaxin (produced during pregnancy
and lactation) increases ligamentous laxity across the pelvic girdle including the SIJ and
symphysis pubis. This persists into the postpartum period.
•
Pain Quality: Dull aching at rest, sharp on movement — consistent with joint/ligament-
mediated nociceptive pain. Weight-bearing activities (standing, walking, stair climbing) typically
aggravate SIJ dysfunction.
•
Aggravating Factors: Rolling in bed, walking, stairs, prolonged standing, asymmetric loading
(standing on one leg, hip thrust yoga postures). These all involve SIJ loading or shear. Carrying
the baby on one hip is a classic aggravating factor.
•
Easing Factors: Rest, lying symmetrically, SIJ belt. Pain that improves with an SIJ belt strongly
supports SIJ aetiology.
•
24-Hour Behaviour: Morning pain/stiffness common with pelvic girdle pain (PGP). Ask about
sleep position — lying on the painful side or in unsupported postures aggravates SIJ.
•
Obstetric History: Mode of delivery (vaginal/caesarean), birth complications, perineal trauma.
Breastfeeding (prolongs relaxin elevation). Previous PGP in pregnancy?
•
Pelvic Floor: As a postpartum patient and yoga instructor, pelvic floor dysfunction (weakness
or hypertonia) is relevant. Ask about urinary incontinence, prolapse symptoms, pain with
intercourse — these may co-exist and influence core stability.

Clinical Reasoning:
Differential 1: Proximal Hamstring Tendinopathy (PHT)
PHT produces deep buttock pain at the ischial tuberosity, aggravated by running (particularly uphill),
sustained hip flexion with knee extension (stretching the hamstring origin), and sitting on hard
surfaces. PHT is distinguished by: pain localising to the ischial tuberosity (palpable), pain on resisted
knee flexion at 90° hip flexion, and positive bent-knee stretch test. FADIR would not typically
reproduce PHT pain (it does not stress the ischial attachment). The location of pain ('deep buttock'
vs. 'sitting-bone pain') and aggravating positions differentiate. In a marathon runner, PHT is
extremely common and should be specifically ruled out.
Differential 2: Gluteal Tendinopathy (Greater Trochanteric Pain Syndrome)
Gluteal tendinopathy (gluteus medius/minimus at greater trochanteric insertion) produces lateral
hip/buttock pain, aggravated by hip adduction positions (crossing legs, sustained hip IR, climbing
stairs) and single-leg loading. Resisted hip abduction would be painful and weak. Distinguishing
from DGS: location is lateral (greater trochanter) not deep central; FADIR may be painful due to
compressive loading of the tendon against the trochanter; sciatic radiation is absent. The painful
resisted hip abduction in this case raises this as a genuine differential. An MRI or ultrasound can
distinguish between tendinopathy and DGS/nerve involvement.
