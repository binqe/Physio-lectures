## Page 3

Introduction
• Definition of Clinical Reasoning: “The thinking and 
decision-making processes that physiotherapists use in 
clinical practice to gather and evaluate information, 
make diagnoses, and determine and implement 
interventions” (Higgs & Jones, 2008).
• Why it matters in MSK physiotherapy:
• Helps make sense of complex patient presentations.
• Guides safe and effective treatment.
• Integrates evidence, patient preferences, and clinical 
expertise (EBP triad).
“A 40-year-old office worker with shoulder pain — what 
questions do you ask first? What models might you use?”

## Page 5

1- Hypothetico-Deductive Model
Definition: Clinical reasoning based on generating multiple hypotheses early, then 
testing them systematically through history-taking and examination (Elstein et al., 
1978).
Example: Patient with knee pain — hypotheses might include meniscal injury, 
patellofemoral pain, OA. You ask targeted questions/tests to rule in/out.
Strengths: Structured, analytical, good for complex/novel cases.
Limitations: Time-consuming; risk of cognitive overload.

## Page 7

Case study (10 minutes)
• A 35-year-old recreational runner presents with 4 months of gradually 
worsening anterior knee pain. It started after she increased training 
mileage and now hurts with running, descending stairs and prolonged 
sitting. 
• She reports occasional clicking but no true locking. There is no history 
of a specific trauma. 
• Symptoms are worse at the end of the day, she can still run but only 
half her usual distance, and she is worried about whether continuing 
to train will “damage” her knee. No systemic symptoms.
• pain at the inferior patella and with a single-leg squat, reduced hip 
abductor strength on the symptomatic side, and aggravated pain on 
patellar compression.

## Page 8

How to use this technique
• Generate 4–6 competing hypotheses.
• For each hypothesis list the one or two most informative history 
questions and objective tests you would perform to rule in/out that 
hypothesis (be explicit about order).
• Decide which hypothesis to prioritise while you gather further data, 
and propose the next 2 immediate management decisions.
• Identify any red flags that would change your approach.

## Page 9

2- Pattern Recognition Model
Definition: Fast recognition of clinical patterns based on prior experience 
and knowledge (Norman et al., 2007).
MSK Example: A patient presents with pain radiating down the posterior leg, numbness in the 
lateral foot, and weakness in plantarflexion → clinician immediately recognizes this as an S1 
radiculopathy without consciously working through each step.
Strengths: Efficient, fast useful in experienced clinicians.
Limitations: high risk of bias, premature closure, less reliable in atypical 
cases.

## Page 10

Case Study 2 (5 minutes)
• A 45-year-old administrative assistant develops a 6-week history of lateral elbow 
pain. It began gradually after a period of heavy gardening. She reports pain when 
turning a screwdriver, pouring from a kettle and lifting a shopping bag. Pain is 
mainly local and focal over the lateral epicondyle, worse with gripping, and she 
notices weakness when shaking hands. No neck pain, no numbness or systemic 
signs.
• She reported pain on resisted wrist extension and with resisted middle-finger 
extension, provocative gripping reproduces symptoms. 
• State the rapid pattern diagnosis you would make and why (what cluster of 
features supports it).
• Outline the immediate management you would offer in a first 2–4 week plan 
(practical, time-limited).

## Page 12

3- Forward Reasoning (Inductive 
Reasoning)
• Definition: Reasoning from clinical data (symptoms, signs) 
directly to diagnosis without generating multiple hypotheses (Eva, 
2005).
• MSK Example: Acute inversion ankle injury → swelling, bruising, 
lateral tenderness → likely lateral ligament sprain.
• Strengths: Efficient in familiar cases.
• Limitations: May miss red flags or alternative causes.

## Page 15

How to use these models in practice
Eg: In case of a patient with ACL: 
• You might recognize a classic ACL tear from the story,
• then use deductive reasoning to test Lachman and pivot shift,
• and if the presentation is unclear, switch to inductive reasoning 
to carefully build the picture.

## Page 16

Narrative Reasoning
Definition: Understanding the patient’s experience, beliefs, 
and story to inform care (Mattingly, 1991).
MSK Example: Chronic low back pain — patient links pain to 
work stress, fears movement → reasoning helps tailor 
education and graded activity.
Strengths: Patient-centred, supports shared decision-
making.
Limitations: Less structured; requires good communication 
skills.

## Page 17

Case Study
• A 50-year-old warehouse operative presents with a 2-year history of low back 
pain. He was off work for 3 months after the initial episode and returned on 
light duties. 
• He says, “I’m scared to lift now — I don’t trust my back. My wife nags me 
about it and I can’t sleep; I worry I’ll slip up and lose my job.” 
• He expresses frustration that clinicians “just give exercises” and that nothing 
addresses why he keeps getting worse. 
• He is low on sleep and reports feeling down.
• Use this case to practise empathic listening, goal-setting aligned with 
work participation, and linking physical rehab to function and meaning.

## Page 19

Pragmatic Reasoning
Definition: Considering practical aspects like time, resources, environment, 
and patient preferences in decision-making (Higgs & Jones, 2008).
Example: Deciding to prescribe home-based exercises instead of supervised 
gym program due to patient’s limited availability.
Strengths: Realistic, adaptable.
Limitations: May conflict with ideal best-practice guidelines.

## Page 22

Dual Process Theory (System 1 & 2 Thinking)
Definition: Two modes of reasoning — fast, intuitive (System 1) 
and slow, analytical (System 2) (Kahneman, 2011).
Experienced physio quickly recognising frozen shoulder (System 
1), but switching to System 2 to carefully rule out red flags.
Strengths: Explains how both experience and analysis contribute 
to reasoning.
Limitations: Risk of cognitive bias when relying too much on 
System 1.

shoulder stiffness and pain. 
• She reports difficulty reaching overhead, reduced sleep due to 
achiness, and increasing difficulty dressing. 
• On examination passive and active external rotation are substantially 
reduced, abduction is limited; rotator cuff strength is hard to assess 
because of pain but grossly present. She has a history of type 2 
diabetes.
• What is your system 1 says and what would your system 2 
considers?

## Page 25

International Framework for Clinical Reasoning (IFCR)
Definition: A comprehensive framework describing reasoning processes across contexts, 
including diagnostic, narrative, interactive, and collaborative reasoning (Ajjawi et al., 2020).
MSK Example: Applying multiple reasoning approaches in a complex case like persistent 
knee pain post-ACL reconstruction.
Strengths: Integrative, modern, evidence-informed.
Limitations: Broad — may be harder for early students to grasp fully.
