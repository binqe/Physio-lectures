- Epidemiology of spinal pain in the globally
- Neck pain globally
- Highly widespread condition, with an age-standardized incidence of 807 per 100,000 globally in 2017
- Neck pain in the UK:
- Common — up to 70% of the population,  will suffer at some point in their lives.
- Prevalence peaks between the ages of 45 and 54.
- Global: Thoracic pain
- Estimated the prevalence of in 13% of the general population
- Less documented
- Low back pain globally:
- In 2020, affected 619 million people (1 in 13), with cases expected to rise to 843 million by 2050 due to population growth and ageing
- Highly prevalent worldwide, affecting up to 60% of adults at some point in their lives, with 3–4% of adults under 45 and 5–7% of those over 45 years experiencing chronic symptoms
- Low back pain the UK
- Most cases are non-specific, accounting for 90–95% of presentations to primary care
- 1% case are specific cause such as fracture, infection, tumour, AS and 8-10% radicular pain
- Prevalence has increased over time, with one-year rates rising from 36.4% to 49.1% and one-month prevalence around 28.5%, peaking in adults aged 41–50 years and remaining common in older adults
- Reported by one in four people aged over 80 years, Most common in women and people aged 40–80 years.

- Anatomy Contd…

## Slide 8

- Anatomy contd..
- Functionally, vertebral column consists of anterior and posterior column
- Anterior column is weight bearing and hydraulic with shock absorbing capability which consists of vertebral bodies intervertebral disks (IVDs)
- Human vertebral column (spine) is designed for the structural stability for full mobility and protection of the spinal cord and axial neural tissues
- Spine also contribute to functional requirement of gait and maintenance of static weight bearing posture
- Basic building block of the spine is the vertebrae
- Although sufficient for static load, not have necessary flexibility for dynamic load bearing

## Slide 9

- Facet Joints
- A motion segment in the vertebral column is defined as two adjacent vertebrae and three joints (one joint is formed between two vertebral bodies and IVDs, and other two joints between facet joints)
- Vertebral column consists of 24 pairs of facet joints
- The facet joints are plane joints as articular surfaces are flat, covered with hyaline cartilage and synovial in nature
- The articular process of facet joints allow certain movement and resists excessive torsions and shear
- Horizontal articular surface  favours axial rotation like in cervical region (progress horizontal to 45 degree vertical in lower cervical segments)
- In thoracic spine almost vertical direction with coronal orientation which facilitate axial rotation and resists anterior displacement
- In lumbar spine vertical in with a curved, J shaped surface essentially in sagittal plane which restricts  rotation and resists anterior shear
- Facet joint play important role in examination and intervention of mechanical spinal disorders

## Slide 10

- Intervertebral discs
- Lies between adjacent superior and inferior vertebral bodies from C2 to S1 and similar in shape to the respective vertebral bodies
- Largest avascular structure in the bodies
- Each disk composed of inner nucleus pulposus and outer annulus fibrosus and limiting cartilage end plates
- The annulus and end plates anchor the IVD to the vertebral bodies
- IVD contribute to 20-25% of the length of vertebral column
- In the cervical and lumbar region, IVDs are thicker anteriorly and this contribute to the normal lordosis
- In the thoracic spine, IVDs is of uniform thickness
- Although incapable of independent motion, it moves during flexion, extension, side flexion and rotation.
- The intervertebral discs (IVDs) have to handle several types of forces — pressure from above (axial compression), sliding forces (shear), and movements such as bending and twisting, either on their own or happening together

## Slide 12

- Major and Accessory Spinal Ligaments
- Major Ligaments
- 1. Anterior Longitudinal Ligament (ALL)
- Origin: Anterior tubercle of atlas (C1) and anterior surface of occipital bone
- Insertion: Anterior surface of sacrum
- Function: Prevents hyperextension of the vertebral column
- 2. Posterior Longitudinal Ligament (PLL)
- Origin: Posterior aspect of vertebral bodies from C2
- Insertion: Posterior surface of sacrum
- Function: Limits hyperflexion; supports intervertebral discs
- 3. Ligamentum Flavum
- Origin: Anterior surface of lamina above
- Insertion: Posterior surface of lamina below
- Function: Maintains upright posture, assists in extension after flexion

## Slide 13

- 1.Interspinous Ligament
- Origin: Inferior edge of spinous process above
- Insertion: Superior edge of spinous process below
- Function: Limits spinal flexion
- 2. Supraspinous Ligament
- Origin: Spinous process of C7 (continuation of nuchal ligament)

- 4. Intertransverse Ligament
- Origin: Inferior border of transverse process above Insertion: Superior border of transverse process below
- Function: Limits lateral flexion of the spine

## Slide 18

- Pain mechanism
- Pain pathways are significant in clinical applications as they underlie pain perception and provide pain management targets
- Pain Mechanism
- 1. Transduction:
- Tissue injury releases chemicals (e.g., bradykinin, prostaglandins, histamine, ATP).
- These activate nociceptors (pain receptors) in the skin, muscles, or organs (silent nociceptors).
- 2. Transmission:
- Pain signals travel via Aδ (fast) and C (slow) fibers → spinal cord (dorsal horn) → brain (thalamus & cortex via spinothalamic tract).
- 3. Modulation:
- Descending pathways from brainstem (e.g., serotonin, norepinephrine, endorphins) inhibit or amplify pain signals in the spinal cord.
- 4. Perception:
- Brain interprets signals as pain, influenced by emotion, memory, and context.

**Notes:** Pain Pathways - TeachMePhysiology

## Slide 19

- Neuroplasticity
- Peripheral Sensitization or hyperexcitability
- Happens at the site of injury.
- When tissues are damaged, they release chemicals (like prostaglandins, bradykinin, histamine).
- These make the pain receptors (nociceptors) more sensitive.
- Result → even light touch or mild stimuli can feel painful.(Example: Sunburned skin feels sore even with a gentle touch.)
- Adaptive and self limiting
- Central Sensitization
- Occurs in the spinal cord and brain.
- Continuous pain signals from the periphery make the spinal neurons overactive.
- This causes increased pain sensitivity and pain spreading beyond the original injury area.(Example: Chronic back pain that becomes widespread.)
- Can be maladaptive and create pain in the absence of any noxious stimulus
- Key mechanism of chronic pain
- Hyperalgesia: Increased pain from stimulus that normally provokes pain: Primary and secondary
- Allodynia: Pain due to a stimulus that does not normally provoke pain

## Slide 22

- Descending modulation
- The brain can control how much pain you feel by sending signals down to the spinal cord.
- Descending Inhibition (reduces pain):
- Brain areas like the periaqueductal gray (PAG) and rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM) release chemicals such as
- Serotonin (5-HT)
- Norepinephrine (NE)
- Endorphins (natural painkillers)
- These block or reduce pain signals before they reach the brain.
- Descending Facilitation (increases pain):
- In some chronic pain states, the brain amplifies pain instead of reducing it.

## Slide 28

- Biopsychosocial model of pain
- Pain is a personal, multidimensional experience, not merely a symptom of tissue damage.
- Arises from a dynamic interaction of biological, psychological and social factors
- These factors are interdependent, not separate — they shape the individual’s lived pain experience.
- A biopsychosocial approach can be applied by a single clinician using multiple strategies that address biological, psychological, and social factors.
- In complex or chronic cases, a multidisciplinary or interdisciplinary team may be required to deliver comprehensive assessment and care.

## Slide 38

- Special Tests for Back Pain ± Radiculopathy
- Lower back
- Straight Leg Raise (SLR) Test
- Assesses lumbar nerve root irritation (L4–S1), Pain re produce at 30–70° hip flexion
- Crossed Straight Leg Raise Test
- Elevating the unaffected leg reproduces pain in the affected leg, specific for herniated disc with nerve root compression
- Femoral Nerve Stretch Test
- Patient lies prone; knee is flexed and hip extended, Pain in anterior thigh suggests L2–L4 radiculopathy
- Slump Test:
- Patient slumps forward with neck flexion, knee extended
- Positive if radicular symptoms reproduced, Tests neural tension in the sciatic nerve and spinal cord
- Faber test: SI joint pathology, Reproduce pain in Hip or SI joint during test
- SI Joint Compression/Distraction test: SI joint pathology, reproduce pain during test
- Neck:
- Cervical distraction test: Cervical nerve root compression, relieve of symptoms during test
- Spurling test:
- Used for Cervical nerve root compression, reproduction of symptoms during test
- Shoulder abduction test: Cervical radiculopathy at C5-C6, relieves symptoms during test
- ULNTTs (Upper limb nerve tension test):
- For median, radial and ulnar nerve, reproduce symptoms
- Lhermitte's sign: :Flexion of neck causes electric shock like sensation down to spine. Common in MS
- Hoffman Test: is indicative of an upper motor neuron lesion
- Used to support or refine working diagnosis

## Slide 48

- Osteoporosis
- Key Facts:
- Osteoporosis and related fractures are a major cause of morbidity in RA patients.
- Remain asymptomatic until fracture occurs
- ~1 in 3 women with RA experience a fracture within 5 years of diagnosis.
- Fragility fractures significantly increase both mortality and morbidity.
- Diagnosis is based on history of fragility fracture or low bone mineral density which is T score equal or less than 2.5.
- Why the Increased Risk?
- Reduced mobility
- Chronic inflammation
- RA treatments – particularly glucocorticoids
- Steroid Use in RA:
- Steroids greatly elevate fracture risk
- Risk exceeds what is expected from changes in bone mineral density alone
- Lifestyle Measures for All Patients:
- Regular exercise
- Increase dietary calcium
- Vitamin D supplements
- Medication Review:
- Minimize steroid dose and duration
- Use lowest effective dose

## Slide 64

- Referred Pain
- Following are causes of referred pain:
- Central disc protrusion - Bilateral leg pain, gait or sphincter disturbance, saddle anaesthesia – this is a neurosurgical emergency - refer immediately.
- Nerve root irritation/compression - There is a dermatomal distribution of pain, numbness or paraesthesia with loss of reflexes or weakness in corrresponding myotomes. It is usually single level. If more than one level is affected, the patient needs urgent assessment. Often, pain in the limbs is greater than pain in the back or neck.
- Referred myofascial pain (trapezius, gluteal muscles, paraspinal muscles) - The pattern of pain is often deep or aching, usually involving proximal muscle groups (thighs/shoulders). It doesn’t usually extend to hands or feet. Nerve stretch tests such as straight leg raise are not provocative of pain.
- Piriformis syndrome - The sciatic nerve passes through the piriformis muscle (a hip external rotator) in less than 10% of the population. Pain distribution is similar to sciatica but tends to be worse during or after exercise. Reflexes, motor and sensory testing are usually normal and nerve stretch tests such as the straight leg raise are negative.
- Spinal canal stenosis - Caused by narrowing of the spinal canal or neural foramina producing root ischaemia and neurogenic claudication. Stenosis of the spinal canal is most often caused by a combination of loss of disc space, osteophytes and a hypertrophic ligamentum flavum. Patients are usually aged over 60 and present with spinal claudication – unilateral or bilateral leg pain, +/- back pain, numbness and weakness which develop after the patient walks a predictable distance. Patients often report it is easier to walk uphill rather than down. It is important to rule out peripheral vascular disease as a differential diagnosis.

## Slide 65

- Nerve root pain management
- The initial management of patients with single-level nerve root symptoms even with neurological signs is the same as for patients with non-specific back pain:
- These patients should be reviewed regularly.
- 50% of patients will improve within six weeks.
- If symptoms are improving but not resolved, then consider a referral to physiotherapy .
- If they have progressive neurological signs or uncontrolled pain, they should be referred for urgent MRI and specialist assessment (i.e. within 1-2 weeks).
- If nerve root signs and symptoms are not resolving at six weeks, consider an onward referral for MRI scan as these patients may be considered for intervention such as nerve root block or surgery.
- There is a difficult balance to strike between not referring patients with symptoms of radiculopathy who are likely to improve with conservative treatment too early, and not waiting too long to refer those who are not improving.

- Used to avoid  the problems of adjacent level disease by preserving motion at the level
- Pain relief due to excision of the painful disc and restoration or improvement of load transfer
- Need larger exposure to front of the spine, longer recovery and higher risk of complication
- 4-7 days hospital stay
- 6 weeks away from work, avoid prolong sitting and heavy lifting
- Facet arthroplasty/Face replacement:
- To restore normal motion and provide stabilization of spinal segment through replacement of facet ( following facetectomy and neural decompression)
- For the treatment of symptomatic facet joint arthritis
- Novel procedure but long term result unknown
- Allow movement to be preserved
- Not widely performed
- Fusion:
- Symptomatic lumbar DDD
- Two vertebrae fuse which act as a solid bone
- Gold standard treatment for back pain
- Aim to fuse arthritis joint
- No heavy lifting for 3 month

## Slide 114

- Take-Home Messages
- Back pain is common but requires careful assessment to identify serious or treatable causes.
- Differentiate between mechanical, non-mechanical, inflammatory, radicular, and visceral sources.
- Red flags (e.g. bowel or bladder dysfunction, weight loss, fever, night pain) must prompt further investigation for conditions like cancer or infection or CES.
- Sciatica and radiculopathy often involve nerve root irritation – assess with focused history and special tests.
- Most mechanical back pain improves with activity, education, and reassurance.
- Multidisciplinary management – including physiotherapy, medication, and lifestyle – is key to optimal outcomes.
- Always consider the patient’s function, not just their pain.
