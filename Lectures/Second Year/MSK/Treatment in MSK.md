## Page 5

What does best practice care for musculoskeletal pain look like? 
Eleven consistent recommendations from high-quality clinical 
practice guidelines: systematic review
1. Care should be patient centred. 
2. Screen patients to identify those with a higher likelihood of serious pathology/red flag conditions.
3. Assess psychosocial factors.
4. Radiological imaging is discouraged unless:
1.
Serious pathology is suspected.
2.
There has been an unsatisfactory response to conservative care or unexplained progression of signs and symptoms.
3.
It is likely to change management.
5. Undertake a physical examination, which could include neurological screening tests, assessment of mobility and/or muscle 
strength.
6. Patient progress should be evaluated including the use of outcome measures.
7. Provide patients with education/information about their condition and management options.
8. Provide management addressing physical activity and/or exercise.
9. Apply manual therapy only as an adjunct to other evidence-based treatments.
10. Unless specifically indicated (e.g. red flag condition), offer evidence-informed non-surgical care prior to surgery.
11. Facilitate continuation or resumption of work.

## Page 7

Pain 
Management
Physiotherapy is commonly a key aspect in managing 
chronic pain, being part of this multidisciplinary, bio-psycho-
social approach.
Evidence has shown that physiotherapists use a variety of 
skills to guide and support people with chronic pain towards 
a better quality of life.
Despite progress in chronic pain management, some 
patients and some clinicians still expect a ‘cure’. It is often 
assumed that ‘hands-on’ physiotherapy will be offered, 
(which is commonly not indicated or appropriate). 
Certain patients fear physiotherapy, exercise and activity, as 
past experience has worsened their pain or not helped at all.

## Page 8

Pain Management 
Intervention
Description
1. Education
General education on injury/disease, acute and chronic pain-physiology, neuroscience education, regimes, activity-modification, ergonomics, 
general health, coping strategies
2. Sensorimotor training
Can also be termed neuromuscular training or motor control exercises and refers to exercises with the purpose of improving sensorimotor 
control - including training of muscular synergies, balanced in time and magnitude through the relearning of motor control - in specific, the 
exercises aim at creating appropriate, automatic and generalized movements to optimize muscular joint stabilization commonly affected by the 
pain
3. Physical activity-advice
Individualized advice on how to optimize the individual patient’s physical activity despite the chronic pain, most often advice on walking, Nordic 
walking, cross-training or cycling.
4. Interventions aiming at 
improving structures and 
functions
Stretching, manual physical therapy techniques, heat or cold treatment, spray-n-stretch-technique, taping – all prior to exercises for movement 
or stabilization
5. Sensory stimulation
Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, acupuncture
6. Physical activity carried 
out together with the 
physiotherapist
Physical activity with direct guidance on for example exercise-bike, cross-trainer or Nordic walking
7. Weight training
Individualized exercises performed in a gym center or weight training with other equipment
8. Relaxation, Mindfulness Relaxation techniques such as breathing exercises, progressive muscle relaxation, autogenous training. Mindfulness-training including paying 
attention to thoughts, feelings, bodily sensations other than pain in the present moment, often while carrying out movements
9. Physiotherapy 
interventions with an 
Acceptance and 
commitment therapy 
(ACT)-inspired approach
Interventions to support acceptance, committed action, values, movement- and behavior change and to reduce fear-of-movement, and also 
specific interventions on motivation - MI, motivating interviewing - aiming at improving self-efficacy in behavior-change
10. Basic body awareness 
therapy and training of 
specified activities
A movement-based physiotherapeutic method developed in Scandinavia where movements aim at enhance body awareness and consciousness 
of the body with the purpose to move with less effort

Therapeutic Exercise 
• Evidence from systematic reviews support the 
use of therapeutic exercise to improve MSK 
pain and chronic illnesses in several 
conditions. 
• Incorporation of exercise and PA and their 
importance are usually introduced during initial 
individual appointments and continued through 
to structured exercise classes or as a 
component of a chronic pain rehabilitation 
program. 
• Using frequency, intensity, type, and time 
(duration) parameters (F.I.T.T.) for specific 
exercises.
• The choice of exercise therapy should be 
closely aligned with patients’ preferences and 
goals as enjoyment in and commitment to the 
type of exercise will help with long-term 
adherence

Manual 
Therapy
Manual therapy may be categorised into four major groups: 
a) Soft tissue 
techniques: Trp 
therapy,
b) mobilisation 
(non-thrust 
manipulation), 
c) static, and 
dynamic stretching 
(Muscle energy 
technique)
d) Neurodynamic 
treatment. 
Manual therapy is a non-surgical type of conservative 
management that includes different skilled hands/fingers-
on techniques directed to the patient’s body (spine and 
extremities) for the purpose of assessing, diagnosing, and 
treating a variety of symptoms and conditions.

## Page 18

Underlying Mechanism of Efficacy
The rationale has changed from biomechanical paradigm to a 
neurophysiological one.
Manual therapy may provide its pain-relieving effects via the activation of 
substantia gelitanosa and descending modulatory pathways in the central 
nervous system.
Moderately painful pressure can lead to short-lasting pain inhibition 
(sometimes referred to as pain-inhibits-pain) in both humans and rodents.
Furthermore, manual therapy is likely to have an influence on pain via more 
cognitive and contextual factors.

## Page 20

Therapeutic 
Ultrasound
• Therapeutic US has been previously used by physical 
therapists on patients with soft-tissue pathologies ( 
with the ability to accurately target deep tissues (3-5 
cm)),  including tendinitis, bursitis, muscle 
spasms, neuromas, and myositis ossificans . 
• Reports have demonstrated prolonged use of US can 
cause dose-dependent adverse effects such as burns, 
soft-tissue necrosis, and hemorrhage.
• There is limited evidence on the clinical efficacy of 
therapeutic US. US has been shown to have benefits 
in patients with knee osteoarthritis (OA) when used in 
addition to laser light therapy and exercise. 
• However, studies including soft-tissue conditions such 
as tendinopathies have shown no benefit from 
independent US usage, indicating a necessity for 
further research on its utility in the field of orthopedics.

## Page 21

Transcutaneous 
Electrical Nerve 
Stimulation 
(TENS) 
• A technique with few adverse effects, offering the most 
common application of ENS for the relief of 
musculoskeletal pain.
• Is applied by surface electrodes offering safe, 
inexpensive, non-invasive, and easy application. 
Mechanism of Action:
• Stimulation of sensory afferent nerve fibers and 
thereby, enhancement of the pain gate control and 
opioid system.
• Activation of serotonin, cholinergic and opioid 
receptors in the spinal cord.

## Page 22

A. Joint Hypomobility (Stiff or Limited Range of 
Motion)
• Low-Grade Joint Mobilization: low-grade (Grade II-III) 
mobilizations to maintain joint mobility and reduce stiffness.
• Stretching and ROM Maintenance: stretching and active range of 
motion exercises to preserve flexibility.
• Low-Impact Aerobic Exercises: Swimming, cycling, or walking to 
maintain joint health without excessive loading.
• Strengthening: surrounding muscle strength with low-resistance 
exercises to reduce joint load.
• Hydrotherapy
• Adjuncts: Heat therapy or TENS for localised pain control.

## Page 23

B. Joint Hypermobility 
• Strengthening of Stabilizers Long-term strengthening programs 
focused on the muscles surrounding the joint to provide stability.
• Proprioceptive Training to improve joint stability and awareness.
• Functional Training relevant to the patient’s activities to build 
joint stability in real-world movements.
• Bracing or Taping use of braces or taping during activities to 
prevent further joint damage.
• Education joint protection strategies for long-term self-
management.

## Page 24

C- Degenerative Joint Disease
• Activity Modification as advising patients on modifying high-impact 
activities to reduce joint stress.
• Strengthening Exercises to maintain muscle strength around the joint 
to reduce the load on the joint.
• Stretching of tight soft tissue and active ROM to preserve flexibility.
• Joint Protection by educating body mechanics to protect joints during 
daily activities. 
• Adjuncts: Use of heat, TENS, or pharmacological interventions (as 
prescribed) for chronic pain relief. Hydrotherapy low-impact exercises 
in water to maintain joint movement and reduce pain.
