What does best practice care for musculoskeletal pain look like? Eleven 
consistent recommendations from high-quality clinical practice guidelines: 
systematic review
1. Care should be patient centred.
2. Screen patients to identify those with a higher likelihood of serious pathology/red flag conditions.
3. Assess psychosocial factors.
4. Radiological imaging is discouraged unless:
1.
Serious pathology is suspected.
2.
There has been an unsatisfactory response to conservative care or unexplained progression of signs and symptoms.
3.
It is likely to change management.
5. Undertake a physical examination, which could include neurological screening tests, assessment of mobility and/or muscle 
strength.
6. Patient progress should be evaluated including the use of outcome measures.
7. Provide patients with education/information about their condition and management options.
8. Provide management addressing physical activity and/or exercise.
9. Apply manual therapy only as an adjunct to other evidence-based treatments.
10. Unless specifically indicated (e.g. red flag condition), offer evidence-informed non-surgical care prior to surgery.
11. Facilitate continuation or resumption of work.

## Page 6

Pain Management
• Physiotherapy is commonly a key aspect in managing chronic pain, 
being part of this multidisciplinary, bio-psycho-social approach.
• Evidence has shown that physiotherapists use a variety of skills to 
guide and support people with chronic pain towards a better quality of 
life.
• Despite progress in chronic pain management, some patients and 
some clinicians still expect a ‘cure’. It is often assumed that ‘hands-on’ 
physiotherapy will be offered, (which is commonly not indicated or 
appropriate). 
• Certain patients fear physiotherapy, exercise and activity, as past 
experience has worsened their pain or not helped at all.

## Page 21

Lateral shift correction
• Passive shift correction is often the starting point. The 
therapist stands on the side of the trunk deviation and 
applies a sustained translatory force to the thorax in the 
opposite direction of the shift, while stabilising the pelvis. 
This is held for several seconds and repeated, observing 
symptom response. A positive response is reduction in leg 
pain and gradual realignment of the trunk.
• Once some correction is achieved, active shift correction is 
encouraged. The patient is taught to move their trunk toward 
the corrected position while maintaining upright posture, 
repeating this throughout the day. This promotes self-
management and prevents recurrence.
• After the shift begins to reduce, extension-based exercises 
are usually introduced, such as repeated lumbar extension in 
standing or lying. These help promote disc material migration 
anteriorly and reduce posterior nerve root compression.

## Page 22

Lateral shift correction
• When passive shift correction is too painful, this usually indicates a 
highly sensitive nerve root and acute inflammatory phase of disc 
pathology. Forcing the correction at this stage can lead to 
peripheralisation of symptoms, increased muscle spasm and further 
protective guarding, which is counterproductive. The priority then 
shifts from mechanical realignment to symptom modulation and 
gradual desensitisation.
• The first step is to find a position of comfort rather than a position of 
correction. This involves placing the patient in a posture that reduces 
leg pain, even if the shift remains visible. Often this is side-lying on 
the non-painful side with pillows supporting the trunk, or lying prone 
if tolerated. The aim is to reduce neural irritation and allow muscle 
guarding to settle.
• Instead of direct manual correction, repeated movements within the 
patient’s tolerance are introduced. These are usually very small-
range movements toward the direction that centralises symptoms. 
For example, gentle side-gliding in standing or very mild lateral shifts 
performed actively by the patient, staying well within pain limits. The 
key principle is symptom-guided movement, not structural force.

• Neural mobilization generally consists of techniques termed neural glides or neural

Contraindications to Vertebral Mobilisations
1. Neurological: Disturbance of bladder and bowel function, or perineal 
anaesthesia, Spinal cord symptoms (e.g. CES or cervical myelopathy).
2. Spinal instability: Down syndrome, Rheumatoid Arthritis and Osteoporosis, 
Spondylolysis, spondylolisthesis
3. Any pathology leading to significant bone-weakening such as tumours, 
infections, long-term corticosteroid medication, fracture 
4. Patients with vertigo need close supervision
5. Laxity of ligaments: Hypermobility, Pregnancy
6. Aortic aneurysm, bleeding into joints, e.g. severe Haemophilia
7. Musculoskeletal deformity

## Page 44

During PPIVMS assessment
• What you are looking for during PPIVM assessment
• During PPIVM assessment you guide the patient’s spine through specific physiological 
movements such as flexion, extension, rotation, or side-flexion while palpating the spinous 
or transverse processes. 
• You are looking for the amount of movement available at each segment, the smoothness of 
the movement, and whether any levels feel restricted or excessively loose. 
• You are also checking whether symptoms are reproduced during the motion. 
• This helps you identify segments that are hypomobile, hypermobile, painful, or 
dysfunctional during normal physiological movement.

## Page 45

During PPIVMS Treatment
• What you are looking for during PPIVM treatment
• During PPIVM treatment you apply repeated passive physiological movements to the 
restricted segment to improve its mobility and reduce symptoms. 
• You look for increasing range, smoother motion, reduced resistance, and less symptom 
reproduction as the sets progress. 
• PPIVMs are often used when the desired functional movement is limited, so treatment 
aims to restore the specific physiological movement rather than only accessory glide. 
• Improvement is confirmed through repeated reassessment of active movement after 
each set.

## Page 46

1. Maitland’s Mobilization
Mobilizations involve repetitive, passive movements applied to 
spinal joints at varying speeds and amplitudes. 
They are graded according to the Maitland Concept:
•
Grade I: A small-amplitude movement near the starting 
position of the range
•
Grade II: A large-amplitude of movement that carries well into 
the range. It can occupy any part of the range that is free of any 
stiffness or muscle spasm that does move into stiffness or 
muscle spasm
•
Grade III: A large-amplitude movement, but one that does 
move into stiffness or muscle spasm
•
Grade IV: A small-amplitude movement stretching into 
stiffness or muscle spasm

## Page 47

During PAIVM Assessment 
• During PAIVM assessment we are palpating the intervertebral spinal mobility (between each two 
vertebrae) when applying a posterior–anterior  (PA) glide. 
• Assessing whether the segment feels normal, stiff, hypermobile, painful, or different compared with 
the levels above and below. 
• Note whether the pain reproduced matches the patient’s symptoms, including location, depth, and 
behaviour. 
• Assessing the quality of the end-feel, the resistance felt under the therapists hands, and any protective 
muscle guarding. 
• Each PA pressure helps determine whether the joint is contributing to the patient’s symptoms and 
whether it needs treatment.

## Page 48

During PAIVM treatment
PAIVMs are defined as producing movements in directions that can not be produced 
actively in isolation but nonetheless need to be available for active movements to occur. 
In the periphery, these movements are generally glides produced by a linear force and 
result in translational rather than rotational movement. 
In the spine, PAIVMs are produced by applying a translational force, but unlike in the 
periphery, the movements that are produced are not purely translational.

## Page 56

Concept
• Mulligan proposed that injuries or sprains might result in a minor 
"positional fault" to a joint causing restrictions in physiological 
movement.
• The techniques have been developed to overcome joint `tracking' 
problems or `positional faults', i.e. joints with subtle biomechanical 
changes.
• Normal joints have been designed in such a way that the shape of the 
articular surfaces, the thickness of the cartilage, the orientation of the 
fibres of ligaments and capsule, the direction of pull of muscles and 
tendons, facilitate free but controlled movement while simultaneously 
minimizing the compressive forces generated by that movement
• Normal proprioceptive feedback maintains this balance. Alteration in any 
or all of the above factors would alter the joint position or tracking during 
movement and would provoke symptoms of pain, stiffness or weakness in 
the patient. It is common sense then that a therapist would attempt to re-
align the joint surfaces in the least provocative way

## Page 57

Principles of Treatment
• A fundamental rule of Mulligan spinal work is that the therapist applies the accessory glide while the 
patient performs the symptomatic movement at the same time. 
• This combined approach ensures that the glide directly influences the joint’s movement pathway.
• The technique is only considered valid if it produces an immediate, observable improvement. 
• If a glide does not change symptoms on the first or second attempt, it is abandoned and another 
direction or level is tried. 
• Mulligan cautions clinicians never to persist with a non-effective glide. 
• Therapists must ensure that the applied force is sustained throughout the patient’s entire movement arc.
• While applying "MWMS" as an assessment, the therapist should look for PILL response to use the same 
as a Treatment .
P- Pain free.
I- Instant result.
LL- Long Lasting.

Limb Movement 
(SMWLMs)
Here a transverse pressure is applied to the side of the 
relevant spinous process as the patient concurrently 
moves the limb through the previously restricted range 
of movement.

movement is of spinal origin of course.

beyond a certain point.
Thus the technique addresses a spinal structural/ 
mechanical restriction, but this may have neural 
implications too.

Myofascial Release 
• Myofascial release (MFR) therapy involves specifically guided low load, long duration 
mechanical forces to manipulate the myofascial complex, intended to restore optimal 
length, decrease pain, and improve function. MFR utilises the manual traction and 
prolonged stretching of the fascia and muscle to break down the adhesions, thus helps to 
decrease the pain and increase flexibility and thereby increase ROM.
• It has been hypothesized that fascial restrictions in one part of the body cause undue 
tension in other parts of the body, due to fascial continuity; in turn, this may create stress 
on any structures that are enveloped, divided, or supported by fascia (Schleip et al, 2003).
