# Trauma MSK Clinical Simulation Workbook for Physiotherapy Students

Trauma MSK Clinical Simulation Workbook for Physiotherapy Students
Learning Objectives for Clinical Simulation:
Assess and identify musculoskeletal impairments related to trauma injuries.
Develop a comprehensive rehabilitation plan for patients with sports-related fractures.
Enhance students' skills in gait analysis, strength testing, and functional assessments.
Foster clinical decision-making in managing pain, mobility, and functional limitations.
Emphasize the importance of patient-centred care, including social and psychological factors in recovery.

| Patient Name: A
Age: 15
Setting: Trauma MSK Clinic

Subjective Assessment
Patient A, a 15-year-old male, was referred to the trauma clinic following a spiral fracture of the left tibia, which he sustained while playing rugby. He reports that the injury occurred during a match when his foot got caught in the mud during a tackle, causing his ankle to twist into eversion. This mechanism resulted in the fracture.
Upon initial injury, Patient A experienced sharp pain with a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) rating of 6-7, which progressively worsened. He was taken to the hospital the same day, where his ankle was immobilized in a cast. At the time of this assessment, he has been transitioned out of the cast and is currently using an orthopaedic ankle boot. He also reported numbness on the sole of the left heel, which has diminished since being in the cast.
Relevant Medical History:
No previous fractures, surgeries, or chronic conditions.
No known allergies.
Not currently taking any medications.
Social and Lifestyle History:
Patient A lives with his father and sister due to parental separation.
He is a secondary school student and has been actively playing rugby for the past 5-6 years.
His injury has temporarily halted his participation in sports, significantly affecting his daily activities and mood. |
| Objective Assessment for Role Play

Scenario Overview
You will be conducting an objective assessment of Patient A, a 15-year-old male who sustained a spiral fracture of the left tibia while playing rugby. The patient has been out of his cast for some time and is currently using an orthopaedic ankle boot. He reports discomfort, muscle weakness, limited ankle range of motion (ROM), and gait abnormalities.

Step 1: General Observation
Observe the patient in a standing position and note any visible asymmetries, weight shifts, or postural deviations.
Check for signs of muscle atrophy around the left ankle.
Assess if the patient is favouring one side (e.g., standing with the left heel slightly raised).
Document any pain behaviours or signs of discomfort during observation

Patient A presents in an orthopaedic ankle boot, with noticeable muscle atrophy around the left ankle.
Slight weight shift towards the right side during stance, indicating compensation for pain and weakness on the left side.
Stands with a slightly raised left heel, suggesting reduced tolerance for weight-bearing on the affected side.

Step 2: Gait Analysis
Ask the patient to walk a few steps and observe their gait pattern:
Look for limping, weight-shifting, or compensatory movements, such as crossing the right foot over the midline during turns.
Pay attention to heel strike, foot positioning, and toe-off phases.
Note any difficulty with turns or changes in speed, indicating balance or coordination issues.
Patient exhibits a noticeable limp due to discomfort in the left ankle. He occasionally crosses his right foot over the midline when turning or slowing down, likely as a compensatory mechanism.

Step 3: Range of Motion (ROM) Assessment
Perform a ROM assessment of the left ankle and compare it to the right side:
Dorsiflexion (DF): Ask the patient to pull their foot upwards.
Plantarflexion (PF): Ask the patient to point their toes downward.
Use a goniometer to measure ROM, if available.
Apply gentle overpressure to assess end range and determine if the patient can achieve greater ROM with assistance.
Document any pain or discomfort reported during ROM testing.
Reduced dorsiflexion (DF) and plantarflexion (PF) on the left ankle compared to the right side.
Application of overpressure during the examination elicited increased ROM in both DF and PF on the left side, indicating potential for improvement with therapy.

Step 4: Muscle Strength Testing
Conduct manual muscle testing (MMT) using the Oxford scale (0-5):
Test plantarflexion strength on both sides by asking the patient to push their foot down against resistance.
Grade the patient’s strength on a scale of 0 (no contraction) to 5 (full strength against resistance).
Assess for muscle bulk and any signs of atrophy or weakness in the lower leg muscles.
Muscle strength assessed using the Oxford scale showed reduced strength in left plantarflexion compared to the right, graded as 4/5.
Muscle bulk on the left side was significantly reduced due to disuse atrophy following immobilization.

Step 5: Sensory Examination
Conduct a sensory assessment to evaluate for numbness and altered sensation:
Lightly touch the sole of the patient’s left heel and ask if there are any changes in sensation compared to the right side.
Use a pinprick or light touch to assess for deficits in pain and temperature perception.
Document any areas of reduced sensation or altered sensation, especially in the sole of the foot.

Step 7: Documentation and Analysis
Record your findings systematically:
Note all measurements, observations, and patient responses.
Highlight any abnormalities in gait, ROM, strength, or sensation.
Based on your objective findings, formulate a problem list that will guide your treatment plan. |
