## Slide 3

- Flexibility
- “To bend or be pliable” (Alter, 2004)
- Definition: ‘the ability of the neuromuscular system to allow for efficient movement of a joint or series of joints through full, non-restricted, pain free range of motion’ (Prentice, 2004)
- Allows movement
- Not always large ranges of movement, sometimes mechanical advantage
- What movements do you NEED flexibility for

**Notes:** Sport related norms

## Slide 4

- Biomechanical Advantage
- Mechanical advantage - The ratio of the output force produced by a machine to the applied input force
- Relate this to “Bio-”
- Essentially what you gain from using a machine
- Therefore in bio terms, what you gain from using your body differently or altering position

## Slide 5

- Biomechanical Advantage
- flexibility  =            biomechanical advantage
- Positional change (assuming plastic deformation)
- Force production change
- Chapter 4
- More sarcomeres therefore more force produced
- However,  research has recently shown increased length DECREASES force production
- Efficiency / aesthetical result

## Slide 6

- Injury
- Tight muscles are more likely to tear
- Relate to stress-strain curve
- More compliant = less susceptible
- Excessive ROM is often not necessary in sport
- Problems associated with it
- Injuries often occur during flexibility training sessions
- Overstretching - relate to stress-strain curve

## Slide 8

- “smoothing out rapid changes” (Alter, 2004) - mediator
- Passive – Primarily tendon
- Active – Cross bridges between actin and myosin

## Slide 10

- Nerve
- Elastic range 6-20% of resting length
- As with any tissue will fail under excessive loading
- Nerves need to slide in their sheaths
- 3 sheaths – epi, peri and endoneurium
- Restriction of this causes ‘Adverse Neural Tension’

## Slide 11

- What can we change?
- Remember there are acute and chronic effects
- Muscular
- Plastic deformation – new resting length
- Creep – constant length held leads to lengthening
- Stress-relaxation – constant length held leads to relaxation
- Neural
- Decreased reflexes
- acute i.e. autogenic inhibition – GTO overriding muscle spindles
- Decreased Hoffmann reflex (H-reflex)
- Spinal cord plasticity (CNS)
- Spinal cord can be altered through reflexes and learned behaviour
- Reducing the stretch reflex should allow muscle to stretch further

## Slide 12

- Stress-Strain curve
- Soft tissues can be altered
- Stress strain relationship
- The stiffer the structure the more force needed to elongate it
- Should we stretch at end of elastic or into plastic range?

## Slide 13

- What can we change?
- Bony changes are not likely after age 11 (Bennell and Khan, 2001)
- Maximal flexibility at age 10-11 then slowly decreasing (Valdivia, 2008)
- Milne and Mierau (1979) related school sitting position to tight hamstrings
- Reflexes associated with flexibility were decreased in dancers (Nielsen et al., 1993)
- Caused by “prolonged co-contraction during ballet positions”
- Change in composition leading to a tendency to stay in a prolonged state
- Modified titin within the muscle fibres
- Modified myosin chain
- Increased compliance and decreased cross-bridges

## Slide 14

- Age related changes
- During growth spurts bones grow quicker than muscle
- Increased tension leads to decreased flexibility
- Adolescence
- Tension causes new sarcomeres
- Muscle changes
- As we age our muscle atrophy
- Replaced by fatty and fibrous tissue leading to stiffness
- Decrease in muscle length with age (Gajdosik, 1997 in Alter, 2004)
- Collagen changes
- Increases rigidity by various methods – Alter (2004, p.41)
