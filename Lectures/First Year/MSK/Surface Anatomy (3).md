## Slide 4

- Gluteal Region: Surface Anatomy
- The gluteal region, commonly referred to as the buttocks, is an anatomical area located on the posterior aspect of the pelvis.
- Boundaries of the Gluteal Region
- Superior: Iliac crest (the upper border of the hip bone).
- Inferior: Gluteal fold (the crease formed where the buttocks meet the upper thigh).
- Medial: Sacrum and coccyx (midline structures of the lower spine).
- Lateral: Greater trochanter of the femur (the bony prominence on the side of the hip).

## Slide 5

- Gluteal Region
- The inferior border of the gluteus maximus muscle forms the gluteal fold.
- The gluteal (natal) cleft extends vertically to separate the buttocks into two prominences.
- In the inferior portion of each buttock, an ischial tuberosity can be palpated; these tuberosities support body weight while seated.
- The gluteus maximus muscle forms most of the inferolateral “fleshy” part of the buttock.
- The gluteus medius muscle may be palpated only in the superolateral portion of each buttock.

## Slide 8

- The Iliac Crests
- The iliac crest forms the superior border of the ilium and the superior lateral margin of the greater pelvis.
- Palpation:
- Ask your model to lie on the side
- Start from the posterior superior iliac spine.
- Trace anteriorly to the anterior superior iliac spine.

## Slide 9

- Iliac Crest: Clinical Relevance
- Surface Landmark for Anatomical Assessments
- Alignment in pelvic examinations.
- Reference point for identifying other structures, such as the L4-L5 vertebral level.
- Landmark in imaging studies (X-rays, CT scans, and MRIs).

## Slide 10

- Iliac Crest: Clinical Relevance
- Muscle Attachments
- The iliac crest serves as the attachment site for several important muscles, including:
- Abdominal muscles (External oblique, Internal oblique, Transversus abdominis)
- Back muscles (Erector spinae, Latissimus dorsi)
- Hip stabilizers (Tensor fasciae latae, Gluteus medius, and Gluteus maximus)

## Slide 11

- Posterior Superior Iliac Spine (PSIS)
- Found on the posterior border of the ilium.
- Identified by dimples above the gluteal muscles.
- Serves as an attachment for:
- Posterior sacroiliac ligaments
- Multifidus muscle
- Consistently crosses the spinous process of S2, making it a reliable lumbar spine landmark.

## Slide 12

- The Ischial Tuberosities
- Known as "sitting bones."
- Two large bony protrusions on the inferior margin of the ischium.
- Best palpated with the patient prone or seated upright with relaxed gluteal muscles.
- Mark the lateral boundary of the pelvic outlet.

## Slide 13

- Palpating the Ischial Tuberosity
- Positioning the Patient:
- Prone Position: Have the patient lie face down on an examination table with legs extended. This position allows for easy access to the ischial tuberosities.
- Locating the Ischial Tuberosity:
- Start at the Gluteal Fold: Place your hands just above the crease where the buttocks meet the upper thigh (gluteal fold).
- Palpate Upward: Move your fingers upward and slightly inward towards the midline to feel the bony prominence deep beneath the gluteal muscles.
- Apply firm pressure to reach the ischial tuberosity, especially in patients with thicker soft tissue.
- Palpation Technique:
- Use the pads of your fingers to apply firm but gentle pressure.
- The ischial tuberosity will feel like a large, round bony prominence.
- Ask the patient to relax their gluteal muscles to make the palpation easier.
- For a seated assessment, place your hand under the patient’s buttock while they are seated upright, and gently press upwards.

## Slide 14

- Clinical Considerations
- Hamstring Tendinopathy: overuse hamstring injury due to hamstrings attachment
- Ischial Bursitis: inflammation
- Avulsion Fracture: forceful contractions of the hamstrings.
- Conditions Assessed with Ischial Tuberosity Palpation
- Sciatica: Differential diagnosis with hamstring or ischial bursitis.
- Sitting Bone Pain: Assessing for tenderness in patients who report pain when sitting for extended periods.

## Slide 15

- Slide 5: The Greater Trochanter
- Large projection on the proximal femur for muscle attachments.
- Located lateral to the hip joint, palpable about 10 cm below the iliac crest.
- Can be felt during hip rotation.
- Important anatomical landmark and attachment site for several gluteal muscles and piriformis,

## Slide 16

- Palpating the Greater Trochanter
- Positioning the Patient:
- Side-Lying Position: This position exposes the greater trochanter for easier palpation.
- Locating the Greater Trochanter:
- Landmarks: Begin by palpating the iliac crest and moving your hand down the lateral side of the thigh.
- The greater trochanter is located approximately 10 cm below the iliac crest and can be felt as a large bony prominence on the outer aspect of the hip.
- Palpation Technique:
- Use the pads of your fingers (index and middle fingers) to apply firm pressure over the lateral aspect of the hip.
- Feel for the rounded contour of the greater trochanter. It should feel like a solid, smooth bump beneath the skin.
- To confirm the location, ask the patient to internally and externally rotate their hip (turning the leg inwards and outwards) while keeping the knee bent. You should feel the greater trochanter move beneath your fingers during rotation.

## Slide 17

- Conditions Assessed with Greater Trochanter Palpation
- Trochanteric Pain Syndrome: Characterized by pain over the lateral hip, often linked to bursitis, tendinopathy, or muscle tears.
- Hip Fractures: In elderly patients, tenderness over the greater trochanter may indicate a femoral fracture, especially after a fall.
- Hip Osteoarthritis: Palpation of the greater trochanter can help differentiate pain from joint-related issues versus soft tissue or bursal involvement.
- Goniometry: Important landmark for the fulcrum in Hip ROM measurements

## Slide 19

- Medial and Lateral Femoral Condyles
- Large rounded projections at the end of the femur forming articulating surfaces with the tibia.
- The medial condyle is larger than the lateral one.
- Joint line of the knee can be felt as a soft depression on either side of the patella.

## Slide 20

- Medial Femur Condyles: Palpation
- Patient Position:
- Position the patient in supine (lying on their back) or sitting with the knee flexed at approximately 90 degrees to relax surrounding soft tissues and allow better access to the joint structures.
- Locate the Patella:
- Begin by identifying the patella (kneecap) as a central reference point on the anterior knee.
- Medial Femoral Condyle:
- Move your fingers to the medial side of the knee, just proximal to the joint line.
- Palpate the medial femoral condyle, a prominent bony structure on the inner aspect of the distal femur.
- To confirm, ask the patient to flex and extend their knee gently. You should feel the condyle moving as the femur articulates with the tibia.

## Slide 21

- Lateral Femur Condyle: Palpation
- Lateral Femoral Condyle:
- Shift your palpation to the lateral side of the knee, just above the joint line.
- Palpate the lateral femoral condyle, which is slightly less prominent than the medial condyle.
- Again, flex and extend the knee to feel the movement of the lateral condyle during articulation.
- Comparison:
- Compare the size, shape, and prominence of the medial and lateral condyles.

## Slide 24

- Anterior Superior Iliac Spine (ASIS)
- The ASIS is the anterior extremity of the iliac crest.
- Approximately 30 cm apart; slightly wider in females.
- Provides attachment for:
- Inguinal ligament
- Sartorius muscle
- Tensor fasciae latae

## Slide 25

- Palpation of the ASIS
- Positioning the Patient:
- Model lies supine (on their back) on a flat surface.
- Ensure the model is relaxed to allow easier palpation of the pelvic structures.
- Identifying the ASIS:
- From the Iliac Crest, move your fingers forward along the iliac crest toward the front of the pelvis.
- The ASIS is the prominent bony point you will feel at the anterior end of the iliac crest.

## Slide 26

- The Patella
- Steps for Palpation
- Position the model:
- Place the model in a supine position (lying on their back) with the knee fully extended and relaxed. For better palpation of the patella's mobility, the quadriceps must be relaxed.
- Locate the Patella:
- Identify the patella at the anterior aspect of the knee. It is a flat, movable bone that can be easily felt beneath the skin.
- Assess the Superior and Inferior Borders:
- Run your fingers along the superior border of the patella, where the quadriceps tendon inserts.
- Move to the inferior border, where the patellar ligament connects the patella to the tibial tuberosity.
- Palpate the Medial and Lateral Borders:
- Use your fingers to feel the medial border of the patella, noting any tenderness or irregularities.
- Similarly, palpate the lateral border, checking for asymmetry, pain, or bony protrusions.
