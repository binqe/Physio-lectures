## Page 11

Avulsion 
Injuries
Acute avulsion injury results from extreme, unbalanced and often 
eccentric muscular contractions, and patients with such injuries 
present with severe pain and loss of function. 
Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to avulsion injuries because 
of the inherent weakness of the apophyses. The many apophyses in 
the pelvis and hip are common sites of avulsion injuries. The single 
most common site of apophyseal avulsion is at the ischial tuberosity. 
Cheerleaders, sprinters, gymnasts, track athletes, American football 
players, and baseball players are commonly affected. 
Treatment for avulsion injury is generally conservative and the 
prognosis is good, but non-union may occur.

Tendon injuries

• If load continues – gross failure
• Tendon stress is from muscle contraction
• Maximal contraction with rapid eccentric load exceeds 
norm (and no reflex relaxation)
• Tendon strength is around twice that of the attaching 
muscle – hence muscle ruptures more common

Diagnosis
• Tendinosis can be difficult to differentiate from partial 
tears
• History is a key factor 
• Pain at rest – becomes less painful with use, run through pain, 
returns after, tenderness and thickening, crepitus
• Sudden pain during explosive activity, had to slow, palpable 
inflam location
• Acute or chronic onset?
• Location may aid diagnosis (e.g. tenosynovitis usually 
near bone prominance)
• Palpation and passive movement
• MRI/CT/USS can be useful but also limited

Tenosynovitis / Tenovaginitis
• Tenosynovitis is a broad term describing the inflammation of 
the fluid-filled synovium within the tendon sheath. Can be 
have overuse, autoimmune,  idiopathic, and infective causes. 
• Difficulty moving the affected joint.
• Tenderness of the tendon sheath
• Enlargement of the affected tendon in a symmetric pattern
• Baseline contracture of the affected tendon
• Pain with passive straightening of the tendon

## Page 38

Rehabilitation:
• A study by Jonsson et al. (2008) demonstrated the 
effectiveness of eccentric exercise in the 
treatment of Achilles tendinopathy.
• A systematic review by Malliaras et al. (2013) 
found eccentric exercise to be effective in 
reducing pain and improving function in patients 
with patellar tendinopathy.
• Eccentric exercises involve lengthening the 
muscle-tendon unit under load, which can 
stimulate collagen synthesis and improve tendon 
strength.

## Page 39

Rehabilitation:
Return to Activity Guidelines
• Gradual return to activity is necessary to 
prevent re-injury and ensure safe rehabilitation 
of the tendon.
• A study by Silbernagel et al. (2017) emphasized 
the importance of: 
• a progressive exercise program
• functional testing
• patient education in facilitating a safe and 
successful return to sport
• Clear guidelines for activity modification, 
workload management, and monitoring of 
symptoms help individuals transition back to 
their desired level of activity.

## Page 40

Rehabilitation:
• Preventing tendon injuries requires addressing 
risk factors, maintaining proper biomechanics, 
and implementing injury prevention strategies.
• Coping with a tendon injury and the associated 
physical limitations can have psychological 
implications for individuals undergoing 
rehabilitation.
• Educating patients on the recovery process, 
setting realistic goals, and providing emotional 
support can aid in managing the psychological 
impact of tendon injuries.
• A study by Ardern et al. (2016) emphasized the 
importance of addressing psychosocial factors 
in rehabilitation to optimize outcomes and 
enhance patient well-being.

Achilles Tendon Loading Protocol
Symptom-management and load-reduction phase: wk 1 to 2 (or longer if needed) 
• Patient status 
• Pain and difficulty with all activities, difficulty performing 10 one-legged heel rises 
• Loading intensity 
• Progress loading up to 100% body weight with slow, controlled motion. If needed, begin with 
aquatic therapy, body-weight support, or isometric plantar flexion. 
• Goals 
• Start to exercise and understand nature of the injury and how to use the pain-monitoring 
model 
• Treatment program: 
• Perform exercise once a day 
• Pain-monitoring model information and advice on exercise activity 
• Circulation exercise (moving foot up or down) 
• Two-legged heel rises standing on the floor (3 x 10 to 15 repetitions) 
• One-legged heel rises standing on the floor (3 x 10 repetitions) 
• Eccentric heel rises standing on the floor (3 x 10 repetitions) 
• Sitting heel rises (3 x 1 O repetitions) 
Silbernagel, K. G., Hanlon, S., & Sprague, A. (2020). Current clinical concepts: conservative 
management of Achilles tendinopathy. Journal of athletic training, 55(5), 438-447.

Achilles Tendon Loading Protocol
• Rebuilding phase: wk 3 to 12 (or longer if needed)
• If pain at the distal insertion of the tendon, continue standing on the floor 
• Patient status
• Tolerates the recovery phase exercise program well, no pain at the distal tendon 
insertion, possibly decreased or increased morning stiffness
• Loading intensity
• Continue to progress external resistance and initiate plyometric exercises according to 
patient tolerance. Goals
• Heavier strength training, increase or start running or jumping activity
• Treatment program: Perform exercises every day and with heavier load 2 to 3x/wk
• One-legged heel rises standing on edge of step with added weight (3 x 15 
repetitions) Eccentric heel rises standing on edge of step with added weight (3 x 15 
repetitions) Sitting heel rises (3 x 15 repetitions)
• Quick-rebounding heel rises (3 x 20 repetitions) Plyometrics training (sport 
specific)

management of Achilles tendinopathy. Journal of athletic training, 55(5), 438-447.

## Page 50

Anti-inflammatories & Steroid injections?
• In the past, anti-inflammatories and steroid injections were commonly used to alleviate symptoms associated 
with tendinopathy. Is this effective?
• Anti-inflammatories, such as ibuprofen and naproxen, were traditionally believed to reduce inflammation and 
pain associated with tendinopathy. In fact, chronic use of anti-inflammatories may actually hinder the natural 
healing process of the tendon by reducing collagen synthesis and weakening the tendon structure.
• Research has shown that steroids may weaken the tendon structure and increase the risk of tendon rupture. 
Additionally, steroids may only provide temporary relief and do not address the underlying cause of tendinopathy.
• While anti-inflammatories and steroid injections may provide short-term relief for symptoms of tendinopathy, 
they are not recommended as long-term treatments. Physiotherapy, eccentric exercise, and other conservative 
treatments have been shown to be more effective in promoting tendon healing and preventing further injury.
