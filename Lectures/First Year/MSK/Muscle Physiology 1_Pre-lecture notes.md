## Slide 11

- Structure of a myofibril / sarcomere

**Notes:** Z-discs (Z-lines): These are protein structures that bisect the sarcomere, marking the boundaries between adjacent sarcomeres. They anchor the thin filaments (actin) and provide structural stability.
H-zone: This is the central region of the sarcomere where only myosin filaments are present. During muscle contraction, the H-zone narrows as the actin filaments slide inward.
A-band: The A-band is the dark region of the sarcomere that contains both actin and myosin filaments. It does not change in length during muscle contraction.
I-band: The I-band is the light region on either side of the A-band. It contains only actin filaments and gets narrower during muscle contraction.
M-line: This is a protein structure in the center of the sarcomere that helps hold the myosin filaments in place.

## Slide 14

- The crossbridge cycle
- Binding of myosin to actin
- Power stroke
- Rigor (low energy state)
- Unbinding of myosin and actin
- Cocking of myosin head (high energy state)

**Notes:** Binding of Myosin to Actin:
In a resting muscle, the myosin heads are in a low-energy state, with an ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi) molecule bound to them.
When a nerve signal triggers a muscle contraction, calcium ions are released, which expose the binding sites on the actin filaments.
The myosin heads then bind to these exposed sites on the actin, forming a cross-bridge.
Power Stroke:
After binding, the myosin head pivots, pulling the actin filament toward the center of the sarcomere. This movement is known as the power stroke.
As a result, the Z-discs move closer together, and the sarcomere shortens.
Rigor (Low Energy State):
After the power stroke, the myosin head is in a rigor state, tightly bound to actin.
In this state, ADP and Pi are still attached to the myosin head, but the myosin can't release the actin filament on its own.
Unbinding of Myosin and Actin:
To detach from actin, a new ATP molecule binds to the myosin head, causing it to detach from the actin binding site.
ATP binding also triggers a conformational change in the myosin head, which readies it for the next cycle.
Cocking of Myosin Head (High Energy State):
ATP is split into ADP and Pi, providing energy for the myosin head. This energy is used to "cock" or reposition the myosin head into a high-energy state.
This high-energy state positions the myosin head in a way that it's ready to bind to actin again once calcium ions are present and binding sites are exposed.

## Slide 22

- Twitch contraction

**Notes:** Nervous Stimulation: A twitch occurs when a nerve impulse (action potential) stimulates a motor neuron, which in turn signals a specific muscle fiber to contract.
Phases of a Twitch:
Latent Phase: This is the brief moment between the nerve impulse and the onset of muscle contraction. It represents the time taken for the action potential to travel along the nerve and initiate the muscle response.
Contraction Phase: This is when the muscle fiber actively shortens and generates force.
Relaxation Phase: After the contraction, the muscle fiber returns to its original length, and tension decreases.
Strength and Duration: The strength and duration of a twitch are relatively constant and do not vary greatly under normal physiological conditions.

## Slide 24

- Frequency and Summation

**Notes:** Summation and tetanus are processes by which muscle fibers can produce sustained contractions. 
They occur when muscle fibers receive repeated nerve impulses in a short period of time.

Summation: This occurs when a single motor neuron repeatedly sends action potentials in quick succession to a muscle fiber. If the impulses are frequent enough, the muscle fiber doesn't have enough time to completely relax between contractions. As a result, the force of each contraction adds up, leading to a more sustained, stronger contraction.

Incomplete Tetanus: In this state, the muscle fiber is stimulated at a rapid rate, but there is still a partial relaxation between contractions. The result is a sustained but not entirely steady level of muscle tension.
Complete Tetanus (Fused Tetanus): When the stimulation frequency is so high that the muscle fiber doesn't have time to relax at all between contractions, a complete tetanus occurs. The contractions fuse together, resulting in a sustained, smooth, and maximal level of muscle tension.
Physiological Tetanus: This is a sustained muscle contraction that occurs during normal, everyday activities. It's the result of repeated motor neuron signaling to muscle fibers.
Pathological Tetanus: This is an uncontrolled, sustained muscle contraction often caused by the toxin produced by the bacterium Clostridium tetani. It leads to severe muscle rigidity.

## Slide 28

- Measurement Methods
- Isometric Muscle Strength Testing
- Isokinetic Muscle Testing
- Handheld Dynamometry
- Manual Muscle Testing (MMT)
- Electromyography (EMG)
- Muscle Biopsy
- Ultrasound Imaging
- Force Plate Analysis

**Notes:** ometric Muscle Strength Testing:
In this method, the muscle contracts against an immovable resistance. It's commonly used to measure static strength and assess the maximum force a muscle or muscle group can generate at a specific joint angle.
Isokinetic Muscle Testing:
Isokinetic testing involves dynamic movements against a constant speed of resistance. It measures both concentric (muscle shortening) and eccentric (muscle lengthening) strength. Specialized isokinetic machines are used for this purpose.
Handheld Dynamometry:
This involves using a handheld device (dynamometer) to measure muscle force. It's particularly useful for assessing muscle strength in a clinical setting, as it's portable and allows for quick measurements.
Manual Muscle Testing (MMT):
MMT is a subjective assessment conducted by a trained examiner. It involves applying resistance against a specific muscle group's movement to grade its strength on a scale (e.g., from 0 to 5).
Electromyography (EMG):
EMG measures the electrical activity of muscles during contraction. Electrodes are placed on the skin or inserted into the muscle to record and analyze the muscle's electrical signals. This method provides insights into muscle activation patterns and timing.
Muscle Biopsy:
A muscle biopsy involves the removal of a small sample of muscle tissue for examination under a microscope. This method is used to study muscle fiber type composition, structure, and potential abnormalities.
Ultrasound Imaging:
Ultrasound can be used to assess muscle size, architecture, and composition. It's non-invasive and provides real-time images of muscle tissue.
Force Plate Analysis:
Force plates measure the ground reaction forces produced during movements. This method is used to analyze factors like balance, gait, and the force exerted by muscles during dynamic activities.

## Slide 30

- Recruitment
- MU recruitment occurs in a specific order according to Henneman’s size principle
- Once one MU is activated, all fibres within the MU will contract maximally
- Smallest MUs recruited first (type I)
- Largest MUs recruited last (type II)

**Notes:** Gradation of Muscle Force:
Motor unit recruitment allows for fine control over muscle force production.
Smaller, weaker motor units are recruited first for lighter tasks.
As the demand for force increases, larger, stronger motor units are progressively activated.
All-or-None Principle within Motor Units:
Once a motor unit is activated, all the muscle fibers it innervates contract maximally (the all-or-none principle).
To generate greater force, the nervous system recruits additional motor units.
Precision and Adaptability:
Motor unit recruitment enables precise control of movement and adaptability to varying demands.
Delicate tasks (e.g., typing) use fewer and smaller motor units, while forceful tasks (e.g., lifting) engage more and larger motor units.
