Example
Condition
Rotator Cuff Tear
Pathophysiology
A rotator cuff tear involves damage to one or more of the four rotator cuff tendons (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis).
Common causes include acute trauma, repetitive microtrauma, or age-related degenerative changes.
Degenerative tears are most prevalent in individuals over 50 years old, with prevalence increasing with age (Yamamoto et al., 2010).
Signs and symptoms
Pain, especially during overhead activities or at night.
Weakness in abduction and external rotation.
Limited range of motion (ROM).
Crepitus or clicking sounds during movement.
Clusters of tests for 
diagnose
Evidence suggests using a combination of tests for greater diagnostic accuracy (Hegedus et al., 2012).
1.
Painful Arc Test (Sensitivity: 74%, Specificity: 81%).
2.
Infraspinatus Strength Test (Sensitivity: 62%, Specificity: 73%).
3.
Drop Arm Test (Sensitivity: 44%, Specificity: 92%).
Note: A positive result on all three tests strongly indicates a rotator cuff tear (Park et al., 2005).
Differential Diagnosis
1.
Adhesive Capsulitis: Marked restriction in both active and passive ROM, particularly external rotation.
2.
Subacromial Impingement Syndrome (SIS): Pain during the painful arc test but without significant weakness. Positive Hawkins-Kennedy Test 
combined with a normal Drop Arm Test supports SIS over a tear.
3.
Cervical Radiculopathy: Neck pain radiating to the shoulder, with associated paraesthesia or weakness in the arm. Spurling’s Test and imaging 
(MRI/CT) for confirmation.
Management 
Advise rest in the acute phase. Following this, activity should gradually be increased.
Advise modification of activities that exacerbate symptoms (such as reaching overhead). Usual activities, within the limits of pain, should be restarted as 
soon as possible.
Physiotherapy may include postural correction, motor control retraining, stretching and strengthening of the rotator cuff and scapular muscles, and manual 
therapy.
Low intensity and high-frequency exercise, that is within the person's pain threshold, should be performed.
Physiotherapy can be continued for a further 6 weeks if there is improvement in the person's symptoms or function (Nice Guidelines, 2022).
References
Hegedus, E. J., Cook, C., Wright, A., et al. (2012). Diagnostic accuracy of clinical tests of the shoulder: a systematic review and meta-analysis. BMJ Open.
Yamamoto, A., Takagishi, K., Kobayashi, T., et al. (2010). Prevalence and risk factors of a rotator cuff tear in the general population. J Shoulder Elbow Surg.
Kuhn, J. E., Dunn, W. R., Sanders, R., et al. (2013). 
Park, H. B., Yokota, A., Gill, H. S., & El Rassi, G. (2005). Diagnostic accuracy of clinical tests for the different degrees of subacromial impingement syndrome 
and rotator cuff tears. J Bone Joint Surg. Am.Effectiveness of physical therapy in treating atraumatic full-thickness rotator cuff tears: a multicenter 
prospective cohort study. J Shoulder Elbow Surg.
Nice Guidelines, Rotator Cuff disorders, 2022.
