## Page 5

Nerve injury classifications – Sunderland 
(degrees) and Seddon (types)
Neurapraxia (conduction block) 1st degree
• Mild compression or stretch leading to myelin 
damage but axon is intact (lasts for 2-12wks)
Axonotmesis 2nd degree 
• Arise from stretch injuries resulting to severance of 
axons but leaving endoneurium (2nd), perineurium 
(3rd) or epineurium (4th) intact (1mm/day from site 
of lesion)
Neurotmesis 3rd - 5th degree 
• Complete severance of nerves (nerve graft surgery)

## Page 9

Pathophysiology of nerve Injury
•
Neurapraxia – no pathological changes or mild changes 
are seen
•
Axonotmesis: Wallerian degeneration occurs distal 
•
Nuclear migration & chromatolysis in cell body (can lead 
to apoptosis) 
•
Wallerian degeneration in axon (distal to lesion) - Calcium 
mediated process
•
Physical fragmentation of both axons and myelin – within 
hours 
•
Dissolution and clearance of the myelin sheath and debris 
(hours to days/weeks) 
•
Expression of nerve growth factors 
•
SCs organise into Bands of Bungner to provide pathway for 
newly sprouting axons during regeneration (approx. 2 
months) 
•
Reinnervation and remyelination

## Page 10

Diagnosing a nerve injury
• Electromyography (EMG)
• Should be done after 2 – 3 weeks
• Muscles show denervation changes
• Complete denervation is characterised 
by low amplitude sharp waves or 
fibrillation potential with muscle at rest 
and absent evoked muscle potential
(MUAP)
• With re-innervation, these changes begin 
to reverse.
• Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS)
• Identifying the type and age of peripheral 
nerve injury
• In Neurapraxia  - compound muscle 
action potential (CMAP) remains normal 
distal to the lesion. Drops to zero 
proximal to the lesion
• In Axonotmesis – it is present in first 
week and falls rapidly after Wallerian 
degeneration

## Page 12

Nerve root disorders (radiculopathy)
Causes 
• spondylosis, intervertebral disc disease, trauma, tumours
Symptoms
• Radicular pains: severe, sharp, shooting and/burning pain  
arising from one or more spinal nerve roots (therefore not 
necessarily dermatomal) can occur in isolation
• Sensory loss in dermatomal pattern – arises from pathology of 
either posterior root or spinal nerve
Signs 
• Muscle weakness in myotomal distribution – arises from 
pathology of either anterior root or spinal nerve
• Reduced/absent deep tendon reflexes – arises from pathology of 
anterior, posterior root or spinal nerve

## Page 17

Brachial plexus damage
Symptoms 
• Sensory loss - lateral deltoid, arm, and forearm & thumb (C5 & C6 
dermatomes)
Signs (C5/6) involvement
• Muscle weakness 
• Shoulder abductors & lateral rotators and elbow flexors
• Atrophy – shoulder and arm
• Deformity (waiter’s tip deformity)
• Numbness of the radial digits of the hand and the shoulder
• Triceps, forearm, wrist, and hand strength are typically intact.
Management
• May require surgery to repair or reconstruct

## Page 18

Diagnosing Brachial plexus injury
• Electromyography (EMG) 
• At 6 weeks post injury can show specific pattern of root involvement
• Can also show signs of recovery if present
• At 3 months, surgical planning is necessary if no signs of recovery
• Nerve conduction testing
• To identify the location of the injury
• MRI/CT myelography
• MRI scanning and myelography of the cervical spine with or without CT 
imaging has been used to diagnose nerve root avulsion
BMJ  Best Practice

Examples of brachial plexus injuries
A 24-year-old man presents 6 weeks after a 
motorcycle accident having sustained 
polytrauma to the right upper extremity. Three 
ipsilateral rib fractures are present and 
humeral and forearm fractures are also noted. 
The patient has 0/5 Medical Research Council 
(MRC) motor scale strength in the entire right 
upper extremity. There is sensory loss in the C4 
through to T1 dermatomes.
There is retention of sensibility in the posterior 
arm.
A 32-year-old man presents to the emergency 
department with a history of having fallen from 
a 10-foot platform at work onto the left 
shoulder, forcing the head and neck away from 
the injured shoulder and stretching the neck on 
the left side.
 He has no shoulder or biceps function but 
retains strong triceps function as well as intact 
functional strength below the elbow. 
He has patchy loss of sensibility over the 
shoulder and forearm as well as the thumb and 
index finger.

Peripheral Nerve injuries
Peripheral nerve lesion (injury): 
• laceration, 
• severe bruising, 
• gunshot, 
• traction, 
• injection, 
• electrical injury
Signs & symptoms
• severe unrelenting pain 
• sensory loss & paraesthesia
• Paralysis/muscle weakness with absent/hyporeflexia
• atrophy
• deformity
• autonomic
