## Page 4

General Information about balance disorders
• According to the American Medical Association, 1 in every 4 patients suffers from some 
level of balance dysfunction.
• 40% of over 65 years old have balance disorders sufficient to put them at risk of falling
• Approximately 2 million physician visits per year involve chief complaints of dizziness or 
loss of balance
• In 2001 alone, 1.6 million older adults were treated in emergency rooms for fall related 
injuries

Client groups which

Rehabilitation

Impairments associated with balance dysfunction
• ↓ muscle strength
• ↓ muscle power
• ↓ muscle endurance
• ↓ coordination
• Disordered sensory and 
perceptual processes
• Cognitive dysfunction
• Executive function impairment
• Adaptive changes in muscle length 
following immobility and disuse – reduced 
range of motion (ROM)
• Impaired motor control 
• Visual, tactile, proprioceptive and 
vestibular impairments
• Damage of higher centres e.g. Basal 
ganglia
These impairments contribute to ↑ incidence of falls

## Page 13

Evidence for 
muscle 
strengthening 
• Many studies have shown that resistance training is 
effective in improving strength in older adults, and in 
some instances associated with improvement in balance 
and not in other studies .
• Systematic review  (Orr et al. 2008) of RCTs to evaluate the 
effect of progressive resistance training on balance
• 29 studies
• 14 found improvements in balance
• Inconsistent results suggest that resistance training 
alone is not sufficient to improve balance control in 
older adults
• Similar mixed results in neurological population
• Improvement in power but no improvement in 
postural control (anterior posterior sway) (Debolt & 
McCubbin 2004)

## Page 16

Postural strategies: Retraining 
postural alignment
• Goal: to help patient develop an initial position that is:
• Appropriate
• Efficient with respect to gravity
• Maximizes stability that places the line of gravity within a patients 
limits of stability
• Verbal and manual cues, mirrors, and kinetic or force 
feedback devices have been used to retrain vertical 
posture
• Patients practice with eyes open and closed – learning to 
maintain symmetrical posture without visual cues.

## Page 17

Evidence on 
use of 
feedback on 
symmetrical 
posture?
• Barclay –Goddard et al. (2004) – systematic 
review examined the use of force-plate 
biofeedback to improve standing balance
• Found that force biofeedback resulted in patients 
standing more symmetrically but did NOT result in 
improved balance during functional activities.
• Van Peppen et al. (2006) – systematic review 
• found a non-significant effect in favour of visual 
feedback on weight distribution, postural sway, 
balance, and gait speed in stroke patients
• Concluded there is no difference between visual 
feedback and conventional therapy.

Movement Strategies
• Goal: To help the patient develop co-
ordinated multi-joint  movement that 
are effective in meeting the demands 
for postural control in sitting and in 
standing
• Includes:
• Strategies that move the COG relative 
to a stationary base of support (in-
place strategies)
• Strategies that change the base of 
support relative to COG (changing 
base of support strategies)
• Strategies that train anticipatory 
balance control

• Approach
• Use external perturbations

• Small perturbations (in –place)

• Mixed use of perturbations
• Multiple directions
• Eyes open, closed
• Compliant and inclined surfaces

## Page 20

Research 
Evidence on 
re-training 
movement 
strategies
• There is evidence that training of reactive 
balance in neurological patients improve the 
organisation, and timing of postural responses
• Marigold et al (2005) found improvements in the onset 
latency of paretic lower extremity muscles in response to 
platform perturbations in people with chronic stroke after 10 
weeks of training.
• Gatts & Woollacott (2006 & 2007) found that three weeks of 
intense Tai-Chi training significantly enhanced balance 
responses by improving the organisation and timing of 
stepping strategies of the swing leg.

## Page 21

Retraining Anticipatory Balance Control
• Patients with anticipatory balance control 
problems often are very unsteady or 
extremely fearful of falling
• Indication for anticipatory postural 
adjustment
•
Inability to move to perform a task
•
Inability to shift weight
Approach
•
Patient can practice controlling centre of gravity in all 
directions, over larger areas at varying speed.
•
Example of movement include reaching, lifting, and 
throwing.
•
Progression can be achieved by increasing:
•
Task demands
•
Speed, effort, degree of external support and task 
complexity
•
Patients can practice movement in:
•
Parallel bars
•
Standing close to a wall
•
In a corner with a chair or table in front
•
Therapist can modify the home environment for continuing 
practice outside of therapy time.

## Page 23

Research 
Evidence for 
anticipatory 
postural 
adjustments
• Garland et al. 2003
• Used surface EMG to investigate the timing of muscle 
activity in the lower extremity relative to the initiation 
of arm movement in an arm raising task in patient with 
stroke
• Before rehabilitation, muscles of the paretic lower limb 
were activated after the initiation of the arm 
movement rather than before.
• After 1 month rehabilitation, arm movements were 
faster,  and recruitment of leg muscles faster.
• 10 out 27 patients showed activation of anticipatory 
muscle activities in the hemiparetic limb
• 12 showed no change in the timing of muscle activity

## Page 24

Sensory strategy
• Goal: help patient learn to effectively co-
ordinate and select appropriate sensory 
information for balance
• Approach: Patient required to maintain 
balance during progressively more difficult 
static and dynamic movement tasks while the 
clinician systematically vary the availability 
and accuracy of one or more senses for 
orientation
• Surface dependent
• Perform tasks while sitting or standing on surfaces 
with decreased somatosensory cues for balance: 
• Carpet, foam, pillows, moving surfaces wobble 
boards.
• Visual dependent 
• Perform tasks where visual cues are absent (eyes 
closed, blindfolded) or reduced (blinders, 
diminished lighting).
• Visual cues can also be made inaccurate through 
use of glasses smeared with petroleum jelly, prism 
glasses, moving curtains with stripes, moving 
visual scenes.
• Enhance ability to use vestibular 
information
• Exercises where one must maintain balance while 
both visual and somatosensory inputs are 
simultaneously reduced, such as standing on foam 
or a wobble board with eyes closed or moving 
visual scene

Cognitive strategy
• ↑ instability and falls during 
performance of multiple tasks in both 
neurological and geriatric populations
• Training balance under dual task 
conditions addresses this.
• Approach: Practicing tasks requiring 
static, anticipatory and reactive balance 
whilst simultaneously varying the 
cognitive demands through secondary 
tasks
• Secondary Tasks:
• Spell words forward/backwards
• Name any words starting with letters A to K
• Name any words starting with letters L to Z
• Remember prices (e.g. bill 
payment/shopping)
• Count backward in 3s or 7s or 5s
• Remember words
• Visual imaginary task (tell road directions 
from home to hospital)
• Recount daily activities

Intervention at the 
functional level
• Goal: Train ability to adapt balance strategy 
to changing tasks and environmental demands
• Patient with neurologic damage may have 
difficulties in perceiving the relevant factors 
within a specific task or environment
• Practicing balance in a wide variety of tasks 
and environment is required for optimal 
recovery

## Page 31

Research evidence for task-oriented balance 
training
• Van Peppen et al. (2004):https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15609840/
• Systematic review found task-oriented training to restore balance and gait, and for 
strengthening for the lower paretic limb
• Wevers et al. (2009): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19461035/ 
• 6 studies were included and found task-oriented training  improved walking distance, gait 
speed and timed-up and go.
• Cano-Manas et al. (2020): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32148744/ 
• Randomised control trial (conventional training + video-based balance training vs 
conventional training alone, found between group improvements in Tinetti gait assessment, 
functional reach test, get up and go test.
