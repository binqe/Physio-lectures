## Page 4

Balance: What is it?
Balance or Postural stability is the ability to maintain the body in equilibrium.
A body is in equilibrium either when it is at rest (static equilibrium) or when it is in steady-state 
motion (dynamic equilibrium)
An object is considered stable or in  equilibrium when the centre of gravity is over its base of 
support

What defines the limits of stability
1. Position of line of gravity within the base of support: Maximal stability when line of gravity passes in the 
centre of the base of support
2. Area of BOS: Larger base = more stable
3. Height of COG: Lower COG = Greater stability
4. Body Mass: Greater mass = Greater Stability Why? Force = mass x acceleration
5. Velocity of COG: Slow sway = Greater stability
•
Slow sway oscillations (front to back or side to side and back again last 2 to 3s) gravity is only significant destabilising force that 
must be overcome.
•
Rapid sway oscillations (1s or less) momentum of body is additional destabilising force

## Page 15

Balance Impairments
From clinical perspective a patient may have impaired balance because:
1.
The position of the COG relative to the base of support is not accurately sensed
2.
The automatic movements required to bring the COG to a balanced position are not 
timely or effectively coordinated
3.
Musculoskeletal injury/ Insufficient muscle strength/damage to individual sensory 
systems
4.
Combination of the above.

Balance: Static vs Dynamic
Static balance
• Ability to hold the body in a specific 
position and posture e.g., lying, sitting, 
standing postures
• Body remains stationary and COG is over 
base of support
• Co-ordinated muscle contractions are 
required to ensure stable equilibrium.

## Page 18

Dynamic Balance - Gait
• Many dynamic movements including 
walking and running are UNSTABLE 
• Walking and running essentially 
consist of a series of interrupted falls
• Before there has been time to fall 
base of support is moved so
equilibrium is restored

## Page 45

Cognitive and balance control
• Specific cognitive domains are associated with fear of falling in older adults and 
people with neurological conditions
• Slower gait speed, shorter stride length, increased stride width, and prolonged double 
limb support time have been found to be associated with fear of falling in older adults 
and people with neurological conditions
• Growing body of literature investigating the effect of cognitive and/or balance training 
on cognitive function domains and postural and gait parameters with varying results
