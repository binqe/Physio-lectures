## Slide 3

- Burden of Disease
- Burden of disease refers to the impact disease has on mortality and morbidity
- Burden of disease can be measured by a metric called Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs), which measures lost health because of premature death, disease or disability
- Global Burden of Disease Study has been collecting data on disease burden since 1990 from over 156 countries and involving over 7000 researchers

## Slide 5

- Global Differences in Disease Cause
- In low-income nations, communicable disease still accounts for more than 60% across many countries
- In high-income nations, NCDs typically account for more than 80% of disease burden whereas communicable diseases are low (<5%)

## Slide 6

- Global Burden of Disease
- Over time, there has been a shift in burden towards NCDs due to reductions in communicable disease, improvements in overall health, living standards and incomes, as well as an ageing population
- At a global level the most common causes of disease burden are:
- cardiovascular diseases (15%)
- cancer (9%)
- neonatal disorders (7%)
- musculoskeletal disorders (6%)
- substance use disorders (5%)

## Slide 7

- Levels of Disease Prevention
- Primary
- Secondary
- Tertiary

## Slide 8

- Primary Prevention
- primary prevention aims to prevent disease before it occurs
- this is achieved by preventing exposures to hazards that cause disease or altering unhealthy behaviours
- Examples include
- regular health check ups for hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, and diabetes
- medication to address known risk factors in high risk groups
- education about healthy lifestyle

## Slide 9

- Secondary Prevention
- secondary prevention aims to reduce the impact of a disease that has already occurred and prevent disease recurrence
- this is achieved by detecting and treating disease as soon as possible to halt or slow its progress and strategies to prevent disease recurrence
- Examples include
- antiplatelet medication after ischaemic stroke
- lifestyle modification, such as healthy diet and smoking cessation
- six monthly post-stroke and annual stroke reviews

## Slide 10

- Tertiary Prevention
- tertiary prevention aims to reduce the impact of an ongoing illness or injury that has lasting effects
- this is achieved by helping people manage long-term illnesses to improve their function, quality of life and life expectancy
- Examples include:
- stroke rehabilitation delivered across the stroke pathway
- support groups to share strategies for living well

## Slide 14

- Non-Modifiable Risk Factors

**Notes:** Age: most powerful independent risk factor 
Gender: females more likely to have a stroke due to increased life expectancy and increased stroke risk with increasing age, males more likely < 55 years
Race: increased stroke in Black African/Caribbean populations, similarly Chinese and Japanese populations have increased risk of haemorrhagic stroke 
Family history: increased risk if a first-degree relative has had coronary heart disease or stroke before the age of 55 years (for a male relative) or 65 years (for a female relative) 
Previous stroke: the risk of recurrent stroke is greatest after first stroke; 2–3% of survivors of a first stroke have another stroke within the first 30 days, 9% in the first 6 months and 10–16% within a year; after the first year, the average annual risk of recurrent stroke for the next 4 years falls to about 5%

## Slide 15

- Risk Factor Management- Hypertension
- 28% relative risk reduction of recurrent stroke with antihypertensive drug use
- optimal target BP for patients with established cardiovascular disease is <135/85mmHg
- Benefits
- 33-46% reduction in fatal or disabling stroke
- 50-76% reduction in the risk of cerebral haemorrhage
- 38-42% reduction in non-fatal myocardial infarction

## Slide 17

- Risk Factor Management- Smoking
- Smokers have 2 – 4 times the risk of stroke compared to non-smokers
- Risk of stroke is similar to non-smoker 5 years after stopping smoking

## Slide 18

- Risk Factor Management- Lipid Control
- people with ischaemic stroke or TIA should be treated with a statin drug unless contraindicated to ensure:
- total cholesterol <4.0 mmol/L
- LDL-cholesterol <1.8 mmol/L
- people with intracerebral haemorrhage should be assessed for lipid-lowering therapy on the basis of their overall cardiovascular risk and the underlying cause of the haemorrhage
