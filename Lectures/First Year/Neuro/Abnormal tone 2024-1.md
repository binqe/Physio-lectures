What is muscle tone?
• When the clinician passively moves a 
joint in a normal, relaxed limb, a certain 
resistance, known as tone, is detected. 
•
inertia of the limb
•
viscoelastic properties of the muscle and 
joint
•
No neural response to stretch  (Burke, 
1998)
Hypotonia (↓ resistance)
• resistance decreases whenever the 
reflex arc is damaged (usually at the 
level of the peripheral motor or sensory 
nerve), but it may also decrease with 
primary muscle or spinal cord disease. 
Hypertonia (↑ resistance)
• increase in resistance to stretch in one 
direction. It often occurs with the 
presence of a lesion of the upper motor 
neurons anywhere along the pathway 
from the motor cortex to the ventral 
horn neurons in the spinal cord. There 
may be neural and non-neural 
contributory impairments

Voluntary activation
• Voluntary activation is the neural drive to 
the motor unit pool supplying a particular 
muscle.
• Even in healthy individuals, full activation is 
rare (usually approx. 95%)
• Measuring voluntary activation using the 
“interpolated twitch” method during 
maximal voluntary isometric contractions 
(MVIC)
• Size of the interpolated twitch can be 
compared with the post-MVIC resting twitch 
to calculate activation failure

Spastic Hypertonia: non neural component
• Not every “tight” muscle is spastic
• Can have increased resistance to passive stretch without spasticity
• Mechanical and morphological adaptive changes:
• Increased cross-bridge connections during contraction
• Shortened muscle fires - loss of sarcomeres
• Build-up of connective tissue due to lack of contractile activity
• Altered muscle fibre properties

RCP Guidelines: impaired tone - spasticity
• Patients with motor weakness should be assessed for presence of spasticity as:
• A cause of pain
• Limiting activities or care
• Risk factor for contracture 
• Interventions should have specific goals and be monitored using appropriate clinical measures
• Positioning  (shown to be effective)
• Active and/or passive movement 
• Pain control 
• Drugs for persistent troublesome focal (Botulinum toxin)/general (baclofen) spasticity
• Splinting of arm or hand should NOT be used routinely after stroke and splinting and casting should 
only be offered after individualised specialist assessment and monitoring

contracture) RCP guidelines
• Patients should have an initial specialist assessment for positioning within 4 hours of

## Page 43

Conclusions on the role of stretching on contractures
• The results of this systematic review are sufficiently robust to indicate that stretch, as typically provided by 
physiotherapists, does not produce clinically meaningful effects on severity of contractures in people with neurological 
or non‐neurological conditions.
• We do not recommend further studies looking at the short‐term effects of stretch on joint mobility in either people with 
neurological or non‐neurological conditions because the quality of evidence indicating that stretch is ineffective is high and 
further studies are unlikely to change these findings. 
• While the quality of evidence about the long‐term effects is less rigorous, there is no theoretical basis upon which to 
believe that stretch may have long‐term effects on joint mobility in the absence of a short‐term effect. 
• There may be worth in examining the effectiveness of stretch administered with motor training or botulinum toxin in 
people with neurological conditions. 
• There may also be worth in specifically looking at the effectiveness of stretch for the prevention of contracture in those at
high risk of developing contracture (e.g. people with traumatic brain injury).

Drugs for spasticity
• Troublesome focal spasticity –Botulinum Toxin 
(phenol)
• Botulinum - acts at neuromuscular junction by 
inhibiting Ach release and paralysing muscle.
• Carried out with splinting and/or rehab and 
reassessed 3-4 months later. 
• Side effects: 
•
Injection site pain
•
haematoma, 
•
muscle necrosis, 
•
phlebitis
• Troublesome generalised spasticity – baclofen 
(tizanidine, cannabinoids)
• Baclofen - acts centrally to reduce the release of 
excitatory neurotransmitters (Glutamate)
• Side effects baclofen

•
