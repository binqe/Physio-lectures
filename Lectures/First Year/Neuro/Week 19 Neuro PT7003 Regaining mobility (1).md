## Slide 3

- Lower Limb and Gait Dysfunction
- Neurological impairment, such as weakness, sensory loss, and pain, can result in gait dysfunction (Lennon et al. 2024)
- Regaining and improving walking is cited as one of the most common goals by people with stroke undergoing rehabilitation and living in the community (Eng and Tang 2007)
- Interventions designed to improve lower limb and gait dysfunction can be aimed at all levels of disability- impairment, activity limitation, participation restriction

## Slide 4

- International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)

## Slide 5

- Rehabilitation for Lower Limb and Gait Dysfunction
- Physiotherapy interventions are guided by a variety of factors, including assessment findings, patient preference and goals, clinician expertise, therapist beliefs and attitudes, research evidence and workplace practices (McGlinchey et al. 2024)
- Evidence supports repetitive and intensive practice of walking and exercise using aids and equipment as necessary to enable safe practice and mobility during usual activities post-stroke (ISWP 2023)
- Reassessment of an intervention’s effectiveness is facilitated through outcome measurement and goal attainment

## Slide 6

- Treadmill Training and Partial Body Weight Support
- whole task practice of gait
- supportive harness to off-load body weight from the legs
- as gait improves, support is decreased as patient required to control a greater percentage of body weight
- research has focused on stroke, Parkinson’s disease and spinal cord injury

## Slide 7

- Treadmill training and body weight support- stroke
- Merholz et al. (2017)
- treadmill training and/or PBWS vs conventional gait training
- 56 RCTs: 3,105 participants (average age 60 years, inpatients and outpatients)
- no significant differences in walking independence
- short term significant benefits on walking speed and endurance in ambulant stroke patients
- no significant benefits at scheduled follow-up/long term

## Slide 8

- Electromechanicaland robot-assisted gait training - stroke
- Merholz et al. (2020)
- electromechanical- and robot-assisted gait training vs normal care
- 62 RCTs: 2,440 participants (age 47 – 76 years)
- increased likelihood of independence in gait and increased walking speed but not walking distance
- non-ambulant stroke survivors particularly in the first 3 months post-stroke more likely to gain benefit than ambulant stroke survivors
- long term effects uncertain

## Slide 9

- Treadmill training and body weight support- Parkinson’s disease
- Merholz et al. (2015)
- treadmill training vs conventional gait training/no treatment
- 18 RCTs: 633 participants (age 58-74)
- significant differences: gait speed and stride length
- no significant differences: walking distance and cadence
- long term effects uncertain

## Slide 10

- Treadmill training- spinal cord injury
- Merholz et al. (2017)
- bodyweight supported treadmill training or robotic locomotor training vs overground gait training and other physiotherapy
- 13 RCTs: 586 participants (age 16-68)
- all levels of spinal involvement (C1-L4) and impairment  (AIS Grade A-D) included
- no significant differences in walking speed or walking distance

## Slide 11

- Strength Training
- weakness is one of the most common impairments post-stroke
- historical concerns about worsening spasticity due to physical exertion have been disputed (Dodd and Morris 2004, Ada et al. 2006)
- resistance training (body weight or equipment) is the most effective modality to improve muscle strength post-stroke (Saunders et al. 2020)

## Slide 12

- Evidence for post-stroke physiotherapy – strengthening exercise
- Veerbeek et al. (2014)
- 467 RCTs (25,373 participants) of 53 interventions
- strong evidence that higher dose of practice is better for muscle strength of paretic leg

## Slide 13

- Endurance Training
- Cardiovascular disease is the leading prospective cause of death in chronic stroke
- Aerobic exercise can
- reduce resting BP
- improve lipid profiles
- improve glucose tolerance and insulin profiles
- Reducing systolic resting BP by 10 mmHg can lead to a 1/3 reduced risk of stroke

## Slide 14

- Physical fitness training post-stroke
- Saunders et al. (2020)
- 75 RCTs: 3017 participants
- cardiorespiratory training (n=32, 1631 participants)
- resistance training (n=20, 779 participants)
- mixed training interventions (n=23, 1207 participants)
- exercise is safe: very few deaths overall, no serious adverse events
- significant reductions in disability by cardiorespiratory and mixed training interventions
- multiple benefits for physical fitness (VO2 peak and strength), mobility (walking speed) and physical function (balance)

## Slide 15

- Practical considerations
- Luo et al. (2020)
- facilities and equipment to support high-intensity (>70% peak heart rate) cardiorespiratory fitness training (e.g. bodyweight support treadmills and/or static/recumbent cycles)
- training dose should be at least 30-40 minutes, 3-5 times a week for 10-20 weeks
- programmes of mixed training should also be available at least 3 days per week for 20 weeks
- exercise aimed at increasing heart rate should be used for those with more severe weakness, such as using arm cycles or seated exercise groups
