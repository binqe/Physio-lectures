What is motor control
“the ability to regulate or direct the mechanisms essential to movement”
• How the central nervous system (CNS) deals with controlling the many different muscles and joints in different 
combinations to perform movements
• How sensory information from environment is used to select and control movement both the initiation and 
modification 
• How do our perception of ourselves, the tasks we perform, and the environment in which we are moving influence 
our movement behaviors
• Why should therapists study motor control?

## Page 6

Motor Learning 
“field of motor learning is the study of the acquisition and /or modification of movement”
• Motor learning occurs through a process of experience, rehearsal, maintenance of learnt movement and carry 
over of learning to other tasks
• Experience is the SENSORY FEEDBACK to the central nervous system during movements
• Rehearsal is the repetition of movements that we call PRACTICE
• Learning is the retention of learned skills and can be evaluated in a RETENTION TEST
• Carry over of learning is the ability to utilise elements of learned skills when performing other similar skills 
and can be evaluated in a TRANSFER TEST

Gentile’s taxonomy 
of motor skill
• A 16-item taxonomy with two key dimensions 
affecting task complexity. 
• The two dimensions are: 
• Environmental Context (stationary or in-motion 
regulatory conditions and the degree of inter-trial 
variability) and 
• Action Function (body stability or transport and 
with or without object manipulation)
• Once task complexity is established then this can 
be used to match and extend the individuals 
capacity in rehabilitation.

## Page 19

Reflex Theory
• Sir Charles Sherrington in 1906
• Reflexes are the basis of complex behaviour
• Stimulus proves a reflex which becomes the 
stimulus for the next response
• Implications: Bobath concept
• Limitations: Does not explain:
• voluntary movement
• how a single stimulus may elicit a variety of responses 
depending on context

## Page 20

Hierarchical Theory 
(Adams 1971)
• Hierarchical organization of the CNS
• Cortical centres control movement in a top-
down manner throughout the nervous 
system.
• Closed loop mode:  sensory feedback is 
needed and used to control movement
• Voluntary movement is initiated by “will”
• Implications: identify and prevent primitive 
reflexes, normalise tone, developmental 
sequencing, restore normal patterns
• Limitations: does not explain how a reflex on 
the lowest hierarchical level would dominate 
motor function (withdrawal reflex).

## Page 21

Motor Programming theory – Schmidt 
1976)
• Focused on the nature of action rather than 
the response
• Motor response can be a response to a 
stimulus or by a central process
• Central motor pattern – capable of generating 
movement in the absence of a stimulus
• Implications:
• Abnormal movements – not just reflexes
• Motor relearning required for functional 
tasks
• Don’t just re-educate muscles in isolation
• Limitations
• Consider the role of musculoskeletal and 
environmental variables to achieve 
movement control

## Page 22

Systems Theory 
(Bernstein 1967)
• ‘‘movements are not controlled centrally nor 
peripherally, but rather are effected by interactions 
among multiple systems’’
• Recognises that the body is a mechanical system 
that responds differently to the same command due 
to the interactions between external forces.
• Implications:
• Assessment and treatment should examine 
deficits in specific systems contributing to 
motor control e.g. MSK, perceptual etc
• Limitations:
• Systems theory does not account for the 
subject’s interaction with the environment.
