Neural plasticity
Neural plasticity: capacity of the brain to alter it function and 
structure in response to injury, use and non-use
Spontaneous recovery occurs after neurological damage; 
there is also neural plasticity
• Enduring changes in neural function and structure 
following damage, experience and therapy.
The brain responds to injury by adaptation aimed at 
restoring function
• Cortical map enlargement to adjacent or remote 
regions of the brain
• Cortical maps can also be modified by inputs such 
as sensory, experience and learning.
This implies that therapy could be directed towards the 
enhancement of these processes for recovery of movement 
and function especially at the early phase of recovery.
Techniques to promote neuroplasticity:
• Afferent information, task specific exercises etc
Later stage rehabilitation would centre of approaches that 
alter non-use of arm

Evidence based Rehabilitation
• Evidence based Medicine as defined by Sackett et al. 1996
 
“is the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making 
decisions about the care of individual patients”
Philosophical approach that integrates:
• Best available research, Clinical reasoning and expert opinion
For EBP, physiotherapists are expected to use a wide range of strategies supported by 
current evidence base
• Cochrane reviews, other meta-analysis or high-quality systematic reviews
• Multi-disciplinary clinical guidelines e.g. Stroke, MS, PD etc
• There are still so many key areas with no evidence or conflicting evidence
• Clinical reasoning skills
• Patient & carers’ needs, wishes and goals

## Page 17

Muscle strengthening in neurology
1.
Repetitive contraction of a weak muscle group using single or multi-joint weightlifting exercise, elastic 
band, isometric or isokinetic exercise etc. 
2.
Functional strength training – strengthen force generation, train inter-segmental control of the limb and 
preserve muscle length e.g. close chain type step-up, leg press, squatting, sit-to-stand, treadmill 
walking.
3.
Practice of functional actions made ineffective by muscle weakness and reduced limb control e.g. stair 
climbing & descent, ramp walking, overground and treadmill walking with the aim for increasing speed 
and distance walked. Repetitive standing and sitting down holding a tray, reaching for, picking up and 
manipulating objects on the ground and above head.
Notes
➢The first type is aimed at individuals with paralysed or denervated muscles. 
➢Second and third types are aimed at people with ability to contract relevant muscles but with insufficient 
force, poor timing of force, poor control of limb or lack of agility. 
➢Training parameters – up to 10 reps with no rest; 3 sets with short breaks
➢Exercises should be progressed.

Implementing Neuroplasticity motor control programmes
Applying motor learning principles to motor control 
programmes
Meaningful activities (Salience) e.g. sit to stand to get to 
toilet, to get food, to go out
High Intensity Practice: Within and outside practice
High Intensity: 3 – 4 times a week, at least 2 hours per day 
crucial 
Feedback: intrinsic & extrinsic feedback
High repetition: 300 reps for upper limb and 2000 steps for 
lower limb
Practice conditions: modification of practice elements to enable 
task to be performed
Task specificity: acquisition of skills required and not 
anatomical movements
Focus of attention: Paying attention to interaction with objects 
rather than movements is more meaningful
Challenge: Task must involve dual tasking and/or multi-
tasking
Instructions: modelling or verbal instructions
Variation and Timing: arrange task to start from various 
starting position
