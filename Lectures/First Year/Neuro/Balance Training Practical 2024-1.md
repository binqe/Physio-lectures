## Page 2

Aims and Learning Outcomes
Aims
• To provide students with the opportunity to practice clinical and communication skills required 
for use in balance rehabilitation 
Learning Outcomes
After the practical students should be able to
• Plan, implement and justify a balance rehabilitation programme
•
Implement a rehabilitation plan to retrain postural alignment, sensory and movement 
strategies.
• Progress a rehabilitation plan to progress balance in siting and standing
• Teach a patient to adapt strategies to changing contexts.

## Page 10

Retraining sensory strategies
•
Exercises for over-reliance on proprioceptive information: 
•
Perform tasks while sitting or standing on surfaces with decreased somatosensory cues 
for balance: carpet, foam, pillows, moving surfaces-wobble boards. 
•
Exercises for over-reliance on visual cues for balance: 
•
Perform tasks where visual cues are absent (eyes closed, blindfolded) or reduced 
(blinders, diminished lighting).
•
Visual cues can also be made inaccurate through the use of glasses smeared with 
petroleum jelly, prism glasses, moving curtains with stripes, moving visual scenes.
•
Exercises for enhanced ability to use vestibular information: 
•
Exercises where one must maintain balance while both visual and somatosensory 
inputs are simultaneously reduced, such as standing on foam or a wobble board with 
eyes closed or moving visual scenes

## Page 11

Progressing 
balance 
exercises
• Practice with sitting and standing
• Increased time in static posture with eyes open and closed
• With reduced base of support (eyes open and closed)
• While changing the orientation of the head and trunk (e.g. 
looking over one’s shoulder, leaning over)
• While performing a variety of upper extremity tasks
beyond base of support
• As patient improves, more difficult and demanding tasks 
can be introduced.
• Incorporating cognitive tasks: e.g. maintaining 
standing balance with a small base of support while 
counting backwards in threes.
• Dual attention: e.g. walking while holding a glass of 
water; sitting on wobble board and trying to maintain 
balance while holding a glass of water with eyes open and 
eyes closed.

## Page 12

Functional exercises
• Example of exercises
• Stepping to picking up objects beyond the person’s limit of  
stability
• Cross over stepping marks on the floor
• Lunges in different directions
• Stepping or dancing in time with music
• Responding to external timing demands – catching a ball, 
kicking a ball, computer games
• Coping with complexity in the environment
• Negotiating an obstacle course
• Making sudden stops and starts
• Stepping over objects
• Tandem walking
• Marching on the spot with dual tasking
• Walking and talking
• Walking and carrying an object

### Guidelines for functional strength training (textbook box)

![Guidelines for functional strength training (textbook box)](images/Balance%20Training%20Practical%202024-1/fig-002.webp)

**Text extracted from figure (OCR):**

Summary of Box 2.1 (see figure for exact wording): exercises should be task-oriented (e.g. step-ups, squats, sit-to-stand, walking, stairs, reaching) and practised as intensively as possible to mild fatigue; grade resistance and repetitions to ability, using body weight, free weights, elastic bands, machines or an inclined treadmill; vary load via step/chair height or object weight; generally <10 repetitions in three sets, or 3-20 repetitions at ~50% of one-repetition maximum for progressive resistance training; higher repetitions at low load for endurance; for very weak muscles use biofeedback, mental practice or functional electrical stimulation; keep normal breathing. Caution: avoid high-intensity resistance training in people at risk from high blood pressure (refers to ACSM Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription, 2006).
