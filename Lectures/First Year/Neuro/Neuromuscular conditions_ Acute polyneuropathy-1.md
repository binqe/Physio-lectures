## Page 10

Pathology of peripheral neuropathy
• Axonal degeneration
• Toxic, metabolic, nutritional and physical insults (cold or trauma)
• Longest axons affected the most
• Distal weakness, wasting or sensory loss
• If neuron remain intact – regenerates, if not, no scope for 
regeneration.
• Demyelination
• Inflammatory diseases 
• Myelin is lost but the axon is preserved.
• Demyelination prevents conduction and produces weakness but 
not wasting
• Repair can be quick
• May also involve axonal degeneration

## Page 12

Gullain Barre Syndrome (GBS)
• Auto-immune disorder  - immune response is directed towards unknown 
antigens
• Incidence: 1.9 cases per 100000 population (European data)
• Can affect any age but most common in men and in older people
• Unknown causes but an infection precedes onset in a third of cases. 
• The response leads to inflammatory process and destruction of myelin 
sheath in larger diameter motor and sensory neurons
• Rapidly progressive paralysis
• Sensory impairment 
• Areflexia

## Page 13

Pathophysiology 
in GBS and 
clinical relevance
Inflammatory process is primary cause of symptoms in GBS of both 
demyelinating and axonal types but where axonal degeneration and  
bystander degeneration occurs in GBS this causes persistent problems
Remyelination after inflammation has resolved gives rise to functional 
recovery unless axonal degeneration has occurred
Collateral sprouting after axonal degeneration aids maintenance of 
function.
Extended periods of denervation may prevent reinnervation by 
regenerating axons and motor neuron death may occur resulting in 
permanent functional loss
Expanded motor units as a result of collateral sprouting may 
decompensate many years after the initial insult leading to an increase 
in symptoms and disability

Signs & Symptoms of LMN 
pathology – Positive Neuropathic

any of the senses (not specific to touch) 
• Hyperalgesia – increased pain from a noxious 
stimulus

Autonomic Signs  & Symptoms
Cardiovascular involvement 
• Altered heart rate variability (HRV) 
• Reduced variability and response 
• Orthostatic/postural hypotension
• Lying to standing
• Tachycardia
• Altered HR response to exercise 
• Check response prior to prescription of exercise 
• Use Borg rating of perceived exertion rather than HR for monitoring intensity

Acute 
Management 
of GBS
• Reduced Vital capacity
• Airway protection
• Critical care support
Address any serious complications
• IV immunoglobulins
• Plasma exchange or plasmapheresis
Pharmacotherapy
• Respiratory interventions e.g. sputum clearance, 
maintaenance of lung volumes, breathing exercises
• Prevention of secondary complications – early mobilisation, 
splinting, positioning, stretches
• Communication to allay anxiety and confusion about 
prognosis
Physiotherapy
