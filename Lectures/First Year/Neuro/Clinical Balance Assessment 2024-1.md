## Page 3

Why assess balance?
• Balance deficits are common in neurologically impaired 
patients
• Common impairments are:
• Motor dysfunction: 
• Musculoskeletal: ↓ strength; trunk instability; ↓ range 
of movement; altered muscle tone
• Biomechanics: ↓  LOS; ↓ balance response; altered  
movement strategies
• Sensory dysfunction:
• Altered sensations:↓ ankle proprioception; vestibular 
damage; dizziness; visual deficits
• Altered sensory integration: impaired attention; 
impaired sensory weighting; delayed anticipatory 
postural response; altered perceived stability limits; 
abnormal perception of vertical.

## Page 7

Clinical Balance Assessment  - Task
• Balance assessment should be performed in the context of a functional 
activity – bed mobility, sitting, standing, walking
• Set an appropriate balance task 
• Safe situation (environment, assistance)
• Incorporate various environmental contexts
• Challenge patient balance:
•
reduce base of support, raising COG, displacing COG toward LOS, unstable surface, altering 
sensory input
• Assess all aspects of balance responses: 
•
posture & movement, balance strategies, balance reactions, all planes of movement

## Page 8

Clinical Balance Assessment - Observation
• Can they balance? Posture, excessive sway
• Can they balance during movement? Strategies used, are they appropriate?
• Does the patient use compensatory strategies
• Is the goal completed successfully?
• Is the task effortful?
• What happens to the balance if sensory input is reduced? (eyes closed)

Quantifying functional limitations:
Get up and Go test  (Timed up and Go (TUG) test)
• quick screening tool for 
detecting imbalance in the 
elderly (Mathias S et al, 1986)
• reliable and consistent 
• correlates well with functional 
capacity 
• highly correlated with gait 
velocity (Evans and Krebs, 1999)

## Page 11

Quantifying functional limitations: 
Functional Reach Test
• developed to detect balance 
problems in older adults (Duncan et 
al., 1990)
• tests limits of stability in forward 
direction
• variable findings: reliable, highly 
predictive of falls, unable to 
differentiate fallers / non-fallers
• does NOT test dynamic balance 
(Wernick-Robinson et al., 1999)
Scores < 6 or 7 inches  = limited functional balance; 
>10 inches = normal values

Quantifying functional limitations: 
Dynamic Gait Index
• assesses ability to modify gait 
according to changing task demands 
(Shumway-Cook and Woollacott, 1995) 
• valid predictor of falls in both older 
adults (Shumway-Cook, 1997) and 
vestibular patients (Whitney et al 
2000) 
• Functional Gait Index (Wrisley et al., 
2004) 
• Clinically meaningful change

## Page 13

Quantifying functional limitations: 
Tinetti Performance  & Mobility Assessment
• Developed to assess balance & 
mobility in older adults (Tinetti, 
1986) 
• Performance-Oriented Mobility 
Assessment (POMA)
• Assesses likelihood of falls 
• Used often with gait evaluation 
• assesses different functions 
than DGI 
• Good inter-rater reliability 
• Armless chair, stopwatch and 15feet walkway 
(4.5metres)
• Patient stands from sitting & stay standing
• Turns 360 degrees and sit back down (Balance)
• Patient walks a few meters at normal speed, 
followed by turning and walking back at a fast but 
safe speed.
• The patient then sits back down (mobility)
• The assessor grades the patient’s performance.
• Uses a 3-point ordinal scale (0-2)
• Gait (scored over 12 and balance over 16 points 
= 28 points

## Page 17

Quantifying functional limitations: 
Single leg Stance
• This is used to assess static postural and 
balance control
• Performed with eyes open and hands on 
the hips
• Patient stands unassisted on one leg, 
timed from the time the other foot 
leaves the ground till when the foot 
touches the ground again or the arm 
leaves the hips
• If unable to stand for 5 seconds or less, 
patient is at greater risk of injury from 
fall.

## Page 18

Quantifying 
functional 
limitations: 
Commonly used 
tests: ‘Walking 
while talking 
(WWT) test’ 
(Vorghese et al., 
2002) 
• This is a dual-task measure of 
divided attention to examine 
cognitive –motor interactions, 
especially for identifying 
fallers.
• Patient walks 6m, turn and 
return (12m).
• Patient is instructed to:
• WWT simple: walking 
while reciting the 
alphabet loud
• WWT complex: walking 
while reciting alternate 
letters of the alphabet 
aloud.
• Scoring: time needed to 
complete the distance
• Simple: Walk and recite 
letters of the alphabet; High 
specificity (89.4%) for 
identifying falls if completed 
in 20 seconds or longer 
• Complex: Walk and recite 
alternate letters of the 
alphabet; High specificity 
(95.6%) for identifying falls 
if completed in 33 seconds 
or longer

•
A tool developed to analyse several

contribute to poor functional balance

et al., 2010) 
•
BESTest: Score of 69% cut-off for 
identifying fallers in people with 
Parkinson’s Disease (Leddy et al., 
2011); 92% accuracy for identifying 
fallers in people with MS (Jacobs and 
Kasser, 2012)
•
Accuracy of identifying a faller 
comparable for the Mini-BESTest and 
BESTest (PD) (Leddy et al., 2011b) 
•
Mini-BESTest: able to classify 
individual patients who show 
significant improvement in balance 
function; Minimal important change 4 
points (Godi et al., 2013).

evaluate performance of 6
