| Condition | Rotator Cuff Tear |
| Pathophysiology | A rotator cuff tear involves damage to one or more of the four rotator cuff tendons (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis).
Common causes include acute trauma, repetitive microtrauma, or age-related degenerative changes.
Degenerative tears are most prevalent in individuals over 50 years old, with prevalence increasing with age (Yamamoto et al., 2010). |
| Signs and symptoms | Pain, especially during overhead activities or at night.
Weakness in abduction and external rotation.
Limited range of motion (ROM).
Crepitus or clicking sounds during movement. |
| Clusters of tests for diagnose | Evidence suggests using a combination of tests for greater diagnostic accuracy (Hegedus et al., 2012).
Painful Arc Test (Sensitivity: 74%, Specificity: 81%).
Infraspinatus Strength Test (Sensitivity: 62%, Specificity: 73%).
Drop Arm Test (Sensitivity: 44%, Specificity: 92%).
Clinical Note: A positive result on all three tests strongly indicates a rotator cuff tear (Park et al., 2005). |
| Differential Diagnosis | Adhesive Capsulitis: Marked restriction in both active and passive ROM, particularly external rotation.
Subacromial Impingement Syndrome (SIS): Pain during the painful arc test but without significant weakness. Positive Hawkins-Kennedy Test combined with a normal Drop Arm Test supports SIS over a tear.
Cervical Radiculopathy: Neck pain radiating to the shoulder, with associated paraesthesia or weakness in the arm. Spurling’s Test and imaging (MRI/CT) for confirmation. |
| Management | Advise rest in the acute phase. Following this, activity should gradually be increased. 
Advise modification of activities that exacerbate symptoms (such as reaching overhead). Usual activities, within the limits of pain, should be restarted as soon as possible.
Physiotherapy may include postural correction, motor control retraining, stretching and strengthening of the rotator cuff and scapular muscles, and manual therapy.
Low intensity and high-frequency exercise, that is within the person's pain threshold, should be performed.
Physiotherapy can be continued for a further 6 weeks if there is improvement in the person's symptoms or function (Nice Guidelines, 2022). |
